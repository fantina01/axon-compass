AXON REVIEW — FULL CURRICULUM ALIGNMENT#

Neuroscience × Policy, Diplomacy & Advocacy — All Ten Projects, All Twenty-Four Weeks#

HOW THIS DOCUMENT WORKS#

The Operational Handbook already defines how every week runs — the reading-analysis pattern, the five tournament formats (Confidence Gap, Prediction Ladder, Signal and the Noise, Distortion Chain, Self-Forecast Duel), the five laboratory formats (Build-a-System Simulation, Message Transmission Simulation, Distortion & Reconstruction Experiment, Identity & System Construction Workshop, Capstone Prototype Development), and the fixed 75-minute session shapes. None of that is repeated here.

What this document provides is the piece that was missing: exactly what content each of Policy, Diplomacy & Advocacy’s ten projects plugs into that fixed structure, week by week, for all 24 weeks. A chapter leader running “Groupthink at the Summit Table” should never have to guess what their Week 10 tournament station is about, or what their Week 15 lab’s manipulated stimulus should be — it’s specified here, tied directly to the real literature, real case studies, and real institutions named in each project file, and specified so that Week 4’s Innovation Proposal, Week 8’s Research Investigation, Week 12’s communication product, Week 16’s simulation, and Week 20’s policy draft all visibly build on each other, exactly the way the Handbook’s own unit-to-unit logic requires.

This field carries a distinct responsibility the other seven don’t share to the same degree: several of its ten projects sit directly on genuinely sensitive terrain — juvenile sentencing, refugee trauma, radicalization, war reporting, disability and neurodiversity. Each project file itself already names the specific ethical guardrail that keeps the project responsible (general youth samples instead of at-risk populations, volunteer role-play instead of real refugees, no reproduction of extremist or graphic content, consent for any personal story used). Every week below carries that same guardrail forward rather than only stating it once in Unit I — because a project that is careful in Week 4 and careless by Week 19 was never actually careful. Every Week 8 and Week 16 entry also states the exact comparison the real literature is checked against, the same standard of concreteness the other seven alignment documents held throughout.

PROJECT 1 — The Adolescent Brain on Trial#

Neuroscience-informed juvenile sentencing reform

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Casey, Jones, and Hare’s 2008 finding that reward-processing limbic regions mature earlier in adolescence than the prefrontal cortex — the “accelerator before the brakes” imbalance. Pre-reading: participants guess whether teenage risk-taking is mostly a choice or has a real developmental basis, before any case law is introduced.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station presents a short case summary and asks participants to predict whether the Supreme Court’s real ruling in Roper v. Simmons leaned on developmental science or purely on legal precedent, before the real 2005 opinion is revealed.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each comparing a different state’s juvenile sentencing guideline language against Casey et al.’s developmental-imbalance finding, rating how well each guideline reflects current science before checking against real statute text.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Developmental Sentencing Brief Toolkit — a template advocacy brief translating prefrontal-maturation research into plain-language arguments usable by public defenders or juvenile-justice reform groups. The proposal documents a real review of the student’s own state’s juvenile sentencing guidelines against current neuroscience, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and the Week 3 comparison trials.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Steinberg’s 2008 refinement — adolescents show good impulse control alone and unhurried, but the prefrontal-limbic mismatch appears specifically under emotional arousal or peer presence, closer to real offense contexts than calm lab tasks. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: states whose juvenile sentencing statutes were updated after Miller v. Alabama (2012) show measurably different sentencing-length distributions for comparable offenses than states that have not updated statutes.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real state statute-update statuses; participants predict which states show the largest sentencing-length gap for comparable offenses.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one real state’s statute-update status. Reception receives only that status; Filtering decides whether it counts as “updated” or “not updated”; Integration adds a comparable-offense sentencing figure; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected sentencing-length pattern from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed pattern still matches the real coded data.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the comparative policy analysis using publicly available sentencing data across a sample of states, coding statute-update status as the independent variable and sentencing-length data as the dependent variable. The poster’s central chart is the real statute-update-versus-sentencing-length comparison, stating the real numbers and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration. The full data-source list and coding scheme are documented as the investigation blueprint.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to communicate “brain science” without it sounding like an excuse for accountability, to a general public wary of that exact framing.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 findings delivered two ways — as a raw sentencing-length statistic versus the documentary framing “The Brain the Law Forgot” — rated for which framing addresses the “brain science means no accountability” misconception most directly.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 statute comparison, passed through the five roles to test specifically whether “developmentally-calibrated sentencing” survives retelling or collapses into “no accountability for teens” — the central misconception this project has to guard against at every retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce the short documentary or investigative podcast “The Brain the Law Forgot,” using the real Week 8 statute comparison, disseminated through youth-justice nonprofit partners, local news op-eds, and school civics classes.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “science says teens aren’t responsible” instead of the real, narrower finding about sentencing calibration.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 statute-update sentencing gap once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific, bounded finding drifts toward the broader misconception.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real statute-comparison dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation reframes the finding as “no accountability”; final recall is collected; comparison shows the reframing’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive model of prefrontal-limbic maturation trajectories across age, showing modeled “control capacity” by age band, documented on GitHub with citations to the underlying imaging studies, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how a real model statute amendment actually gets drafted, reviewed, and revised before reaching a legislator’s desk.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about how state legislators or public defenders will react to the model statute amendment, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the real path a model statute amendment takes through a state legislature and public defenders’ offices to find the realistic point to submit it.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the model statute amendment aligning the state’s juvenile sentencing guidelines with current developmental-neuroscience consensus, through a formal policy brief, peer review, and revision cycle, citing the real Week 8 comparative data and the Week 16 maturation model, with limitations (the correlational nature of sentencing-outcome data) explicitly disclosed, aimed at state legislators, public defenders’ offices, and juvenile-justice reform nonprofits.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 guess through real constitutional case history and a real statute comparison to a model statute amendment.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the statute-comparison map and maturation-model visualization as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test the policy defense with an unfamiliar peer raising due-process and public-safety counterarguments.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the toolkit, the real comparative investigation, the documentary, the maturation model, and the model statute amendment into one draft, walking from constitutional case history through to the amendment.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition pairs the statute-comparison map with the maturation-model visualization; reflection defense addresses due-process and public-safety counterarguments directly, citing the same evidence base throughout.

PROJECT 2 — Diplomacy Under Duress#

Stress neuroscience and crisis negotiation protocols

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Arnsten’s 2009 finding that acute stress chemically impairs prefrontal cortex function while strengthening more reflexive responses. Pre-reading: participants guess whether a negotiator under time pressure makes better or worse decisions than the same negotiator with no deadline, before any pilot exercise runs.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station gives participants a short negotiation scenario under a tight time limit; they predict their own decision quality before an outside rubric scores it.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each a short negotiation exercise under a different manufactured time-pressure level, recording rubric-scored decision quality at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Stress-Resilient Negotiation Protocol — a structured decision checklist and pacing method modeled on hostage-negotiation and crisis-diplomacy training, tested via a simulated high-pressure Model UN–style negotiation exercise. The proposal documents observed decision quality in a pilot negotiation under manufactured time pressure, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Vecchi, Van Hasselt, and Romano’s 2005 description of the FBI’s Behavioral Change Stairway Model — a real, professionally-developed crisis-negotiation framework built around de-escalation before any attempt at influencing behavior. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: negotiators using the protocol under simulated time pressure reach more mutually beneficial outcomes than those without it.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real negotiation scenarios with escalating time pressure; participants predict which pressure level produces the steepest drop in outcome quality without the protocol.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one real negotiator’s mid-session decision. Reception receives only the decision; Filtering decides whether it looks rushed or deliberate; Integration adds whether the protocol was in use; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected outcome score from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed score still matches the real rubric data.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the between-subjects simulated negotiation exercise with a standardized scoring rubric, comparing protocol-trained and untrained negotiators under identical time pressure. The poster’s central chart is the real protocol-versus-no-protocol outcome-quality comparison, stating the real numbers and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to explain that good diplomacy is a trainable cognitive skill, not just innate composure under pressure.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 findings delivered two ways — as a raw outcome-quality comparison versus “Why Diplomats Train Their Brains, Not Just Their Arguments” — rated for which framing lands with a Model UN or debate audience.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 protocol-versus-no-protocol comparison, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific outcome-quality gap survives retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Why Diplomats Train Their Brains, Not Just Their Arguments,” using the real Week 8 exercise data, disseminated through Model UN networks, debate programs, and diplomacy-focused youth nonprofits.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “the protocol obviously fixes everything” without recalling the real, specific outcome-quality gap.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 outcome-quality gap once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific gap inflates.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real protocol-comparison dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation inflates the reported gap; final recall is collected; comparison shows the inflated figure’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive stress-reasoning-degradation curve, showing predicted decision-quality decline as physiological arousal increases and how the protocol’s pacing structure is designed to interrupt that curve, documented on GitHub, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how a curriculum recommendation actually reaches the program directors who run Model UN and debate leagues.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether program directors will adopt stress-resilience training into standard negotiation curricula, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map youth diplomacy programs’ curriculum-adoption process to find the realistic point to make the ask.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that youth diplomacy programs formally incorporate stress-resilience training into negotiation curricula, citing the real Week 8 and Week 16 evidence, aimed at program directors and educational diplomacy nonprofits.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 guess through a real protocol-versus-no-protocol outcome gap to a curriculum-adoption recommendation.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the stress-reasoning-curve model as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer running the protocol live.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the protocol, the real exercise data, the explainer, the model, and the curriculum-policy ask into one crisis-negotiation portfolio.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition runs a live timed negotiation using the protocol; reflection defense states the real outcome-quality gap found between protocol and no-protocol conditions.

PROJECT 3 — The Empathy Gap in Foreign Aid Messaging#

Neuroscience of compassion fade in advocacy campaigns

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Slovic’s 2007 “psychic numbing” finding — emotional response to suffering does not scale proportionally with victim count, and can even decrease as the number grows. Pre-reading: participants guess whether a single named victim or a large casualty statistic would move them to donate more, before any pilot test runs.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station shows a single-victim appeal and a statistic-heavy appeal for the same real cause; participants predict which will raise more donation-intention votes before the pilot is scored.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each testing a different single-victim narrative against a matched statistical framing, recording peer donation-intention responses at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Compassion-Fade-Resistant Messaging Framework for a real or simulated humanitarian appeal, contrasting statistic-heavy messaging with single-victim narrative messaging. The proposal documents a pilot test of peer donation-intention responses to both message types, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Small, Loewenstein, and Slovic’s 2007 finding that prompting analytical thought before donating reduced generosity toward an identified victim, and Kogut and Ritov’s 2005 finding that a single identified victim outperforms an identified group of the same size. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: single-victim narrative appeals generate higher donation-intention scores than statistically-framed appeals describing larger affected populations, even when the underlying cause is identical.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real appeal pairs (single victim, small identified group, large statistic); participants predict the ranking order for donation-intention score, per Kogut and Ritov’s singularity finding.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one raw appeal type. Reception receives only the framing type (narrative or statistical); Filtering decides which is likely to score higher; Integration adds the real donation-intention score; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected ranking from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed ranking still matches the real survey data.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the between-subjects survey experiment with randomized message assignment, comparing single-victim narrative appeals to statistically-framed appeals for the identical underlying cause. The poster’s central chart is the real donation-intention comparison, stating the real numbers and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to explain compassion fade responsibly, without exploiting any real victim’s story in the process of explaining it.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 findings delivered two ways — as a raw donation-intention comparison versus “Why One Story Moves Us More Than a Million Statistics” — rated for which framing stays respectful of real victims while still landing the point.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 comparison, passed through the five roles to test specifically whether the finding survives retelling without any real victim’s story being invoked without consent — the most important checkpoint in this project.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce the short documentary “Why One Story Moves Us More Than a Million Statistics,” using the same messaging-framework findings, disseminated through partner humanitarian nonprofits and student advocacy clubs, with explicit care not to exploit real victims’ stories without consent or attribution.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “statistics never work” — an overstated claim — despite the real, more specific Week 8 finding.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 donation-intention gap once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific gap inflates toward an absolute claim.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real narrative-versus-statistical dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation inflates the reported gap into an absolute; final recall is collected; comparison shows the overstatement’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive compassion-fade curve showing predicted empathic-response decline as described victim count increases, based on published psychophysiological data, documented on GitHub, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how a hybrid messaging guideline actually reaches an NGO communications director’s real desk.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether NGO communications directors will adopt hybrid (narrative-anchored, statistically honest) messaging guidelines, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map a humanitarian organization’s communications-decision process to find the realistic point to make the ask.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that humanitarian communications offices adopt hybrid messaging guidelines — narrative-anchored, statistically honest — to maximize both engagement and accuracy, citing the real Week 8 and Week 16 evidence, aimed at NGO communications directors.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 guess through a real, ethically-collected donation-intention comparison to a hybrid-messaging recommendation.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the compassion-fade curve as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer, checking specifically that no real victim’s story is used without consent.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the messaging framework, the real experiment, the documentary, the compassion-fade model, and the hybrid-messaging recommendation into one humanitarian-advocacy portfolio.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition displays the compassion-fade curve beside the real narrative-versus-statistical comparison; reflection defense states the real numbers found and restates the ethical care taken throughout.

PROJECT 4 — Groupthink at the Summit Table#

Social conformity neuroscience in diplomatic delegations

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Janis’s 1972 case studies of major U.S. foreign-policy failures (most famously the Bay of Pigs), identifying an illusion of unanimity, self-censorship of doubts, and pressure on dissenters as the core groupthink pattern. Pre-reading: participants guess whether a high-status, cohesive group makes better or worse decisions than a group of strangers, before any pilot exercise runs.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station runs a short unstructured group-decision exercise; participants predict how much conformity pressure they’ll observe before the exercise, then compare to what actually happened.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each a new unstructured group-decision exercise, recording observed conformity/groupthink patterns (dissent suppressed, unanimity assumed) at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Structured Dissent Protocol for diplomatic-style group decision-making — a formal “devil’s advocate” rotation and anonymous pre-vote — tested in a simulated Model UN or student-government crisis-decision exercise. The proposal documents observed conformity/groupthink patterns in a pilot unstructured group decision exercise, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Berns et al.’s 2005 brain-imaging finding that conforming to an incorrect group answer was associated with changes in perception-related brain activity, and Esser’s 1998 review finding that directive leadership and insulation from outside opinions predict groupthink more strongly than cohesiveness alone. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: groups using the structured dissent protocol reach more accurate, higher-quality decisions (per an objective scoring rubric) than groups without it.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real historical group-decision case studies with escalating groupthink-symptom severity; participants predict which case shows the clearest illusion of unanimity, per Janis’s framework.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one raw group’s decision-in-progress. Reception receives only the group’s stated consensus; Filtering decides whether dissent was voiced or suppressed; Integration adds whether the dissent protocol was in use; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected decision-quality score from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed score still matches the real rubric data.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the between-groups simulated crisis-decision exercise with standardized outcome scoring, comparing protocol and no-protocol groups. The poster’s central chart is the real decision-quality comparison, stating the real numbers and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to connect a famous historical failure (Bay of Pigs) to a testable mechanism students can run themselves.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 findings delivered two ways — as a raw decision-quality comparison versus “Why Good Diplomats Build In Disagreement” — rated for which framing lands with a Model UN or student-government audience.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 protocol-versus-no-protocol comparison, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific decision-quality gap survives retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Why Good Diplomats Build In Disagreement,” using the real Week 8 exercise data, disseminated through Model UN and student-government networks.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the protocol, the real exercise data, the podcast, the judgment-decline model, and the scheduling-policy ask into one fatigue-and-diplomacy portfolio.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition displays the real decision-quality-versus-time curve; reflection defense states the real numbers found, hour by hour.

PROJECT 10 — Neurodiversity in Diplomatic Training#

Designing advocacy programs that don’t assume one cognitive profile

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Davidson, Giannotti, and Warfa’s 2018 systematic review finding significant variation in processing speed among autistic individuals, with some notably slower on timed tasks despite strong performance given more time. Pre-reading: participants guess whether a fast, timed debate format measures knowledge or measures something else entirely, before any peer interviews are conducted.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station gives participants the same argument to prepare under a tight time limit and then under an extended time limit; they predict how much their own performance will differ before comparing both attempts.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each with a different neurodivergent peer describing a specific barrier they’ve faced in a standard debate/Model UN format, recording the described barrier at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Neurodiversity-Inclusive Advocacy Training Redesign — offering alternative formats (written-argument tracks, extended prep-time options, structured-turn-taking formats) alongside traditional formats in a debate/Model UN–style program. The proposal documents interviews with neurodivergent peers about barriers in current advocacy/debate program formats, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 interview trials.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Kapp et al.’s 2013 neurodiversity framework — many autism-related traits are differences in cognitive style, and format accommodations can substantially close observed performance gaps — and research on flexible-format academic accommodation (extended time, written alternatives, structured turn-taking) narrowing performance gaps without reducing rigor. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: neurodivergent participants show improved performance (per the same rubric) and equal or higher engagement scores under redesigned formats compared to standard formats.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real format features (strict time limit, extended time, written-alternative option, structured turn-taking, unstructured rapid-fire); participants predict which feature produces the largest performance-gap narrowing, per the accommodation literature.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one raw session’s format type. Reception receives only the format name; Filtering decides whether it’s standard or redesigned; Integration adds the real rubric score; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected performance-gap size from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed gap still matches the real matched-group data.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the matched-group comparison across standard and redesigned format sessions, measuring performance rubric score and engagement rating. The poster’s central chart is the real standard-versus-redesigned performance-and-engagement comparison, stating the real numbers and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to present real, consented participant experiences without ever speaking for someone whose story hasn’t been explicitly shared with permission.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 findings delivered two ways — as a raw performance-gap statistic versus the documentary framing “Diplomacy Doesn’t Have to Sound Like Just One Thing,” using real consented participant experiences — rated for which framing lands with a debate/Model UN audience while staying respectful.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 performance-and-engagement comparison, passed through the five roles to test specifically whether consented participant experiences stay attributed and accurate through retelling — the most important checkpoint in this project.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce the documentary “Diplomacy Doesn’t Have to Sound Like Just One Thing,” using real, consented participant experiences tied to the same redesign, disseminated through debate/Model UN organizations and neurodiversity-advocacy nonprofits.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember a specific participant’s experience as generalizing to all neurodivergent students, rather than the real, individually-varying finding.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 performance-gap finding once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how easily an individually-varying finding generalizes into a universal claim.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real, individually-varying Week 8 dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation restates it as a universal rule; final recall is collected; comparison shows the overgeneralization’s persistence, followed by full debrief connecting to Davidson et al.’s finding of real individual variation.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive model of how processing-time and format flexibility affect predicted performance-gap size between neurotypical and neurodivergent profiles, based on published processing-speed variance data, documented on GitHub, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how a format-adoption recommendation reaches national debate and Model UN governing organizations.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether national debate/Model UN governing bodies will adopt alternative-format tracks, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map a national debate or Model UN governing organization’s real format-adoption process to find the realistic point to make the ask.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that debate/Model UN/advocacy program governing bodies formally adopt alternative-format tracks, citing the real Week 8 and Week 16 evidence, aimed at national debate and Model UN governing organizations.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 guess through real, consented peer interviews and a real matched-group performance comparison to a governing-body format-adoption recommendation.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the processing-time/format-flexibility model as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test the redesigned format with an unfamiliar peer.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the redesign, the real matched-group study, the documentary, the processing-time model, and the governing-body policy ask into one inclusive-diplomacy-training portfolio.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition runs a live redesigned-format session alongside the standard format for comparison; reflection defense states the real performance-and-engagement numbers found.

CROSS-PROJECT NOTE#

Every project above runs the same 24-week skeleton from the Operational Handbook — five tournaments, five labs, five main outputs building toward one capstone. Every one of Policy, Diplomacy & Advocacy’s ten projects was built to the same depth throughout: a real case, mechanism, or historical episode named explicitly (Roper v. Simmons and prefrontal-limbic maturation, the FBI’s crisis-negotiation model, the identifiable-victim effect, the Bay of Pigs and conformity neuroimaging, identity fusion and belonging, allostatic load and trauma-informed care, variable-ratio reinforcement in platform design, compassion fatigue in conflict journalism, sleep deprivation’s effect on complex decision-making, processing-speed variance and inclusive format design), a real comparison the chapter’s own peer or public data actually produced, and an explicit statement of which earlier week’s evidence each later step depends on, from Week 1 through Week 24. For the five projects sitting closest to genuinely sensitive terrain — juvenile sentencing, foreign-aid victim messaging, radicalization, refugee trauma, and war-reporting imagery — the specific ethical guardrail named in that project’s own file (general-population samples, volunteer role-play, no operational or graphic content, explicit consent for any personal story used) is carried into every week that project touches, tested directly at the Week 11 Message Transmission checkpoint, and restated plainly at the Week 24 Summit rather than left behind in Unit I.
