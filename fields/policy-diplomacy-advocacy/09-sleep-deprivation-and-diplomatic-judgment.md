# 9. Sleep Deprivation and Diplomatic Judgment

*The Case for Negotiation Scheduling Reform*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Policy, Diplomacy & Advocacy &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `policy-diplomacy-advocacy/09-sleep-deprivation-and-diplomatic-judgment`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Does sleep deprivation during marathon negotiation sessions (common in international summits) measurably impair the judgment quality of diplomats, and should negotiation scheduling norms change accordingly?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
High-profile negotiations are frequently conducted through overnight sessions under the assumption that exhaustion signals resolve, despite decades of sleep-deprivation research showing impaired risk assessment and emotional regulation under sleep loss — a case where diplomatic tradition may be actively working against good decision-making.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Walker, M. P. (2017). Why We Sleep: Unlocking the Power of Sleep and Dreams. Scribner.

Walker's widely-read synthesis of sleep research summarizes extensive evidence that sleep deprivation impairs prefrontal cortex function, emotional regulation, and risk assessment — the same categories of judgment central to high-stakes negotiation. This is a useful, accessible entry point summarizing a large research literature, though the project's own citations should also draw on the underlying peer-reviewed studies below rather than relying on the book alone.

### 2. Killgore, W. D. S. (2010). Effects of sleep deprivation on cognition. Progress in Brain Research, 185, 105-129.

Killgore's peer-reviewed review specifically examines how sleep deprivation affects complex decision-making and risk assessment, finding that sleep-deprived individuals often show impaired ability to adapt decisions when circumstances change, and reduced sensitivity to the emotional significance of risky choices — closely relevant to marathon negotiation sessions where circumstances and stakes shift over many hours.

### 3. Harrison, Y., & Horne, J. A. (2000). The impact of sleep deprivation on decision making: A review. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 6(3), 236-249.

This review specifically examined decision-making (as distinct from simpler vigilance or reaction-time tasks) under sleep deprivation, finding that complex, innovative decision-making — the kind required in real negotiation — was more consistently impaired by sleep loss than simple, repetitive tasks. This distinction is important for the project because it shows sleep deprivation's effects are not uniform across all cognitive tasks — they hit hardest exactly where negotiation demands the most: flexible, creative problem-solving under changing conditions.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This project takes a real, entrenched diplomatic tradition — the marathon overnight negotiation session — and builds a genuine evidence-based case against it. Unit I's pilot extended-negotiation exercise shows real decision-quality change over time. Unit II's within-subject comparison across hours of continuous negotiation produces a real, scored decline curve. Unit III explains that specific finding to the Model UN and debate communities who run their own marathon sessions every competition season. Unit IV's judgment-decline model visualizes exactly what the Unit II study measured, hour by hour. Unit V's scheduling-reform recommendation is aimed both at youth diplomacy programs directly and, aspirationally, at real diplomatic norms. A mentor in international relations sees a student willing to question a real, celebrated tradition using actual evidence rather than just intuition — a genuinely distinctive and memorable stance.
