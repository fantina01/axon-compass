# 3. The Empathy Gap in Foreign Aid Messaging

*Neuroscience of Compassion Fade in Advocacy Campaigns*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Policy, Diplomacy & Advocacy &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `policy-diplomacy-advocacy/03-the-empathy-gap-in-foreign-aid-messaging`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Does the well-documented neural phenomenon of "compassion fade" (reduced emotional/neural response to suffering as victim numbers increase) undermine mass-casualty humanitarian appeals, and can message design counteract it?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Humanitarian organizations consistently find that appeals featuring one identifiable victim raise more donations than appeals citing large casualty statistics — a finding rooted in measurable neural and psychophysiological responses (Slovic's "identifiable victim effect") with direct consequences for how much real aid gets raised.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Slovic, P. (2007). "If I look at the mass I will never act": Psychic numbing and genocide. Judgment and Decision Making, 2(2), 79-95.

Slovic's influential paper documents "psychic numbing" and "compassion fade": as the number of people described as suffering increases, people's emotional response and willingness to help typically does not increase proportionally, and can even decrease, compared to hearing about a single identifiable victim. This is the founding conceptual paper behind the entire project, directly naming and explaining the phenomenon the project's messaging framework is designed to counteract.

### 2. Small, D. A., Loewenstein, G., & Slovic, P. (2007). Sympathy and callousness: The impact of deliberative thought on donations to identifiable and statistical victims. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 102(2), 143-153.

This study experimentally compared donations to an identified individual victim versus statistical information about many victims, and additionally found that prompting people to think analytically before donating actually reduced generosity toward the identified victim — suggesting the identifiable-victim effect operates through fast, emotional processing that deliberate analytical thought can dampen. This is directly useful for the project's messaging-framework design, since it suggests analytical framing (like raw statistics) may unintentionally suppress the emotional response campaigns are trying to generate.

### 3. Kogut, T., & Ritov, I. (2005). The 'identified victim' effect: An identified group, or just a single individual? Journal of Behavioral Decision Making, 18(3), 157-167.

Kogut and Ritov tested whether the identifiable-victim effect requires a single named individual specifically, or whether a small, identified group produces the same boost in generosity — finding that a single identified victim generated significantly more sympathy and donations than an identified group of the same size, suggesting the effect is tied to singularity, not just identifiability. This refines the project's messaging strategy: a campaign built around one named person is likely to outperform one built around a small named group, not just one built around named individuals in general.
