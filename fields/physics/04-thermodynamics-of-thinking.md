# 4. Thermodynamics of Thinking

*Modeling the Brain's Energy Budget*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Physics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `physics/04-thermodynamics-of-thinking`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can basic thermodynamics and energy-conservation principles explain why the brain — only about 2% of body weight — consumes roughly 20% of the body's resting energy?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
The brain's disproportionate energy use is a well-documented physiological fact with a genuine physics explanation rooted in ion-pump energy costs (maintaining electrochemical gradients against diffusion) — connecting basic thermodynamics/energy concepts directly to a striking, memorable biological fact.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Attwell, D., & Laughlin, S. B. (2001). An energy budget for signaling in the grey matter of the brain. Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow and Metabolism, 21(10), 1133-1145.

Attwell and Laughlin built a detailed, bottom-up energy budget for the brain, calculating exactly how much energy is consumed by each component of neural signaling — action potentials, postsynaptic effects, and maintaining the resting membrane potential — using real anatomical and physiological data from rodent cortex. This is the core source the project's own bottom-up calculation is modeled on, and it's what makes the project's estimate a genuine, methodologically grounded calculation rather than a rough guess.

### 2. Raichle, M. E., & Gusnard, D. A. (2002). Appraising the brain's energy budget. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 99(16), 10237-10239.

Raichle and Gusnard's widely-cited paper compiles the well-known figure that the brain consumes roughly 20% of the body's resting energy despite being about 2% of body weight, and discusses how much of this energy is devoted to baseline, ongoing signaling rather than task-specific effort — directly supporting the project's myth-busting claim that intense mental effort doesn't burn dramatically more calories than the brain's constant baseline activity already does.

### 3. Sokoloff, L. (1960). The metabolism of the central nervous system in vivo. In Handbook of Physiology, Section I: Neurophysiology (Vol. 3). American Physiological Society.

Sokoloff's early, foundational work using radioactive tracers to measure brain glucose and oxygen consumption established many of the baseline whole-brain metabolic figures still cited today, including how remarkably stable brain energy consumption is regardless of whether a person is actively problem-solving or simply resting with eyes closed. This is the older, foundational physiological measurement work underlying the more recent, more precisely itemized Attwell and Laughlin estimate.

## Why This Project Impresses

Uses only order-of-magnitude estimation and basic energy concepts (well within reach of a strong high-schooler) to arrive at a genuinely striking, well-supported conclusion — an excellent example of rigor without requiring advanced math.

---

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

Every artifact in this project builds toward the same striking, well-supported number: your brain costs about 20% of your body's energy just to exist, whether or not you're doing hard math. Unit I's first bottom-up estimate is a rough sketch. Unit II turns it into a genuine order-of-magnitude calculation, checked directly against Attwell and Laughlin's real, detailed energy-budget research. Unit III explains that exact number to correct a myth almost everyone believes — that thinking hard burns noticeably more calories. Unit IV shows precisely how sensitive the estimate is to its assumptions, teaching real scientific humility about estimation. Unit V asks a curriculum committee to make this the standard example bridging physics energy concepts and biology metabolism units. A science teacher or admissions reader sees a project that used nothing more than order-of-magnitude reasoning — genuinely available to any student — to arrive at a real, myth-correcting, quotable scientific fact.
