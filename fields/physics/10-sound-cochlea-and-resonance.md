# 10. Sound, Cochlea, and Resonance

*Modeling Frequency Discrimination with Resonant Cavities*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Physics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `physics/10-sound-cochlea-and-resonance`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can the physics of resonant cavities (the same principle behind why different-length organ pipes produce different pitches) explain how the cochlea's physical structure separates sound into different frequencies?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
The cochlea's tonotopic organization — different locations along its length responding to different frequencies — has a genuine physical basis in resonance and traveling-wave mechanics (the basilar membrane's varying stiffness), directly connecting intro-physics acoustic-resonance concepts to how hearing actually works.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. von Bekesy, G. (1960). Experiments in Hearing. McGraw-Hill.

Georg von Bekesy's Nobel Prize-winning research directly observed and measured traveling waves along the basilar membrane inside the cochlea, discovering that the membrane's varying stiffness and width along its length cause different frequencies to produce peak vibration at different physical locations — high frequencies near the base, low frequencies near the apex. This is the foundational, directly observed physical mechanism the project's resonant-tube analogy is built to illustrate, and it's Nobel-recognized, directly measured science, not just theory.

### 2. Robles, L., & Ruggero, M. A. (2001). Mechanics of the mammalian cochlea. Physiological Reviews, 81(3), 1305-1352.

This comprehensive review updates von Bekesy's original findings with modern, more precise measurements of cochlear mechanics, confirming and refining the tonotopic (frequency-to-position) mapping while adding detail about active amplification mechanisms in the living cochlea that von Bekesy's original cadaver-based measurements couldn't capture. It's a useful modern companion source, and it honestly notes an important limitation of the project's simple passive-resonance model: the living cochlea actively amplifies certain frequencies using a mechanism the simple resonant-tube analogy doesn't capture.

### 3. Hudspeth, A. J. (2014). Integrating the active process of hair cells with cochlear function. Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 15(9), 600-614.

Hudspeth's review focuses on how the cochlea's hair cells actively amplify weak sounds and sharpen frequency selectivity beyond what passive mechanical resonance alone would achieve — extending the story beyond simple physical resonance into active biological amplification. This is a useful, honest addition for the project's discussion section: the resonant-tube demo captures the passive mechanical basis of tonotopy well, but the real cochlea does more active, biological work on top of that passive foundation.

## Why This Project Impresses

Uses hands-on, physically buildable resonance demonstrations to illuminate a genuine anatomical mapping principle — tangible, testable, and appropriately challenging without requiring the full mathematical traveling-wave cochlear model.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

Every artifact in this project builds toward the same physical fact: your inner ear works like a set of tuned resonant tubes. Unit I builds a hands-on physical demo showing resonant frequency depends on physical dimension. Unit II turns that demo into a genuine comparison against real, published cochlear tonotopic maps — checking whether the physical model's frequency-position pattern actually matches the real biology. Unit III explains that exact validated comparison to an audience who's never realized their own hearing runs on the same physics as a musical instrument. Unit IV extends the physical demo into a continuous simulation, producing a frequency-map curve directly comparable to both the hands-on demo and the real biological data. Unit V asks for this exact demonstration to become the standard applied-physics lesson connecting acoustics and auditory biology. A physics or music-and-science mentor sees a hands-on, buildable, Nobel-Prize-grounded demonstration of one of the most elegant structure-function relationships in the entire body.
