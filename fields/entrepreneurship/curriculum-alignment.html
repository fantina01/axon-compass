Neuroscience × Entrepreneurship — All Ten Projects, All Twenty-Four Weeks#

HOW THIS DOCUMENT WORKS#

The Operational Handbook already defines how every week runs — the reading-analysis pattern, the five tournament formats (Confidence Gap, Prediction Ladder, Signal and the Noise, Distortion Chain, Self-Forecast Duel), the five laboratory formats (Build-a-System Simulation, Message Transmission Simulation, Distortion & Reconstruction Experiment, Identity & System Construction Workshop, Capstone Prototype Development), and the fixed 75-minute session shapes. None of that is repeated here.

What this document provides is the piece that was missing: exactly what content each of Entrepreneurship’s ten projects plugs into that fixed structure, week by week, for all 24 weeks. A chapter leader running “The Snack Swap Cart” should never have to guess what their Week 10 tournament station is about, or what their Week 15 lab’s manipulated stimulus should be — it’s specified here, tied directly to the real product, real participants, and real literature named in each project file, and specified so that Week 4’s Innovation Proposal, Week 8’s Research Investigation, Week 12’s communication product, Week 16’s simulation, and Week 20’s policy draft all visibly build on each other, exactly the way the Handbook’s own unit-to-unit logic requires.

This field’s defining feature — stated in every one of its ten project files — is that nothing here is hypothetical. Every “Research Investigation” is a real test on real peers. Every “Policy Draft” is a real pitch to a real decision-maker (a school store, a cafeteria, a counseling office, a local business), not a policy brief written to an imagined institution. Every entry below preserves that: Week 8’s real comparison, Week 12’s real dissemination channel, and Week 20’s real ask are all named specifically, with the real evidence each one is built on stated explicitly, the same standard of concreteness the other six alignment documents held throughout. Where a project’s honest result might be a disappointing one (Project 9’s mood board, for instance), that possibility is written into the plan itself, not smoothed over — because reporting an honest result, including a setback, is exactly what this field’s own project files repeatedly say mentors respect most.

PROJECT 1 — The Focus Kit#

A real product built from attention-span science

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Kaplan’s 1995 Attention Restoration Theory — directed attention fatigues with sustained use and can be restored by brief breaks. Pre-reading: participants guess, from their own experience, whether a 5-minute break genuinely restores focus or just feels like a pause, before any product is designed.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station has participants work on a monotonous task for a stretch, predict how their focus will hold up, then compares against a version with a short scheduled break inserted, per Ariga and Lleras’s finding. Log every predicted-vs-real gap; Week 4’s proposal cites the pattern.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each testing a different candidate kit element (a timer, a printed checklist, a phone-lockbox idea) on a small group of real peers, recording quick reactions and a rough usability read at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): build the first real version of the Focus Kit — a physical kit (timer, checklist, lockbox idea) or a digital tool (printable planner, simple app concept) built around attention-restoration research. The proposal documents the actual prototype and names who tested the first version, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and the Week 3 element-testing trials.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Ariga and Lleras’s 2011 finding that brief, occasional mental breaks measurably delay the vigilance decrement during long tasks, and Mackworth’s 1948 classic finding that sustained attention reliably declines within about 30 minutes even on motivating tasks. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: peers who use the Focus Kit for a study session report better focus and get more done than peers who study without it.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real study-session scenarios with different break structures; participants predict which structure produces the least reported drop in focus by the end.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one real peer’s self-rated focus score partway through a session. Reception receives only that score; Filtering decides whether it looks like a normal or steep drop; Integration adds whether the kit was used; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected end-of-session rating from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed rating still matches the real data.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): give the kit to one real group of peers for a study session and not to another, then compare self-rated focus and amount of work completed — this doubles as real product-testing data, not just a school assignment. The poster’s central chart is the real focus-rating and completed-work comparison between groups, stating the real numbers and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration. The feedback form peers fill out is documented as real customer feedback, disguised as a school assignment.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to pitch a real, tested product to the exact audience who needs it, without sounding like a gimmick.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 results delivered two ways — as a raw focus-rating comparison versus a short pitch video showing the kit in action — rated for which framing makes a stressed peer actually want to try it before exams.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 results, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific numbers survive retelling, and whether “focus products are gimmicks” gets addressed or creeps back in unchallenged.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce the short pitch video showing the Focus Kit in action and the real Week 8 test results, targeting students before exams, posted where students actually are — school social media, study-group chats, a table at school.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a viewer might remember “it definitely works” without recalling the real, specific before/after numbers from Week 8.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 focus-rating gap once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific numbers drift.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real before/after dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation inflates the stated gap; final recall is collected; comparison shows the inflated figure’s persistence, followed by full debrief on why honest numbers matter more to a real pitch than an inflated one.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the simple before/after tracker — focus ratings and things-completed for everyone who tried the kit, turned into an easy chart anyone can read in ten seconds, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset. Documented as a spreadsheet or one-page results chart, shared openly so anyone can see the real numbers.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how a real institutional ask (not a hypothetical policy brief) actually gets made and heard.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether the school store, library, or PTA will say yes to carrying or distributing the kit, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the school store’s, library’s, or PTA’s actual decision process to find the realistic point to make the real ask.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): write and deliver the real ask to the school store, library, or PTA — could the Focus Kit be sold or given out during exam week — citing the real Week 8 test results and the Week 16 results chart as the pitch’s evidence, aimed at the actual decision-maker (librarian, student store manager, PTA contact).

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 honest guess through a real tested product to a real institutional ask.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the table/booth setup as capstone centerpiece — the kit, the results chart, the pitch video; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test the live pitch with an unfamiliar peer playing a skeptical decision-maker.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the kit, the real test results, the pitch video, the results chart, and the real institutional ask into one draft, honestly naming what real testers said didn’t work and what would change next.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition is a real table or booth showing the kit, the results, and the pitch video, with the actual pitch made or being made to the school; reflection defense states what real people said when they tried it.

PROJECT 2 — Sleep Better Challenge#

A real peer program built on sleep science

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Carskadon’s 2011 finding that adolescents experience a genuine biological delay in their circadian rhythm. Pre-reading: participants guess whether a one-week peer sleep challenge could realistically produce a measurable change, before any program exists.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station has participants predict how much their own sleep and mood would improve over one week of consistent bedtime and reduced screen time, before any real challenge is run.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each testing one candidate challenge rule (consistent bedtime, less screen time before bed, a wind-down routine) on a small group of real peers for a few nights, recording quick self-reported reactions at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design and launch the real Sleep Better Challenge — a one-week program peers can sign up for, with a few concrete, science-backed actions and a daily check-in. The proposal documents the real program design and how peers actually sign up, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and the Week 3 rule-testing trials.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Irish et al.’s 2015 review finding real but modest evidence behind specific sleep-hygiene practices, and Owens and the Adolescent Sleep Working Group’s 2014 report on realistic, achievable behavior changes for this age group. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: peers who complete the challenge will report better sleep and better mood/focus at the end of the week than at the start.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real sleep-hygiene practices with escalating evidence strength (per Irish et al.); participants predict which practice peers will find easiest to actually sustain for a full week.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one real participant’s day-3 check-in rating. Reception receives only that rating; Filtering decides whether it looks like early improvement or not; Integration adds the participant’s actual rule-completion record; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected end-of-week rating from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed rating still matches the real data.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): real participants rate their sleep and focus before starting and again after finishing the week, and results are compared. The poster’s central chart is the real before/after comparison for every real participant, stating the real average improvement and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to share real program results — including any participants who didn’t improve — honestly enough to recruit a second, bigger round.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 results delivered two ways — as a raw before/after statistic versus “We Ran a Real Sleep Challenge — Here’s What Happened” — rated for which framing makes a peer want to sign up for round two.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 results, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific improvement numbers survive retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “We Ran a Real Sleep Challenge — Here’s What Happened,” using the actual participant results, disseminated through the same channels used to recruit the first round, so it doubles as recruitment marketing for round two.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “everyone slept way better” without recalling the real, more modest average improvement from Week 8.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 average improvement once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific number inflates.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real before/after dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation inflates the reported improvement; final recall is collected; comparison shows the inflated figure’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the simple results chart showing each real participant’s before/after numbers, shared openly — real proof, not a projection — calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how a real institutional ask to a health teacher or wellness club actually gets made and considered.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether the health teacher, counselor, or wellness club will agree to run the challenge school-wide, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the school’s health/wellness program structure to find the realistic point to make the real ask.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): deliver the real pitch to the school’s health teacher, counselor, or student wellness club to run the Sleep Better Challenge school-wide next term, using the real Week 8 results as the pitch’s evidence.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 guess through a real, run program with real improvement data to a school-wide expansion pitch.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the results chart and expansion pitch as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test the pitch with an unfamiliar peer playing the wellness-club contact.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the challenge design, the real before/after investigation, the recap video, the results chart, and the school-wide pitch into one draft, honestly naming which participants didn’t improve and why.

Week 24 (Summit): the Summit presentation is the actual pitch for running the challenge again, bigger, backed by real participants who can speak to what worked.

PROJECT 3 — The Two-Minute Reset#

A product test for a stress-relief tool

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Jerath et al.’s 2006 proposed mechanism — slow exhalation stimulates the vagus nerve, shifting the body toward its calming (parasympathetic) response. Pre-reading: participants guess, honestly, whether two minutes of slow breathing could meaningfully lower their own stress, before testing anything.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station has participants rate their stress, try two minutes of slow breathing, predict their post-breathing rating, then rate it for real.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each testing a slightly different breathing pace or format (a printed card, a two-minute audio track, a simple counted sequence) on a small group of real peers, recording before/after stress ratings at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): build the real tool — a guided breathing card, a two-minute audio track, or a simple printed sequence — based on real slow-breathing science. The proposal documents the tool and names who tested the first version, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 format-testing trials.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Zaccaro et al.’s 2018 systematic review confirming slow-paced breathing (under about 10 breaths per minute) reliably reduces physiological stress markers, and Arch and Craske’s 2006 controlled study of a short focused-breathing exercise reducing negative emotional response. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: peers who use the tool for two minutes report lower stress right afterward than before.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real breathing-pace scenarios (faster to slower); participants predict which pace produces the largest reported stress drop, calibrating toward the under-10-breaths-per-minute finding.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one real tester’s before-rating. Reception receives only that number; Filtering decides whether it’s “high” or “moderate” stress; Integration adds whether they used the tool; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected after-rating from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed rating still matches the real data.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): collect real before/after stress ratings from real testers using the tool for two minutes. The poster’s central chart is the real before/after comparison for every tester, stating the real average drop and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to pitch a tiny, fast product to the exact moment people need it — right before exams.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 results delivered two ways — as a raw before/after statistic versus a short video showing the tool being used with the real numbers on screen — rated for which framing gets a stressed student to actually try it.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 before/after numbers, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific stress-rating drop survives retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce the short video showing the tool in use and the real before/after numbers, shared where stressed students actually are — before exams, during finals week.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a viewer might remember “it instantly fixes stress” rather than the real, more modest drop found in Week 8.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 average stress-rating drop once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific number inflates.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real before/after dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation inflates the stated drop; final recall is collected; comparison shows the inflated figure’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the simple results chart of every real tester’s before/after stress rating, shared openly as proof, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how a real institutional ask (posted in classrooms, handed out at the library) actually gets approved during exam week.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether the school will agree to make the tool available during exam week, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the school’s exam-week logistics (classrooms, library, front office) to find the realistic point to distribute the tool.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): deliver the real pitch asking the school to make the tool available during exam week, citing the real Week 8 results and the Week 16 results chart, aimed at the actual decision-maker.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 honest guess through a real, fast, tested tool to a real exam-week distribution ask.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the live demo as capstone centerpiece — visitors try the tool and rate their own before/after stress; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the tool, the real before/after investigation, the video, the results chart, and the exam-week ask into one draft.

Week 24 (Summit): the Summit is the live pitch and demo — visitors try the tool themselves and rate their own before/after stress in real time; reflection defense states the real average drop found.

PROJECT 4 — Study Group Matchmaker#

A real service solving a real scheduling problem

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Roediger and Karpicke’s 2006 testing-effect finding — active retrieval strengthens memory more than passive review. Pre-reading: participants guess whether explaining material to a study partner counts as “real studying” or just feels productive, before any matching service exists.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station has participants predict how confident they’d feel on a topic after explaining it to a partner versus reviewing it alone, then test both approaches on a short topic.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each pairing two peers on a different topic to explain material to each other, recording self-rated confidence before and after at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): build the real sign-up (a form, a shared spreadsheet, a group chat) where classmates list their class and study style and get matched into small study groups. The proposal documents the real sign-up and matching method, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 pairing trials.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Chase et al.’s 2009 “protégé effect” — preparing to teach material increases effort and engagement — and Fiorella and Mayer’s 2013 finding that actually teaching (not just expecting to) produces better learning outcomes. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: students in matched study groups report better understanding and higher confidence going into a test than students who studied alone.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real study scenarios (solo review, group review without explaining, group review with explaining, etc.); participants predict which scenario produces the highest confidence rating, calibrating toward Fiorella and Mayer’s finding.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one real sign-up entry (class and study style). Reception receives only the class name; Filtering decides which group it should be matched into; Integration adds the study-style preference; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected match from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed match still matches what the real matching method would produce.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): compare real ratings from real participants who used the matching service versus those who studied solo. The poster’s central chart is the real group-versus-solo confidence comparison, stating the real numbers and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to use real participant quotes, not abstract claims, to recruit the next round of sign-ups.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 results delivered two ways — as a raw confidence-rating comparison versus “We Matched Real Study Groups — Here’s What They Said” with real quotes — rated for which framing gets more sign-ups.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 results and quotes, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific numbers and quotes survive retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “We Matched Real Study Groups — Here’s What They Said,” using real participant quotes and results, shared to recruit more sign-ups for the next round.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “everyone loved it” without recalling the real, more mixed range of participant experiences.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 confidence-rating gap once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific gap drifts.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real group-versus-solo dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation inflates the reported gap; final recall is collected; comparison shows the inflated figure’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to use real footage and real judge quotes from an actual event to promote the next one.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 results delivered two ways — as a raw feedback-format comparison versus “We Ran a Real Pitch Night — Here’s What Real Judges Said” with real footage — rated for which framing recruits more presenters and judges for next season.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 feedback-format comparison, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific usefulness-rating gap survives retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “We Ran a Real Pitch Night — Here’s What Real Judges Said,” using real footage and real quotes from the event, shared to promote next season’s event.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “every pitch got great feedback” without recalling the real, specific format comparison from Week 8.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 feedback-format usefulness gap once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific gap drifts.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real feedback-format dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation narrows the reported gap; final recall is collected; comparison shows the narrowing’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the simple results dashboard showing every real pitch’s votes/feedback scores, shared openly with all presenters, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how a real ask for co-sponsorship from a school or local business actually gets pitched using this season’s real turnout numbers.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether a school or local business will agree to co-sponsor next season’s event, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map a realistic local business’s or the school’s sponsorship-decision process to find the point to make the ask.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): deliver the real pitch to the school or a local business to co-sponsor or formally support next season’s Pitch Night, using this season’s real turnout and feedback-quality data as proof it’s worth continuing.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 recalled-feedback comparison through a real, tested feedback format and a real event to a real sponsorship pitch.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the results dashboard and sponsorship pitch as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test the pitch with an unfamiliar peer playing the business contact.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the event plan, the real feedback-format investigation, the recap video, the results dashboard, and the sponsorship pitch into one draft.

Week 24 (Summit): the Summit itself doubles as (or directly follows) the real Pitch Night — the capstone presentation is genuinely public, in front of real outside judges, not just teachers and classmates; reflection defense states the real feedback-format comparison found.

CROSS-PROJECT NOTE#

Every project above runs the same 24-week skeleton from the Operational Handbook — five tournaments, five labs, five main outputs building toward one capstone. Every one of Entrepreneurship’s ten projects was built to the same depth throughout, with the field’s defining discipline carried into every single week rather than only its own unit: Week 4’s prototype is a real thing real people tried; Week 8’s investigation is a real comparison with real participants, not a hypothetical study; Week 12’s communication product is aimed at a real, specific audience through a real channel; Week 16’s “simulation” is an honest chart of real results, not a projection; and Week 20’s policy draft is a real ask delivered to a real, named decision-maker — a school store, a cafeteria, a counseling office, student government, a local business — never a hypothetical policy brief. Where a real result might be mixed or disappointing (Project 9’s mood board, Project 6’s notification detox), that possibility is built into the plan itself from Week 5 onward, because reporting an honest result — including a setback — is exactly what this field’s own project files say mentors and accelerators respect most.
