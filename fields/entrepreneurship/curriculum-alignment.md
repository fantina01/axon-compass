### HOW THIS DOCUMENT WORKS

The Operational Handbook already defines *how* every week runs — the reading-analysis pattern, the five tournament formats (Confidence Gap, Prediction Ladder, Signal and the Noise, Distortion Chain, Self-Forecast Duel), the five laboratory formats (Build-a-System Simulation, Message Transmission Simulation, Distortion & Reconstruction Experiment, Identity & System Construction Workshop, Capstone Prototype Development), and the fixed 75-minute session shapes. None of that is repeated here.

What this document provides is the piece that was missing: **exactly what content each of Entrepreneurship's ten projects plugs into that fixed structure, week by week, for all 24 weeks.** A chapter leader running "The Snack Swap Cart" should never have to guess what their Week 10 tournament station is about, or what their Week 15 lab's manipulated stimulus should be — it's specified here, tied directly to the real product, real participants, and real literature named in each project file, and specified so that Week 4's Innovation Proposal, Week 8's Research Investigation, Week 12's communication product, Week 16's simulation, and Week 20's policy draft all visibly build on each other, exactly the way the Handbook's own unit-to-unit logic requires.

This field's defining feature — stated in every one of its ten project files — is that nothing here is hypothetical. Every "Research Investigation" is a real test on real peers. Every "Policy Draft" is a real pitch to a real decision-maker (a school store, a cafeteria, a counseling office, a local business), not a policy brief written to an imagined institution. Every entry below preserves that: Week 8's real comparison, Week 12's real dissemination channel, and Week 20's real ask are all named specifically, with the real evidence each one is built on stated explicitly, the same standard of concreteness the other six alignment documents held throughout. Where a project's honest result might be a disappointing one (Project 9's mood board, for instance), that possibility is written into the plan itself, not smoothed over — because reporting an honest result, including a setback, is exactly what this field's own project files repeatedly say mentors respect most.

## PROJECT 1 — The Focus Kit
*A real product built from attention-span science*

#### UNIT I — Weeks 1–4

**Week 1 (Reading):** Article I focus is Kaplan's 1995 Attention Restoration Theory — directed attention fatigues with sustained use and can be restored by brief breaks. Pre-reading: participants guess, from their own experience, whether a 5-minute break genuinely restores focus or just feels like a pause, before any product is designed.

**Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP):** the station has participants work on a monotonous task for a stretch, predict how their focus will hold up, then compares against a version with a short scheduled break inserted, per Ariga and Lleras's finding. Log every predicted-vs-real gap; Week 4's proposal cites the pattern.

**Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment):** three trials, each testing a different candidate kit element (a timer, a printed checklist, a phone-lockbox idea) on a small group of real peers, recording quick reactions and a rough usability read at every trial.

**Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I):** build the first real version of the Focus Kit — a physical kit (timer, checklist, lockbox idea) or a digital tool (printable planner, simple app concept) built around attention-restoration research. The proposal documents the actual prototype and names who tested the first version, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and the Week 3 element-testing trials.

#### UNIT II — Weeks 5–8

**Week 5 (Reading):** Article II focus is Ariga and Lleras's 2011 finding that brief, occasional mental breaks measurably delay the vigilance decrement during long tasks, and Mackworth's 1948 classic finding that sustained attention reliably declines within about 30 minutes even on motivating tasks. Research Summary II must state the season's testable hypothesis precisely: peers who use the Focus Kit for a study session report better focus and get more done than peers who study without it.

**Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER):** stations present five real study-session scenarios with different break structures; participants predict which structure produces the least reported drop in focus by the end.

**Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION):** the stimulus is one real peer's self-rated focus score partway through a session. Reception receives only that score; Filtering decides whether it looks like a normal or steep drop; Integration adds whether the kit was used; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected end-of-session rating from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed rating still matches the real data.

**Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation):** give the kit to one real group of peers for a study session and not to another, then compare self-rated focus and amount of work completed — this doubles as real product-testing data, not just a school assignment. The poster's central chart is the real focus-rating and completed-work comparison between groups, stating the real numbers and citing the Week 6 tournament's calibration. The feedback form peers fill out is documented as real customer feedback, disguised as a school assignment.

#### UNIT III — Weeks 9–12

**Week 9 (Reading):** Article III focus is how to pitch a real, tested product to the exact audience who needs it, without sounding like a gimmick.

**Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE):** the real Week 8 results delivered two ways — as a raw focus-rating comparison versus a short pitch video showing the kit in action — rated for which framing makes a stressed peer actually want to try it before exams.

**Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION):** the ground truth is the real Week 8 results, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific numbers survive retelling, and whether "focus products are gimmicks" gets addressed or creeps back in unchallenged.

**Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project):** produce the short pitch video showing the Focus Kit in action and the real Week 8 test results, targeting students before exams, posted where students actually are — school social media, study-group chats, a table at school.

#### UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16

**Week 13 (Reading):** Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a viewer might remember "it definitely works" without recalling the real, specific before/after numbers from Week 8.

**Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN):** state the real Week 8 focus-rating gap once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific numbers drift.

**Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT):** observation is the real before/after dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation inflates the stated gap; final recall is collected; comparison shows the inflated figure's persistence, followed by full debrief on why honest numbers matter more to a real pitch than an inflated one.

**Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation):** build the simple before/after tracker — focus ratings and things-completed for everyone who tried the kit, turned into an easy chart anyone can read in ten seconds, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset. Documented as a spreadsheet or one-page results chart, shared openly so anyone can see the real numbers.

#### UNIT V — Weeks 17–20

**Week 17 (Reading):** Integration article focus — applied to how a real institutional ask (not a hypothetical policy brief) actually gets made and heard.

**Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL):** sealed predictions about whether the school store, library, or PTA will say yes to carrying or distributing the kit, opened in Week 19.

**Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP):** map the school store's, library's, or PTA's actual decision process to find the realistic point to make the real ask.

**Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation):** write and deliver the real ask to the school store, library, or PTA — could the Focus Kit be sold or given out during exam week — citing the real Week 8 test results and the Week 16 results chart as the pitch's evidence, aimed at the actual decision-maker (librarian, student store manager, PTA contact).

#### UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24

**Week 21 (Synthesis):** the throughline runs from a Week 1 honest guess through a real tested product to a real institutional ask.

**Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT):** finalize the table/booth setup as capstone centerpiece — the kit, the results chart, the pitch video; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test the live pitch with an unfamiliar peer playing a skeptical decision-maker.

**Week 23 (Capstone Draft):** integrate the kit, the real test results, the pitch video, the results chart, and the real institutional ask into one draft, honestly naming what real testers said didn't work and what would change next.

**Week 24 (Summit):** exhibition is a real table or booth showing the kit, the results, and the pitch video, with the actual pitch made or being made to the school; reflection defense states what real people said when they tried it.

## PROJECT 2 — Sleep Better Challenge
*A real peer program built on sleep science*

**Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment):** three trials, each testing one candidate challenge rule (consistent bedtime, less screen time before bed, a wind-down routine) on a small group of real peers for a few nights, recording quick self-reported reactions at every trial.

**Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I):** design and launch the real Sleep Better Challenge — a one-week program peers can sign up for, with a few concrete, science-backed actions and a daily check-in. The proposal documents the real program design and how peers actually sign up, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and the Week 3 rule-testing trials.

#### UNIT II — Weeks 5–8

**Week 5 (Reading):** Article II focus is Irish et al.'s 2015 review finding real but modest evidence behind specific sleep-hygiene practices, and Owens and the Adolescent Sleep Working Group's 2014 report on realistic, achievable behavior changes for this age group. **Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER):** stations present five real sleep-hygiene practices with escalating evidence strength (per Irish et al.); participants predict which practice peers will find easiest to actually sustain for a full week.

**Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL):** sealed predictions about whether the health teacher, counselor, or wellness club will agree to run the challenge school-wide, opened in Week 19.

## PROJECT 3 — The Two-Minute Reset
*A product test for a stress-relief tool*

## PROJECT 6 — The Notification Detox Challenge
*A real program testing focus and phone habits*

#### UNIT I — Weeks 1–4

**Week 1 (Reading):** Article I focus is Ferster and Skinner's 1957 finding that variable-ratio reinforcement — reward after an unpredictable number of actions — produces the most persistent, hard-to-disengage-from behavior of any reward schedule. Pre-reading: participants count how many notifications they'd guess they receive in a typical day, before tracking a real number.

**Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP):** the station has participants predict how their own focus would change over one distraction-free hour with notifications off versus on, before actually trying both.

**Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment):** three trials, each a short work period with notifications either on or off, recording a quick self-rated focus/restlessness score at every trial.

The proposal documents the real program rules and sign-up method, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial pattern.

**Week 5 (Reading):** Article II focus is Kushlev, Proulx, and Dunn's 2016 controlled finding that notifications-on participants reported significantly higher inattention and restlessness than notifications-off participants, and Kushlev and Dunn's 2019 finding that notifications reduce felt connection during time with others. ### CROSS-PROJECT NOTE

Every project above runs the same 24-week skeleton from the Operational Handbook — five tournaments, five labs, five main outputs building toward one capstone. Every one of Entrepreneurship's ten projects was built to the same depth throughout, with the field's defining discipline carried into every single week rather than only its own unit: Week 4's prototype is a real thing real people tried; Week 8's investigation is a real comparison with real participants, not a hypothetical study; Week 12's communication product is aimed at a real, specific audience through a real channel; Week 16's "simulation" is an honest chart of real results, not a projection; and Week 20's policy draft is a real ask delivered to a real, named decision-maker — a school store, a cafeteria, a counseling office, student government, a local business — never a hypothetical policy brief. Where a real result might be mixed or disappointing (Project 9's mood board, Project 6's notification detox), that possibility is built into the plan itself from Week 5 onward, because reporting an honest result — including a setback — is exactly what this field's own project files say mentors and accelerators respect most.
