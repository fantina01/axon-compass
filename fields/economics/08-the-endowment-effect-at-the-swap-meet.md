# 8. The Endowment Effect at the Swap Meet

*Ownership, Value, and School Resale Markets*

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Does the endowment effect (overvaluing items merely because one owns them) measurably distort peer-to-peer resale prices among students, and can awareness reduce pricing inefficiency?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters

```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Kahneman, D., Knetsch, J. L., & Thaler, R. H. (1990). Experimental tests of the endowment effect and the Coase theorem. Journal of Political Economy, 98(6), 1325–1348.

This is the classic "mug experiment": participants randomly given a coffee mug demanded roughly twice as much money to give it up as other participants were willing to pay to acquire the identical mug — despite having owned it for only a few minutes. This large, consistent gap between "willingness to accept" and "willingness to pay" is the endowment effect, and this paper is its founding empirical demonstration, directly mirrored by the project's own WTA/WTP measurement design.

### 2. Thaler, R. H. (1980). Toward a positive theory of consumer choice. Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 1(1), 39–60.

Thaler's earlier paper first proposed the endowment effect as a theoretical idea, connecting it to Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory: giving up an owned item is coded psychologically as a loss, and losses are weighted more heavily than equivalent gains (loss aversion), which is why people demand more to part with something than they'd pay to get it in the first place. This paper is useful for the project because it makes the theoretical link between the endowment effect and loss aversion explicit, tying two of the project's related literature threads together.

### 3. List, J. A. (2003). Does market experience eliminate market anomalies? Quarterly Journal of Economics, 118(1), 41–71.

List tested the endowment effect in a real-world marketplace setting (a sports-card trading show) and found that participants with more market trading experience showed a smaller endowment-effect gap than novice traders — suggesting the effect can shrink with practice and experience, not just theoretical awareness. This is directly relevant to the project's school resale-market context: it suggests students who trade more frequently on the platform may show smaller WTA/WTP gaps over time, a testable extension of the core hypothesis.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

What makes this portfolio unusually strong is that it's genuinely original — most students citing the endowment effect just describe Kahneman's mug experiment. This student ran their own version, on their own school's actual resale economy. Unit I's WTA/WTP pilot data is real and self-collected. Unit II's full experiment measures the actual gap, and whether training narrows it. Unit III explains that specific, self-collected finding to the exact community it came from. Unit IV's asymmetric valuation model visualizes what the Unit II data actually showed. Unit V's ask — an awareness tip built into the school's own resale platform — is something the school could genuinely implement next week. Because every stage draws on data the student personally collected from their own peer marketplace, the whole portfolio reads as original research, not a repeated classic experiment — which is precisely the distinction admissions readers and research mentors are trained to notice and reward.
