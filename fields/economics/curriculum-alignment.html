AXON REVIEW — FULL CURRICULUM ALIGNMENT#

Neuroscience × Economics — All Ten Projects, All Twenty-Four Weeks#

HOW THIS DOCUMENT WORKS#

The Operational Handbook defines how every week runs — the reading-analysis pattern, the five tournament formats (Confidence Gap, Prediction Ladder, Signal and the Noise, Distortion Chain, Self-Forecast Duel), the five laboratory formats (Build-a-System Simulation, Message Transmission Simulation, Distortion & Reconstruction Experiment, Identity & System Construction Workshop, Capstone Prototype Development), and the fixed 75-minute session shapes. None of that is repeated here.

This document goes one level deeper than the Education alignment did: every week below doesn’t just name what content plugs into the fixed structure, it explains how to actually build that content — with concrete numbers, sample wording, and explicit statements of which earlier week’s evidence each step depends on. That standard is held constant across all ten projects below, start to finish — Project 10’s Week 24 reads no thinner than Project 1’s Week 1.

PROJECT 1 — The Cost of Now#

Modeling temporal discounting in teen financial decisions

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is delay discounting — the finding that people value a reward less the further away it is, and that this discount is steeper for near-term delays than long-term ones (hyperbolic, not straight-line). Before the reading, have every participant answer two concrete choice questions and record the exact answers: “Would you rather have $20 today or $30 in one month?” and “Would you rather have $20 in six months or $30 in seven months?” Most people flip their preference between these two logically-equivalent-shaped questions, and that flip is the pre-reading hypothesis the whole unit tests. Research Summary I must name the discounting mechanism, restate the $20/$30 example in the participant’s own words, and propose one real financial habit (of a teenager, specifically) this mechanism might explain.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station is a short quiz on real published discount-rate statistics. Participants predict their own quiz score before taking it, then compare — this builds the habit of checking gut intuition against real published numbers, which is exactly the discipline the Unit II hypothesis will require. Log every scorecard; Week 4’s proposal cites the size of the average confidence-accuracy gap as motivating evidence.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): run the same $20-today-vs-$30-later choice question three times across the session, each time changing one variable (the delay length, the dollar gap, or the framing as “gain” vs. “avoiding a loss”). Record every participant’s choice at every trial. The reflection question is specific: “At what point did your own answer flip, and does that match a hyperbolic curve or a straight line?”

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): build the Discounting-Aware Financial Literacy Module — a short lesson explaining the beta-delta discounting model in plain terms, paired with concrete counter-strategies (automatic transfers, commitment devices). The proposal must cite three specific pieces of evidence: the Week 1 pre-reading flip rate, the Week 2 confidence-accuracy gap size, and the Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Laibson’s hyperbolic discounting model and McClure et al.’s brain-imaging evidence. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: “Peers exposed to the discounting-aware module will show reduced discount rates on a follow-up choice task, and higher self-reported savings intentions, compared to peers who receive standard financial-literacy content covering the same amount of time.”

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): build stations from five real discounting scenarios with escalating dollar amounts, all with a one-month delay. Participants predict at which dollar level most of the room will switch preference, rehearsing the pattern that people discount small amounts more steeply than large ones.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a realistic financial decision scenario. Pass it through the five roles — Reception receives it vaguely, Filtering decides what matters, Integration adds a prior-experience card, Reconstruction rebuilds it from memory, Decision makes the final call. Log exactly which detail got dropped or distorted by the time Decision received it.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the real choice-task comparison: one group studies the discounting-aware module, a matched control group studies standard content, then both complete an identical discount-rate choice task and a savings-intention self-report. The poster’s central chart plots discount rate for both groups side by side, citing the Week 6 tournament’s pattern directly.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how communication framing shapes whether a message about self-control lands or gets dismissed as preachy.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): prepare the Week 8 finding in two scripted framings — clinical vs. personal — read aloud to different small groups, rating convincingness and relatability. The framing that scores higher becomes the required tone for Week 12.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground-truth message is the real Week 8 finding. Team 2 delivers it three ways; Team 3 tracks which facts survive; Team 4 reconstructs from memory; Team 5 decides whether to trust and share it. Log which delivery style preserved the most accurate version.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Why $20 Today Beats $30 Next Month (In Your Brain)” as a 3–6 minute podcast, opening with the Week 1 flip-question, presenting the Week 8 data with the winning framing, closing with the Week 4 counter-strategies.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why someone might remember being “usually patient with money” even when their choice-task data says otherwise.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): read a short five-fact financial scenario, distract, recall again. Score how many facts survived and flag exaggerations — this quantifies the drift the Week 16 simulator needs to account for.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is a story about waiting for a larger reward; initial recall is collected; the manipulation subtly changes the dollar amount or delay; final recall is collected; compare, then run the full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive discounting-curve simulator — sliders for discount rate and delay, producing a live subjective-value curve — calibrated using the real Week 8 crossover points.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how a school’s financial-literacy curriculum became “identity” without incorporating discounting science.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): each team seals three predictions about their policy pitch’s reception, opened and scored in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the school’s financial-literacy program — origin, influence, stakeholder, environmental, system — to determine the policy’s realistic feasibility.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the curriculum-standard recommendation, citing Week 8’s data and Week 16’s model, addressing feasibility objections directly using the Week 19 map.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline traces the $20-vs-$30 question from Week 1 through a tested finding, a podcast, a working simulator, to a policy proposal.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): confirm the simulator as capstone; map at least three evidence links; finalize the interface; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the module, investigation, podcast, simulator, and policy draft with a real implementation plan.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition pairs the module with the live simulator; reflection defense states real limitations honestly.

PROJECT 2 — Loss Aversion in the Lunch Line#

A real cafeteria signage test using loss-framed messaging

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is prospect theory and loss aversion. Before reading, have participants rate how bad losing $10 feels versus how good finding $10 feels — the gap is the pre-reading data point. Research Summary I must define the reference-point concept and give one non-cafeteria example.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): present a gain-framed gamble (“keep $5, or flip for $12 or nothing”), then a loss-framed version of the identical math. Log the prediction-versus-actual mismatch across framings.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials presenting the same food choice under no message, a gain-framed message, and a loss-framed message using printed mock signage, recording the choice at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the real redesigned cafeteria signage and its placement, citing the Week 1 rating gap, Week 2 reversal data, and Week 3 trial results.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Thaler and Sunstein’s choice-architecture framework and the Hanks/Just/Wansink/Smith cafeteria field study. Research Summary II states the hypothesis: loss-framed signage increases healthy selections versus control or gain-framed signage, without price or option changes.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real published nudge-study outcomes; participants predict which produced the larger effect, calibrating realistic expectations.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a student’s lunch-line journey passed through the five roles, dramatizing how many places an intended nudge can get lost before reaching an actual food choice.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the real A/B signage rotation across real cafeteria service periods, tallying selection rate by condition. The poster’s central chart is a three-bar comparison, citing Week 6’s effect-size discussion.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how a persuasive message’s tone determines whether an audience feels nudged helpfully or manipulated.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): two framings of the Week 8 finding — “a trick that works on your brain” vs. “a free way to make healthy easier to notice” — tested for defensiveness versus interest.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real signage result, passed through the five roles simulating how it might travel from the team to the wider student body without distortion into “they tricked us.”

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “We Tested Food Signs on Real Students — Here’s What Happened,” using the winning framing, presented to student government and cafeteria staff.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why staff might remember “no real difference” despite the real tally data.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): read the real Week 8 percentage once, distract, recall — quantifying how a real number degrades into a vaguer impression.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real tally sheet; initial recall is collected; the manipulation alters one number; final recall is collected; comparison shows how easily a false number sticks, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive prospect-theory value-curve tool, calibrated so its steepness matches the chapter’s own Week 1 loss/gain rating gap.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how cafeteria decisions are embedded in existing food-service contracts.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether nutrition-services staff will make the signage permanent, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map who controls signage decisions and what a permanent change would realistically cost.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the district signage-guideline recommendation, citing Week 8’s data and the Week 19 approval pathway.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from the Week 1 rating gap through real cafeteria data to a near-free policy ask.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the value-curve simulator, evidence-mapped to prospect theory and the real data; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the signage, study, video, model, and policy draft.

Week 24 (Summit): real signage photos displayed beside the live simulator; reflection defense addresses sample-size limits honestly.

PROJECT 3 — The Dopamine Auction#

Modeling reward prediction error in consumer bidding behavior

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Schultz’s reward-prediction-error findings. Pre-reading: recall a real time bidding/competing got the participant caught up, rating surprise versus excitement separately.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): run a genuine miniature silent auction with play-money bids; participants predict the final winning bid before it closes. The underestimate gap is the first real evidence of escalating auction excitement.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three consecutive mock auctions for different items, tracking each participant’s own bid pattern across all three for signs of escalation.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Auction Awareness Toolkit using the team’s own real Week 2 data as demonstration, citing Week 1’s gap and Week 3’s escalation pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Thaler’s “winner’s curse.” Research Summary II states the hypothesis: informed participants will bid closer to a stated rational value than uninformed participants.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real past auction outcomes from the team’s own data; participants predict which bidder overbid the most relative to real value.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a live bidding moment narrated through the five roles, dramatizing how escalation compounds bid over bid.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run two mock-auction sessions (toolkit vs. no toolkit), comparing average bid-to-value ratio. The poster’s chart shows overbid percentage by condition, citing Week 6’s winner’s-curse framing.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is whether excitement, as a topic, lands as a warning or a fun psychology reveal.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the Week 8 finding delivered as a warning versus a fun reveal, tested for which framing participants say they’d actually apply.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real overbid finding, passed through the five roles testing whether the actionable advice survives retelling or gets dropped.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Your Brain on Bidding Wars,” placing actionable advice prominently per Week 11’s finding, distributed to consumer-education partners and the economics club.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a bidder might remember staying calm despite real escalation in their bid history.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): show a real anonymized bid sequence, distract, recall the exact winning bid — quantifying how the specific number degrades.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is a real auction sequence; initial recall is collected; the manipulation alters a bid amount; final recall is collected; comparison shows the false number’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive reward-prediction-error simulator, calibrated using the real escalation pattern from Week 3.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how “getting a good deal” ties to self-image as a smart shopper.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether a real consumer-protection organization will review the toolkit, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the consumer-protection landscape to find where this toolkit’s specific angle adds something new.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the auction/bidding-awareness module recommendation, citing Week 8’s data and Week 16’s model, aimed at the organization from Week 19.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a mock auction’s real data to a specific pitch to a real organization.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the prediction-error simulator, evidence-mapped to Schultz’s mechanism and the real bid data.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the toolkit, investigation, video, model, and policy ask.

Week 24 (Summit): a live mock auction runs at the exhibition table, letting visitors bid and see their own prediction-error spike.

PROJECT 4 — Stress Hormones and Bad Trades#

A stress-aware decision protocol tested under real time pressure

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Coates and Herbert’s real trading-floor cortisol study. Pre-reading: recall a rushed decision and rate whether the same choice would hold up calmly.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): a decision-game station run with and without a visible countdown timer; participants predict whether the timer changes their choices before finding out.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials of a risk-choice game under escalating time pressure (none, 30s, 10s), tracking risk tolerance shifts.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Stress-Aware Decision Protocol, citing Week 1’s reflection, Week 2’s timer data, and Week 3’s pressure trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Arnsten’s mechanism for why acute stress impairs prefrontal function. Research Summary II states the hypothesis: protocol users under pressure reach higher-quality decisions than non-users.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real negotiation-under-pressure scenarios; participants predict outcome quality, building the rubric vocabulary Unit II requires.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a negotiation under a countdown timer, passed through the five roles, dramatizing exactly where a rushed decision drifts from a careful one.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the real between-subjects negotiation exercise, scoring outcomes with a shared rubric. The poster compares rubric scores by condition, citing Week 6’s rubric vocabulary.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is whether “stay calm” advice lands as useful or dismissive.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the Week 8 finding framed as generic advice versus a specific mechanism-based protocol, tested for credibility with students facing real high-stakes moments.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real rubric-score finding, tested through the five roles for whether the specific protocol survives retelling intact.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Why Diplomats Train Their Brains, Not Just Their Arguments,” targeted at Model UN, debate, and personal-finance clubs.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a stressed decision-maker might remember more deliberation than actually occurred.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): show a real negotiation outcome, distract, recall the exact terms — quantifying detail loss under delay.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is a real negotiation transcript; initial recall is collected; the manipulation alters a term; final recall is collected; comparison shows the false term’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive stress-risk-tolerance curve, calibrated using Week 3’s real time-pressure data.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how “grace under pressure” ties to identity in competitive activities.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether a youth investing platform will adopt the protocol, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map where high-pressure decisions already happen in the participants’ own community to find the most realistic adoption point.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the stress-awareness module recommendation, citing Week 8’s data and Week 16’s model, aimed at the platform from Week 19.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a measured negotiation-quality difference to a pitch to a real educational platform.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the stress-risk model, evidence-mapped to Arnsten’s mechanism and the real rubric data.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the protocol, investigation, video, model, and policy ask.

Week 24 (Summit): the live negotiation exercise runs as an interactive demo; reflection defense addresses the small-sample limitation honestly.

PROJECT 5 — The Scarcity Mindset#

A bandwidth-preserving decision aid tested via ethical simulation

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir & Zhao’s finding on scarcity and cognitive performance, including the honest caveat about a later statistical critique. Pre-reading: recall a time worry made an unrelated task hard to focus on.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): a mildly demanding budgeting task followed by an unrelated cognitive task; participants predict their unrelated-task score first. Kept genuinely low-stress per the Ethics Checklist.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials comparing unrelated-task performance with and without a preceding budgeting “load.”

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Bandwidth-Preserving Decision Aid, citing Week 1’s reflection, Week 2’s confidence data, and Week 3’s trial comparison.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Mullainathan & Shafir’s framework and Shah, Mullainathan & Shafir’s tunneling finding. Research Summary II states the hypothesis: scarcity-condition participants using the aid outperform those without it.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real scarcity-study outcomes; participants predict which showed the larger gap, calibrating realistic expectations.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a simulated budgeting decision under mild, ethical scarcity, passed through the five roles, dramatizing how scarcity narrows attention.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the real 2×2 design (scarcity/no-scarcity × aid/no-aid), scoring unrelated-task performance. The poster is a 2×2 grid of average scores, citing Week 6’s discussion.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is whether a poverty-and-cognition message reinforces stigma or genuinely humanizes.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the Week 8 finding framed as “poverty causes bad decisions” versus “scarcity taxes anyone’s bandwidth,” tested for avoiding stigma.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real finding, tested through the five roles specifically for whether the non-stigmatizing framing survives — the most important checkpoint in the project.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce the documentary using only the verified non-stigmatizing framing, reviewed by an adult advisor before release, targeting poverty-focused nonprofits.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a decision might be remembered as “just a bad choice” without its scarcity context.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): show a real anonymized budgeting decision, distract, recall — quantifying how easily the context (not just the decision) gets dropped.

PROJECT 6 — Social Proof and the Neural Herd#

A social-spending-awareness module tested with real peer data

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Berns et al.’s conformity study and Asch’s classic experiments. Pre-reading: name one real “everyone had it” purchase and rate its real influence honestly.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): an Asch-style station with planted wrong answers on an objective judgment; participants predict their own resistance beforehand. Log real conformity versus predicted resistance.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials of a preference choice, run privately then again after fake “most people chose X” statistics, tracking shift.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Social Spending Awareness Module, citing Week 1’s self-reported estimate, Week 2’s conformity data, and Week 3’s shift pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is the neural conformity mechanism and Cialdini & Goldstein’s review. Research Summary II states the hypothesis: module completers report reduced peer-influenced purchase intention versus control.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five “trending item” scenarios; participants predict how much a fake popularity statistic shifts intent.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a purchase decision under social pressure, passed through the five roles, dramatizing where social value overrides practical value.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the pre/post survey with a matched control class. The poster compares scores by condition, citing Week 6’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the Week 8 finding framed as a warning versus a relatable story, tested for resonance.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real finding, tested through the five roles for which framing survives retelling better.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Why Everyone Buying It Makes Your Brain Want It Too,” using the relatable framing, distributed through teen finance platforms.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

PROJECT 7 — Anchoring in the Aisles#

Testing how fake “original prices” distort real value judgments

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Tversky and Kahneman’s anchoring demonstration. Pre-reading: show an arbitrary two-digit number, then ask an unrelated estimate — the correlation previews the mechanism.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): participants estimate a “fair price” after seeing a high or low anchor, predicting how close their estimate lands to the group average.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials of a mock-store price-judgment task with different fake-discount sizes, tracking estimated fair value.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Anchor-Resistant Shopping Toolkit, citing Week 1’s demonstration, Week 2’s data, and Week 3’s trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Ariely, Loewenstein & Prelec’s arbitrary-anchor study and Nunes & Boatwright’s field study. Research Summary II states the hypothesis: toolkit users show a smaller anchoring deviation than controls.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real pricing scenarios; participants predict which produces the largest value shift.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a shopper encountering a price tag, passed through the five roles, dramatizing how far valuation can drift.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the between-subjects mock-store task, measuring deviation from a true value. The poster compares deviation by condition.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is whether a consumer-protection message lands as alarmist or empowering.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the Week 8 finding framed as “retailers are tricking you” versus “a free sharper-shopper skill,” tested for practical uptake.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real deviation-reduction finding, tested through the five roles for which framing keeps checklist steps intact.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “That ‘Was $80, Now $40’ Sign Is Playing Your Brain,” using the empowering framing, distributed to consumer-education nonprofits.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why “I got a great deal” persists regardless of real numbers.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): show a real anchored scenario, distract, recall the exact discount percentage — quantifying degradation.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is a real scenario; initial recall is collected; the manipulation alters the discount size; final recall is collected; comparison shows the false number’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive reference-dependent valuation curve, calibrated with Week 8’s real deviation data.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

PROJECT 8 — The Endowment Effect at the Swap Meet#

An original willingness-to-pay/willingness-to-accept study on the school’s own resale economy

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler’s mug experiment. Pre-reading: give half the room an item, collect real WTA from owners and WTP from non-owners for an identical item.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): predict the ownership-price gap size before repeating the Week 1 exercise with a new item.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials with different items and fresh owner/non-owner splits, tracking gap consistency.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Endowment-Aware Pricing Guide, citing the real Week 1 gap, Week 2 prediction data, and Week 3 consistency pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Thaler’s loss-aversion framing and List’s market-experience finding. Research Summary II states the hypothesis: real sellers show a WTA/WTP gap, and trained peers show a smaller gap.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real resale-platform item categories; participants predict which shows the largest gap.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a real resale listing decision, passed through the five roles, dramatizing where the owned-item premium enters.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the full WTA/WTP experiment with and without training. The poster compares gap size by condition, citing Week 6’s predictions.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the Week 8 finding framed as criticism versus a pricing hack, tested for real usability.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real gap finding, tested through the five roles for which framing survives as useful advice.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Why You Think Your Stuff Is Worth More Than It Is,” shared through the school marketplace group.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a seller might remember pricing fairly despite real overvaluation data.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): show a real listing price and fair value, distract, recall the exact gap — quantifying degradation.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive asymmetric-valuation model, calibrated with Week 8’s real dollar-gap data.

PROJECT 9 — Delay of Gratification, Revisited#

A context-controlled replication of the “marshmallow test” logic

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Mischel’s original studies. Pre-reading: predict how strongly a child’s delay ability would predict later success — most predict strongly, previewing the tension Week 5 complicates.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): run a real age-appropriate delay task; participants predict their own wait time first.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials of a simulated-game delay task, tracking whether trust in the “later payout” changes wait-choice across trials.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Context-Controlled Delay Task, citing Week 1’s reflection, Week 2’s data, and Week 3’s trust pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Watts, Duncan & Quan’s 2018 replication. Research Summary II states the hypothesis precisely, modeled on the real replication’s design: the correlation shrinks once context is controlled.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five scenarios where a correlation shrank after adding a control; participants predict which shrank the most.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a child’s delay decision, passed through the five roles, dramatizing how easily context gets dropped, leaving only “has willpower or doesn’t.”

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the correlational study with covariate control. The poster shows both correlation values side by side, citing Week 6’s training.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is communicating nuance without sounding dismissive of the popular claim.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the Week 8 finding framed as “debunked” versus “measured something real, just not what people assumed,” tested for credibility.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real shrinkage finding, tested through the five roles for whether nuance survives or collapses into a headline.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “The Marshmallow Test Doesn’t Mean What You Think,” distributed to psychology/economics clubs and teacher PD.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, testing whether Unit III’s nuanced framing actually stuck.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): read the real finding once, distract, restate — checking whether the covariate-control qualifier survives.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the precise Week 8 finding; initial recall is collected; the manipulation drops the qualifier; final recall is collected; comparison shows the nuance loss, followed by full debrief connecting to science-reporting oversimplification generally.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive confound-visualization tool using the chapter’s own real Week 8 data as the worked example.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how “grit” narratives are embedded in youth-program identity.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about a mobility nonprofit engaging with the nuanced framing, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map how “teach delay” became a default policy response, finding room for a context-aware alternative.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation against over-relying on delay-focused messaging, citing Week 8’s data and Week 16’s model.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a famous claim, re-tested carefully, to more responsible policy advice.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the confound-visualization tool, evidence-mapped to Watts et al. and the chapter’s real data.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the task, investigation, video, model, and recommendation.

Week 24 (Summit): reflection defense frames the project as a case study in scientific self-correction.

PROJECT 10 — Neuroeconomics of Fairness#

An original classroom Ultimatum Game and its policy application

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is the Ultimatum Game’s basic logic. Pre-reading: record real yes/no answers to a specific unfair-split scenario before any theory.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): run a real round with play money; participants predict their own decision at a specific split before receiving it.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials with three split ratios, recording every real accept/reject decision.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Fairness-Aware Ultimatum Simulator, citing Week 1’s answer, Week 2’s data, and Week 3’s rejection pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Sanfey et al.’s imaging study. Research Summary II states the hypothesis: rejection rates rise sharply with unfairness.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five split ratios; participants predict the real rejection percentage for each.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is receiving an unfair offer, passed through the five roles, dramatizing how emotion drives the decision.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the repeated-measures game across real splits. The poster plots rejection rate against unfairness, citing Week 6’s predictions.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is whether the finding lands as trivia or meaningful institutional insight.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the Week 8 finding framed as trivia versus institutional relevance, tested for real-world connection.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real rejection finding, tested through the five roles for whether institutional relevance survives.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Why We’d Rather Get Nothing Than Get Cheated,” tying it to fairness in school resource allocation.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a rejection might be remembered as obvious rather than close.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): show a real offer-decision pair, distract, recall the exact split — quantifying degradation.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is a real offer; initial recall is collected; the manipulation alters the split; final recall is collected; comparison shows persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive rejection-probability model, calibrated directly with Week 8’s real curve.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how fairness perception ties to group trust and legitimacy.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about student-government adoption of fairness thresholds, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map current allocation decisions to find where a fairness threshold applies.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the fundraiser profit-sharing recommendation, citing Week 8’s data and Week 16’s model.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a classic paradigm recreated to a real, current student-government decision.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the rejection-probability model, evidence-mapped to Sanfey et al. and the real data.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the game, investigation, video, model, and policy ask.

Week 24 (Summit): the live game runs as an interactive demo, letting visitors compare their own choice to the chapter’s real rejection-rate curve.
