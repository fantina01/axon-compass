# 5. The Scarcity Mindset

*Cognitive Bandwidth and Decisions Under Financial Stress*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Economics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `economics/05-the-scarcity-mindset`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Does financial scarcity consume cognitive bandwidth in ways that impair unrelated decision-making, and can simple bandwidth-preserving interventions help?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
The scarcity-mindset research (Mullainathan & Shafir) found that financial worry taxes cognitive resources similarly to a significant IQ-point loss — a striking, policy-relevant finding for programs serving low-income populations that this project can test in a scaled-down, ethical classroom analogue.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Mani, A., Mullainathan, S., Shafir, E., & Zhao, J. (2013). Poverty impedes cognitive function. Science, 341(6149), 976–980.

Mani and colleagues ran two studies: one where they experimentally induced financial worry in shoppers and measured immediate cognitive performance on unrelated tasks, and one comparing the same Indian sugarcane farmers' cognitive performance before harvest (when poor) versus after harvest (when comparatively wealthy). Both found that financial scarcity itself, not just being poor as a fixed trait, measurably reduced cognitive performance on the spot — the same person performed worse under financial worry than under financial ease. This is the foundational finding behind the project's entire premise. (Important, honest caveat: a formal published comment on this paper by Wicherts & Scholten, 2013, raised statistical concerns about how income was categorized in the original analysis — the project's own write-up should note this debate rather than presenting the finding as fully settled.)

### 2. Mullainathan, S., & Shafir, E. (2013). Scarcity: Why Having Too Little Means So Much. Times Books/Henry Holt.

This book (by two of the same authors as the Science paper above) extends the "scarcity mindset" concept into a general theory: scarcity of any resource — money, time, even social connection — captures attention and consumes limited mental "bandwidth," leaving less cognitive capacity for everything else, including decisions unrelated to the scarce resource itself. It's useful as the accessible, broader statement of the theory the project is built around, going beyond the single 2013 Science paper's specific experiments.

### 3. Shah, A. K., Mullainathan, S., & Shafir, E. (2012). Some consequences of having too little. Science, 338(6107), 682–685.

This companion study found that scarcity causes people to focus intensely on the scarce resource itself (a kind of productive "tunneling") while neglecting other, sometimes more important considerations — explaining specifically how scarcity can lead to both unusually sharp focus on urgent problems and costly neglect of other decisions at the same time. It adds nuance the project should include: scarcity isn't simply "less thinking," it's a reallocation of attention that has both an upside and a downside.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This is one of the more emotionally serious topics in the field, and the portfolio's power comes from how carefully and consistently it's handled at every stage — that consistency itself is what should impress a reader. The Unit I simulation is designed to responsibly demonstrate the mechanism without exploiting real hardship. Unit II turns it into a real, ethically-designed 2x2 study. Unit III takes those results to a public audience with real care not to stigmatize poverty as a mindset failure — the same careful framing established in Unit I is still visible in Unit III's messaging. Unit IV visualizes the bandwidth mechanism the Unit II study measured. Unit V's policy ask — simplifying paperwork for assistance programs — is a genuinely actionable, low-cost intervention directly supported by the project's own evidence chain. A mentor or reviewer sees not just good science, but consistent ethical judgment sustained across six units — which is exactly the kind of maturity research institutions and social-impact organizations are actively looking for.
