# 10. Neuroeconomics of Fairness

*The Ultimatum Game and Perceived Injustice*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Economics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `economics/10-neuroeconomics-of-fairness`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Why do people reject unfair-but-positive offers in the Ultimatum Game, and what does the underlying neural response to perceived unfairness (insula activation) suggest about designing fairer economic institutions?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
The Ultimatum Game demonstrates that humans routinely sacrifice material gain to punish unfairness — a finding with direct implications for designing compensation systems, tax policy communication, and institutional trust, and one of the clearest bridges between brain-imaging findings (Sanfey et al.) and real economic behavior.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Sanfey, A. G., Rilling, J. K., Aronson, J. A., Nystrom, L. E., & Cohen, J. D. (2003). The neural basis of economic decision-making in the Ultimatum Game. Science, 300(5626), 1755–1758.

This is the founding neuroeconomics study of the Ultimatum Game. Using brain imaging, Sanfey and colleagues found that unfair offers activated both the anterior insula (associated with negative emotion and disgust) and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (associated with cognitive control), and that stronger insula activation predicted a greater likelihood of rejecting the unfair offer — direct neural evidence that emotional response to unfairness, not pure financial self-interest, drives the well-documented tendency to reject unequal splits even at a personal cost. This is the paper the project's entire premise is built on.

### 2. Güth, W., Schmittberger, R., & Schwarze, B. (1982). An experimental analysis of ultimatum bargaining. Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 3(4), 367–388.

This is the original behavioral (pre-neuroimaging) paper that introduced the Ultimatum Game itself, showing that real participants frequently rejected unequal splits, directly contradicting the standard economic prediction that a purely self-interested responder should accept any positive offer. This paper is useful because it establishes that the fairness-driven rejection phenomenon was a robust, well-replicated behavioral finding for over two decades before Sanfey et al. investigated its neural basis — the project should understand both layers, not just the imaging study.

### 3. Henrich, J., Boyd, R., Bowles, S., et al. (2001). In search of Homo economicus: Behavioral experiments in 15 small-scale societies. American Economic Review, 91(2), 73–78.

This large cross-cultural study ran Ultimatum Game experiments across 15 different small-scale societies around the world and found substantial variation in typical offers and rejection rates between cultures — some societies showed much higher or lower fairness standards than the typical Western university-sample results. This is an important, honest addition for the project: it shows that while a fairness-sensitivity mechanism appears to be widespread in humans, exactly how "fair" is defined and enforced is shaped significantly by culture, not fixed and universal.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This project's throughline is a single, elegant question — will you turn down free money to punish someone who was unfair to you? — answered with increasing depth at every stage. Unit I's classroom Ultimatum Game generates real rejection-rate data from real peers. Unit II turns that into a properly designed repeated-measures study across a full range of unfair splits. Unit III explains the same real data to an audience that immediately recognizes the feeling of being cheated. Unit IV's rejection-probability model is fit directly to the numbers the project's own game produced. Unit V takes the exact same fairness-sensitivity finding and applies it somewhere concretely useful — designing fairer profit-sharing splits for a real school fundraiser. A mentor sees a student who took one of neuroeconomics' most famous paradigms, ran it themselves instead of just reading about it, and then found a genuinely practical use for what they found — that combination of original data collection and real application is rare and memorable.
