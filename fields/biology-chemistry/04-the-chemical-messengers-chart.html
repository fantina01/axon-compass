4. The Chemical Messengers Chart#

Mapping Hormones and Neurotransmitters Like a Periodic Table

Field: Neuroscience × Biology & Chemistry  ·  Project ID: biology-chemistry/04-the-chemical-messengers-chart

Big Question

Can organizing the brain and body’s major chemical messengers (like a simplified reference chart, similar to how the periodic table organizes elements) make an overwhelming topic easy to actually use and remember?

Why This Matters

Students often learn about neurotransmitters and hormones as an unconnected list of names to memorize; organizing them by function and chemical type (the same organizing instinct behind the periodic table) turns memorization into genuine understanding.

Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II#

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they’re what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

1. Nestler, E. J., Hyman, S. E., & Malenka, R. C. (2009). Molecular Neuropharmacology: A Foundation for Clinical Neuroscience (2nd ed.). McGraw-Hill.#

This standard neuropharmacology textbook organizes neurotransmitters and hormones by their chemical class (such as amine, amino acid, or peptide-based) and by their functional effects, providing the organizing framework the project’s reference chart is directly modeled on. It’s the authoritative source for checking that the project’s categorization choices are scientifically accurate.

2. Mayer, E. A. (2011). Gut feelings: The emerging biology of gut-brain communication. Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 12(8), 453-466.#

Mayer’s review discusses how chemical messengers like serotonin, though often associated purely with mood and the brain, are also produced and act extensively outside the brain (much of the body’s serotonin is actually in the digestive system) — a genuinely surprising fact useful for the project’s chart, since it complicates the common assumption that each messenger has one single, simple job.

3. Sapolsky, R. M. (2004). Why Zebras Don’t Get Ulcers (3rd ed.). Holt Paperbacks.#

Sapolsky’s accessible synthesis explains how stress-related hormones like cortisol and adrenaline interact with the body’s broader physiology, illustrating why organizing messengers only by chemical structure (and not also by their interacting functional systems) can miss important relationships between them. This is a useful, readable companion source for building out the “function” dimension of the project’s reference chart, alongside the more technical chemical-classification textbook above.

Exact Steps, Unit by Unit#

Every step below plugs into the fixed season process described in The Universal Process — tournaments and laboratories run identically regardless of field. Only the content below is specific to this project.

Unit I — Learning Innovation Challenge#

Weeks 1–4 · Required artifact: Innovation Proposal I

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Run the Week 2 Confidence Gap tournament (see the Universal Process chapter).

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] Students design a “Chemical Messenger Reference Chart” — organizing major neurotransmitters and hormones (like dopamine, serotonin, cortisol, adrenaline) by basic chemical category (e.g., amino-acid-derived vs. steroid-based) and by general function (mood, stress, alertness). Innovation Proposal I documents the chart design and category choices.

Unit II — Research Investigation#

Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: Research Investigation / Prospectus

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 6 Prediction Ladder tournament + Week 7 Build-a-System laboratory.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] Literature review: basic biochemistry of major neurotransmitters and hormones, organized around their chemical building blocks and general effects.

  • [ ] Hypothesis: peers using the organized reference chart will correctly recall more messenger-function pairings on a follow-up quiz than peers given the same information as an unorganized list.

  • [ ] Variables: chart format (organized vs. unorganized list) as the thing being compared; quiz recall accuracy as the outcome.

  • [ ] Methodology: two peer groups study the same information in different formats, then take an identical recall quiz, and scores are compared.

Unit III — Public Communication & Social Impact#

Weeks 9–12 · Required artifact: Communication Product + Dissemination Plan

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 10 Signal and the Noise tournament + Week 11 Message Transmission laboratory.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] A short video, “A ‘Periodic Table’ for Your Brain Chemicals,” using the same chart; dissemination through school biology and chemistry classes.

Unit IV — Memory/Decision Systems Simulation#

Weeks 13–16 · Required artifact: Simulation Model IV

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 14 Distortion Chain tournament + Week 15 Distortion & Reconstruction laboratory.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] Simulates an interactive version of the chart where users click a messenger and see its chemical category, main functions, and related messengers highlighted; documented on GitHub.

Unit V — Policy Formation Simulation#

Weeks 17–20 · Required artifact: Policy Draft Document

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 18 Self-Forecast Duel tournament + Week 19 Identity & System Construction laboratory.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] Policy proposal recommending biology and chemistry curricula adopt this organized chart format as the standard teaching tool for neurotransmitters and hormones, aimed at science curriculum coordinators.

Unit VI — Integration & Summit#

Weeks 21–24 · Required artifact: Capstone Project

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 22 Capstone Prototype Development laboratory, leading into the Week 24 Summit.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] Capstone integrates the chart, the recall-comparison study, the video, the interactive tool, and the curriculum proposal into one “chemical messengers” portfolio.

Skills This Project Builds#

  • Organizing complex biochemical information using a clear classification system.

  • Designing a controlled recall-comparison experiment (organized vs. unorganized study materials).

  • Building an interactive digital reference tool.

  • Explaining chemical classification systems to a general audience.

  • Proposing and justifying a curriculum-adoption recommendation to a science department.

Why This Project Impresses#

Demonstrates genuine biochemistry organization skills and a real, tested teaching-tool improvement — practical and immediately reusable by other students.

How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio#

Every unit builds the same organizing idea: neurotransmitters and hormones aren’t a random memorization list, they’re a structured chemistry system. Unit I designs the first categorized chart. Unit II tests whether organization itself improves recall compared to an unorganized list. Unit III explains that same organizing principle to biology and chemistry classes drowning in vocabulary. Unit IV turns the chart into an interactive reference tool. Unit V asks it become the standard teaching tool for this unit district-wide. A chemistry or biology teacher sees a genuinely useful study tool, built and tested by a student, solving a problem every student in the subject actually has.

Maximize Your Impact — What To Do With This After the Season#

  • Propose the reference chart as the standard teaching tool for your school’s neurotransmitter/hormone unit.

  • Submit the recall-comparison study to a science fair under biochemistry or science education.

  • Publish the interactive chart tool publicly so students at other schools can use it to study.
