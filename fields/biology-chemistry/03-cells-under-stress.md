# 3. Cells Under Stress

*Comparing Neuron Health Under Simulated Conditions*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Biology & Chemistry &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `biology-chemistry/03-cells-under-stress`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Using basic cell biology (what cells need to survive: oxygen, nutrients, stable environment), can students explain why neurons are especially vulnerable to things like poor sleep or chronic stress?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Neurons have unusually high energy demands and limited ability to regenerate compared to many other cell types, making them a clear, teachable example of how basic cell-biology principles (energy needs, cell structure, waste removal) connect directly to real vulnerability and long-term brain health.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Attwell, D., & Laughlin, S. B. (2001). An energy budget for signaling in the grey matter of the brain. Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow and Metabolism, 21(10), 1133-1145.

(Verified in the Physics field's "Thermodynamics of Thinking" project.) Attwell and Laughlin's detailed energy budget shows neurons consume disproportionately large amounts of energy maintaining their signaling capability — direct evidence for this project's claim that neurons have unusually high energy demands compared to many other cell types.

### 2. Harris, J. J., Jolivet, R., & Attwell, D. (2012). Synaptic energy use and supply. Neuron, 75(5), 762-777.

This review specifically examines how neurons' energy needs are supplied by nearby blood vessels and support cells, and what happens when that energy supply is disrupted even briefly — since, unlike many other cell types, neurons have very limited ability to store energy reserves locally. This is directly relevant to the project's core claim: neurons' high energy demand combined with limited local energy storage makes them unusually vulnerable to disruptions in oxygen or nutrient delivery.

### 3. Rink, C., & Khanna, S. (2011). Significance of brain tissue oxygenation and the arachidonic acid cascade in stroke. Antioxidants & Redox Signaling, 14(10), 1889-1903.

This review discusses how quickly neural tissue is damaged when oxygen delivery is interrupted, compared to the much greater resilience of many other tissue types to similar oxygen shortages. It's included as supporting evidence for the project's comparison between neurons and more resilient cell types, grounded in real, clinically-relevant physiology.

### Unit II — Research Investigation
*Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: **Research Investigation / Prospectus***

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Hypothesis:** peers who receive the explainer kit will more accurately answer a follow-up quiz about why sleep and stress affect the brain specifically, compared to a group that only receives general "sleep is good for you" messaging.

- [ ] **Methodology:** two peer groups receive different messaging, then both take the same follow-up quiz, and scores are compared.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This project's throughline is a single comparison — a neuron against a more resilient cell — made sharper at every stage. Unit I builds the first comparison chart. Unit II tests whether teaching that comparison actually improves peer understanding of why sleep and stress hit the brain specifically. Unit III explains the same comparison to health classes tired of generic "sleep is good" messaging. Unit IV visualizes exactly why neurons' energy and resource needs make them uniquely vulnerable. Unit V asks biology curricula to adopt this exact comparison permanently. A biology mentor sees real cell-biology fundamentals used to make a genuinely important health message land harder than generic advice ever could.
