# 2. Average You

*Using Mean, Median, and Range to Understand Reaction Time*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Mathematics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `mathematics/02-average-you`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can basic averages (mean, median, range) reveal real patterns in how fast people react — and whether things like sleep or phone use change it?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Reaction time is easy to measure (a falling ruler, a stopwatch, a simple online test) and gives a genuine window into brain processing speed — and averages are exactly the right tool, no fancier math needed, to find real patterns in the data.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Killgore, W. D. S. (2010). Effects of sleep deprivation on cognition. Progress in Brain Research, 185, 105-129.

(Verified in the Policy field's "Sleep Deprivation and Diplomatic Judgment" project.) Sleep-deprived individuals show measurably impaired reaction time and reduced ability to adapt decisions as conditions change — the direct evidence behind this project's hypothesis that less-slept peers will show slower, more variable reaction times than better-rested peers.

### 2. Van Dongen, H. P. A., Maislin, G., Mullington, J. M., & Dinges, D. F. (2003). The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness: Dose-response effects on neurobehavioral functions and sleep physiology from chronic sleep restriction and total sleep deprivation. Sleep, 26(2), 117-126.

This carefully controlled study measured reaction time and cognitive performance across two weeks of restricted sleep, finding that performance decline builds up cumulatively night after night — meaning even moderate, sustained sleep restriction (not just one all-nighter) can produce impairment comparable to much more extreme short-term deprivation. This matters for the project because it justifies comparing typical school-night sleep variation, not just extreme sleep-deprivation cases.

### 3. Lim, J., & Dinges, D. F. (2010). A meta-analysis of the impact of short-term sleep deprivation on cognitive variables. Psychological Bulletin, 136(3), 375-389.

This meta-analysis pooled results across many sleep-deprivation studies and confirmed that simple reaction time is one of the cognitive measures most reliably and consistently affected by sleep loss, more so than some more complex cognitive tasks. This is directly useful for the project's specific choice of reaction time as the outcome measure — it's one of the most sensitive, well-validated indicators available for this kind of comparison.

### Unit II — Research Investigation
*Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: **Research Investigation / Prospectus***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 6 **Prediction Ladder** tournament + Week 7 **Build-a-System** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Literature review:** basic facts about reaction time and the nervous system, in plain language (nerve signals take time to travel, tiredness slows reactions).
- [ ] **Hypothesis:** people who report less sleep the night before will have a slower average reaction time than people who report more sleep.
- [ ] **Variables:** hours of sleep reported as one group; reaction time (mean, median, and range) as the outcome.
- [ ] **Methodology:** collect reaction times from a group of peers along with their reported sleep hours, then calculate and compare the mean, median, and range for the "more sleep" group versus the "less sleep" group.

## Why This Project Impresses

Shows real, hands-on use of the exact statistics vocabulary (mean, median, range) every student already learns, applied to a genuinely interesting question about sleep and the brain.

---

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

Every unit of this project makes the same three numbers — mean, median, range — mean something real and personal. Unit I collects real reaction-time data from real people. Unit II turns it into an actual sleep-versus-reaction-time comparison, calculated properly across a real group. Unit III explains that comparison to an audience who's never thought of their own tiredness as something with an actual measurable number attached. Unit IV builds a live calculator so any student can find their own mean, median, and range instantly. Unit V asks the school to teach statistics through this exact hands-on, personally relevant example instead of an abstract textbook dataset. A math or health teacher sees required statistics vocabulary turned into a genuinely interesting personal discovery — proof that basic stats, applied to a real question, is more convincing than any textbook example could be.
