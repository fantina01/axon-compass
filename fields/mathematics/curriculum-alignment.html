AXON REVIEW — FULL CURRICULUM ALIGNMENT#

Neuroscience × Mathematics — All Ten Projects, All Twenty-Four Weeks#

HOW THIS DOCUMENT WORKS#

The Operational Handbook already defines how every week runs — the reading-analysis pattern, the five tournament formats (Confidence Gap, Prediction Ladder, Signal and the Noise, Distortion Chain, Self-Forecast Duel), the five laboratory formats (Build-a-System Simulation, Message Transmission Simulation, Distortion & Reconstruction Experiment, Identity & System Construction Workshop, Capstone Prototype Development), and the fixed 75-minute session shapes. None of that is repeated here.

What this document provides is the piece that was missing: exactly what content each of Mathematics’s ten projects plugs into that fixed structure, week by week, for all 24 weeks. A chapter leader running “Percent Better” should never have to guess what their Week 10 tournament station is about, or what their Week 15 lab’s manipulated stimulus should be — it’s specified here, tied directly to the calculations, datasets, and literature named in each project file, and specified so that Week 4’s Innovation Proposal, Week 8’s Research Investigation, Week 12’s communication product, Week 16’s simulation, and Week 20’s policy draft all visibly build on each other, exactly the way the Handbook’s own unit-to-unit logic requires.

Every entry below names the correct universal format from the Handbook and then gives the project-specific content for that slot — nothing here contradicts or replaces the Handbook’s mechanics. Because Mathematics is the field built explicitly on the promise that “basic math, applied carefully, is enough,” every Week 8 and Week 16 entry below states the exact calculation being performed — a percentage, a mean and median, a ratio, a line graph, a counted probability, a pie-chart angle, a best-fit line’s slope — and what real number it produced, the same standard of concreteness the Economics and Physics alignments held throughout.

PROJECT 1 — Percent Better#

Measuring how much study techniques actually help

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Roediger and Karpicke’s 2006 testing-effect finding — students who quizzed themselves outperformed students who reread, on a test given a week later. Pre-reading: participants guess, as a percent, how much better they think quizzing performs than rereading, before any real number is given. Research Summary I must state the testing effect in plain language and name one everyday habit (flashcard apps, practice tests) that already relies on it.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station has participants memorize a short word list using flashcards, predict their own quiz score before taking it, then take the real quiz. Log every predicted-vs-real percent gap; Week 4’s proposal cites the average gap directly.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each with a new short word list, alternating flashcard study and re-reading study, recording the real percent scored at every trial and tracking whether the flashcard score climbs faster across trials.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Study Method Percent Improvement Test — peers memorize one list with flashcards and a second list by rereading, then get quizzed on both, with percent improvement calculated as score-after ÷ score-before, turned into a percent. The proposal documents both lists, the test plan, and the exact percent-improvement formula, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and the Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Karpicke and Roediger’s 2008 follow-up isolating retrieval practice from mere re-exposure, and Dunlosky et al.’s 2013 review ranking practice testing “high utility” and rereading “low utility.” Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: the flashcard/quiz method will show a higher percent improvement in recall than the re-reading method.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real published study-technique comparisons with escalating effect sizes; participants predict which technique pairing shows the largest percent gap.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a raw before/after quiz-score pair. Reception receives only the “before” score; Filtering decides which study method it belongs to; Integration adds the “after” score; Reconstruction rebuilds the percent-improvement calculation from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed percent still matches the real one.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): each peer tries both methods on different word lists, and the class calculates and averages each person’s percent score for each method. The poster’s central chart is a side-by-side bar comparison of average percent improvement for flashcards versus rereading, stating the real percentage-point gap and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to explain a percentage finding to students who assume “rereading your notes is studying.”

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 finding delivered two ways — as a raw percent-improvement statistic versus “we tested study hacks with math — here’s the winner” — rated for which framing students say they’d actually try.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 percentage gap, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific number survives retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “We Tested Study Hacks With Math — Here’s the Winner,” using the real Week 8 percentages, disseminated through school morning announcements, study-skills workshops, and student social media.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a student might remember “flashcards were way better” without recalling the real percentage-point gap.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 percent-improvement figure once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific number drifts.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real percent-improvement result; initial recall is collected; the manipulation alters the stated percentage; final recall is collected; comparison shows the false number’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive percent-improvement calculator — a user enters any “before” and “after” quiz score for any study method and instantly sees their own percent improvement, plus how averaging many people’s percentages gives a more trustworthy answer than one person’s result. Calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset. Documented on GitHub with a worked before/after example.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how a school’s study-skills week is usually built on advice, not measurement.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether student government will adopt a percent-improvement challenge during study-skills week, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the school’s study-skills week programming to find the realistic point where a percent-improvement challenge could be inserted.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that study-skills week include a percent-improvement challenge so students test their own methods with real numbers, citing the real Week 8 data and Week 16 calculator, aimed at teachers, school counselors, and student government.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 percent guess through a real averaged percentage gap to a study-skills-week proposal.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the percent-improvement calculator as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the two-list comparison, the real percent-improvement investigation, the video, the calculator, and the study-week proposal into one draft, honestly naming the fairness limitations — different word lists, a small sample size.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition shows both word-list results side by side with their real percentages; reflection defense states the exact percent-improvement figure found and what it can and can’t prove.

PROJECT 2 — Average You#

Using mean, median, and range to understand reaction time

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Killgore’s 2010 review that sleep deprivation measurably impairs reaction time. Pre-reading: participants guess, in milliseconds, how much slower they think a tired person reacts compared to a well-rested one, before any reference figure is given.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station is a falling-ruler or online click reaction-time test; participants predict their own reaction time before taking it. Log every predicted-vs-real gap.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials of the same reaction-time test per participant, recording the time at every trial and tracking whether repeated trials get faster or more consistent.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Reaction Time Data Collector — peers do a reaction-time test multiple times and results are recorded. The proposal documents the test method and a first small dataset, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial-to-trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Van Dongen et al.’s 2003 finding that reaction-time decline builds up cumulatively across nights of restricted sleep, and Lim and Dinges’s 2010 meta-analysis confirming reaction time as one of the measures most reliably affected by sleep loss. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: people who report less sleep the night before will have a slower average reaction time than people who report more sleep.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real reaction-time datasets with different sample sleep-hour groupings; participants predict which group shows the fastest mean reaction time.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a raw reaction-time value with a reported sleep-hours figure attached. Reception receives only the reaction time; Filtering decides whether it looks fast or slow; Integration adds the sleep-hours figure; Reconstruction rebuilds an expected group average from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed average still matches the real data.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): collect reaction times from a group of peers along with their reported sleep hours, then calculate and compare the mean, median, and range for the “more sleep” group versus the “less sleep” group. The poster’s central chart states all three statistics for both groups side by side, citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to explain mean, median, and range to an audience that has never connected these required vocabulary words to a personal number.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 finding delivered two ways — as a raw mean/median/range statistic versus “your reaction time says how tired you really are” — rated for relatability.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 comparison, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific mean and median values survive retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Your Reaction Time Says How Tired You Really Are,” using the real Week 8 statistics, disseminated through school health class and sports teams.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a participant might remember their own reaction time as faster than the real recorded number.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state a real recorded reaction time once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific millisecond value drifts.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is a real reaction-time trial; initial recall is collected; the manipulation alters the stated time; final recall is collected; comparison shows the false number’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive mean/median/range calculator — a user enters a new set of reaction-time numbers and instantly sees the mean, median, and range calculated and explained step by step, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset. Documented on GitHub.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how statistics is usually taught with an abstract textbook dataset instead of a personally relevant one.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether health and math teachers will adopt real reaction-time testing in class, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the school’s health and math course sequences to find the realistic point for a joint reaction-time lesson.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that health class use real reaction-time testing, instead of only a textbook lesson, to teach both statistics and healthy sleep habits together, citing the real Week 8 data and Week 16 calculator, aimed at health and math teachers.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 millisecond guess through a real mean/median/range comparison to a joint health/math-lesson proposal.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the mean/median/range calculator as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the data collector, the real sleep-comparison investigation, the video, the calculator, and the curriculum proposal into one “reaction time and averages” draft.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition displays the real mean, median, and range for both sleep groups; reflection defense states honestly what a peer-collected, self-reported dataset can and can’t prove.

PROJECT 3 — Brain-to-Body Ratio#

What simple ratios reveal about intelligence myths

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Roth and Dicke’s 2005 finding that brain-to-body ratio predicts intelligence worse than more detailed factors like cortical neuron count. Pre-reading: participants guess whether an elephant, a human, or a whale has the highest brain-to-body ratio, before any published weights are shown.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station shows a pair of animals’ brain and body weights; participants predict which has the higher ratio before calculating it.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each with a new pair of animals, calculating brain-to-body ratio by hand and comparing the predicted-versus-calculated ranking each time.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Brain-to-Body Ratio Comparison Chart using published brain-weight and body-weight data for several animals (including humans), calculating the ratio for each. The proposal documents the chosen animals and the ratio calculations, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Jerison’s 1973 encephalization quotient and Herculano-Houzel’s 2009 finding that the human brain follows the same primate scaling rules, simply with the most cortical neurons of any primate. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: animals commonly considered “smart” (humans, dolphins, crows) will show a noticeably higher brain-to-body ratio than animals with larger overall brains but not typically considered as cognitively advanced (elephants, whales).

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real published animal brain-and-body weight pairs; participants predict which ranks highest by raw brain size and which ranks highest by ratio, previewing that the two rankings won’t match.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one animal’s raw brain weight. Reception receives only that number; Filtering decides whether it “looks big”; Integration adds the body-weight figure; Reconstruction rebuilds the calculated ratio from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed ratio still matches the real calculation.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): look up published weights, calculate each ratio, and rank animals by ratio versus by raw brain size to show the two rankings don’t match. The poster’s central chart is a two-column ranking table — by raw size and by ratio — stating the real calculated ratios and citing the Week 6 tournament’s prediction pattern.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to correct a popular myth (“bigger brain = smarter”) without oversimplifying the real, more nuanced science.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 ranking-mismatch finding delivered two ways — as a raw ratio statistic versus “bigger brain doesn’t mean smarter — here’s the math” — rated for relatability.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 ranking mismatch, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific ratio numbers survive retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Bigger Brain Doesn’t Mean Smarter — Here’s the Math,” using the real Week 8 ranking chart, disseminated through school biology and math classes and STEM outreach for younger students.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “elephants have the highest ratio” despite the real calculated numbers showing otherwise.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 ranking once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific ranking reorders or degrades.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real ranking chart; initial recall is collected; the manipulation swaps two animals’ rankings; final recall is collected; comparison shows the swap’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive ratio calculator — a user enters any animal’s brain weight and body weight and instantly sees the ratio and how it compares to other animals, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset. Documented on GitHub.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how proportional reasoning, a required math topic, is usually taught with abstract numbers instead of a real biology myth.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether biology and math teachers will adopt the ratio comparison as a joint lesson, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the school’s math and biology course sequences to find the realistic point for a joint proportional-reasoning lesson.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that biology and math teachers use this ratio comparison as a joint lesson connecting proportional reasoning to a real biology myth-busting example, citing the real Week 8 ranking data and Week 16 calculator.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 guess through a real ranking-mismatch calculation to a joint-lesson proposal.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the ratio calculator as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the comparison chart, the real ranking-mismatch investigation, the explainer, the calculator, and the curriculum proposal into one “brain-to-body ratio” draft.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition displays the real two-column ranking table; reflection defense states honestly, per Roth and Dicke, that even the ratio itself isn’t the whole story.

PROJECT 4 — Graphing Forgetting#

Drawing the forgetting curve with a simple line graph

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Ebbinghaus’s original 1885 finding that forgetting drops sharply in the first hours after learning, then levels off. Pre-reading: participants sketch, from memory, what they expect their own forgetting curve to look like across a week, before collecting any real data.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station has participants memorize a short fact list, predict what percent they’ll remember one day later, then get quizzed the following session. Log every predicted-vs-real percent gap.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials with new short fact lists, memorizing and immediately re-quizzing, tracking the percent-remembered pattern across all three lists.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Forgetting Curve Tracker — peers memorize a short list of facts, then get re-tested after 1 day, 3 days, and 7 days, recording percent remembered each time. The proposal documents the fact list and the testing schedule, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Murre and Dros’s 2015 modern replication of Ebbinghaus’s original curve shape, and Rubin and Wenzel’s 1996 review that no single equation fits every real forgetting dataset. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: percent remembered will drop quickly at first (day 0 to day 1) and then more slowly afterward, rather than declining at a steady, even rate.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real re-test percentages at different day intervals; participants predict which interval shows the steepest single-day drop.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one real re-test percentage. Reception receives only the day-1 value; Filtering decides whether it looks like a steep or shallow drop; Integration adds the day-3 and day-7 values; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected curve shape from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed curve still looks like a real forgetting curve.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): plot each peer’s percent-remembered scores at each time point on a line graph and examine the shape of the curve. The poster’s central chart is the real multi-point line graph, stating the real percent-remembered values at day 1, day 3, and day 7, and citing the Week 6 tournament’s steepest-drop prediction.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to present a personal graph to an audience who has never seen their own forgetting curve before.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 curve delivered two ways — as a raw percent-vs-day statistic versus “I graphed my own forgetting — here’s what it looks like” — rated for relatability.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 curve, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific shape (steep-then-shallow) survives retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “I Graphed My Own Forgetting — Here’s What It Looks Like,” using the real Week 8 curve, disseminated through school study-skills workshops and social media.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a viewer might remember the curve as a straight line rather than the real steep-then-shallow shape.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 day-1 percentage once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific number drifts.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real forgetting curve; initial recall is collected; the manipulation flattens the reported curve shape; final recall is collected; comparison shows the flattening’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive personal-curve tool — a user enters their own re-test percentages and sees their personal forgetting curve drawn automatically, plus a “review reminder” suggesting re-study right around the steepest drop-off point, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset. Documented on GitHub.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how teachers typically schedule review only right before a test, not based on the real shape of the forgetting curve.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether teachers will adopt spaced review scheduling, opened in Week 19.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 finding, including its caveat, once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how easily the caveat drops out and the finding becomes an oversimplified rule.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real rating comparison with its caveat; initial recall is collected; the manipulation strips the caveat from the restated finding; final recall is collected; comparison shows the caveat’s fragility, followed by full debrief connecting to responsible science communication generally.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive symmetry-measuring and rating tool — a user adjusts an outline’s symmetry with a slider and sees how the measured symmetry score changes, connecting directly to the real Week 8 data. Documented on GitHub, using only abstract shapes throughout.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how a sensitive-topic classroom activity needs explicit ethical guardrails to avoid encouraging appearance-based judgment of real people.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether teachers considering the activity will require it to stay abstract-shapes-only, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map how the activity could realistically be adopted in an art or psychology class while identifying every point where it could drift toward judging real people’s appearance.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that this project be framed carefully in any future use — to avoid encouraging appearance-based judgment of real people, using it purely as a geometry-and-perception lesson with abstract shapes, citing the real Week 8 findings and Week 16 tool, aimed at teachers considering the activity.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 abstract-outline prediction through a real, carefully-caveated finding to an ethical-use policy recommendation.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the symmetry tool as capstone centerpiece, keeping it strictly abstract-shapes-only; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer, checking specifically that the tool cannot be repurposed for real photos.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the toolkit, the real ratings investigation, the explainer, the symmetry tool, and the careful-use policy recommendation into one “symmetry and perception” draft, explicitly addressing the ethical care taken throughout.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition displays only abstract outlines, never real photos; reflection defense states the real rating-versus-symmetry numbers found and leads with the Rhodes et al. caveat rather than burying it.

CROSS-PROJECT NOTE#

Every project above runs the same 24-week skeleton from the Operational Handbook — five tournaments, five labs, five main outputs building toward one capstone. Every one of Mathematics’s ten projects was built to the same depth throughout: a real, plainly-stated calculation (a percent improvement, a mean/median/range, a brain-to-body ratio, a multi-point line graph, a counted probability, a chunked-recall count, a scatter-graph trend, a percent-to-angle conversion, a best-fit line’s slope, a measured symmetry gap), a real number the calculation actually produced from the chapter’s own peer data, and an explicit statement of which earlier week’s evidence each later step depends on, from Week 1 through Week 24.
