# 5. The Odds of Guessing

*Probability and Multiple-Choice Test Strategy*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Mathematics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `mathematics/05-the-odds-of-guessing`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can basic probability (counting outcomes, simple odds) show students exactly when guessing on a multiple-choice test is worth it — and connect that to how the brain handles uncertainty?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Students guess on tests constantly but rarely think about the actual odds involved; connecting simple probability (like "1 in 4 chance" for a 4-option question) to real test strategy — and to how the brain naturally struggles with judging uncertain situations — makes an abstract math topic immediately useful.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124-1131.

(Verified in the Economics field's "Anchoring in the Aisles" project.) This foundational paper established that people are often poor, biased judges of probability, relying on mental shortcuts rather than genuine statistical reasoning — directly relevant to this project's finding that peers' guessed odds of a correct answer rarely match the real, simply-calculated probability.

### 2. Lichtenstein, S., Fischhoff, B., & Phillips, L. D. (1982). Calibration of probabilities: The state of the art to 1980. In Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases. Cambridge University Press.

This review specifically examined how well people's stated confidence in their answers matches their actual accuracy, finding a consistent pattern of overconfidence — people are typically more certain than their real accuracy justifies, especially on harder questions. This is directly useful for the project's comparison between peers' guessed odds and the real, calculated probability, since overconfidence is one specific, well-documented reason those guesses tend to be wrong in a predictable direction.

### 3. Windschitl, P. D., & Wells, G. L. (1998). The alternative-outcomes effect. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 75(6), 1411-1423.

This study found that people's probability judgments are influenced by how many alternative outcomes they can easily imagine, not just the true underlying odds — meaning a test format that makes wrong answers feel more or less "available" to imagine can shift someone's sense of their own odds, separate from the real, calculated probability. This adds a useful nuance to the project: guessing-odds misjudgment isn't random error, it follows identifiable psychological patterns worth discussing explicitly.

## Why This Project Impresses

Uses only basic counting-based probability (no formulas beyond "1 out of however many options") to reveal a genuinely useful, surprising truth about human judgment.

---

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

Every unit of this project sharpens the same gap: what people think their odds are, versus what the odds actually are. Unit I calculates the real odds for different guessing formats. Unit II turns it into a genuine comparison between peers' guessed odds and the real, calculated numbers. Unit III explains that gap to an audience who guesses on tests constantly without ever calculating the real odds. Unit IV builds a tool showing the real math step by step for any format. Unit V asks test-prep programs to teach this exact lesson. A math teacher sees a student who used nothing more advanced than "1 divided by however many options" to reveal a genuine, well-documented pattern in how people misjudge probability — proof that basic division, applied carefully, can produce a real and useful insight.
