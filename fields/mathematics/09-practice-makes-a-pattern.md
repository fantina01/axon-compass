# 9. Practice Makes a Pattern

*Simple Equations for Predicting Skill Improvement*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Mathematics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `mathematics/09-practice-makes-a-pattern`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can a simple equation (the same kind used for "y = mx + b" in algebra class) predict how much practice time it takes to reach a skill goal, based on real practice-and-improvement data?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
"Practice makes perfect" is vague; turning real practice-time-and-improvement data into a simple straight-line equation gives students a concrete, useful prediction tool — and a genuinely satisfying real-world use for basic algebra.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Newell, A., & Rosenbloom, P. S. (1981). Mechanisms of skill acquisition and the law of practice. In Cognitive Skills and Their Acquisition. Lawrence Erlbaum Associates.

Newell and Rosenbloom analyzed practice-and-performance data across many different skills and tasks, and found a remarkably consistent mathematical pattern: performance improves quickly at first and then more slowly, following what they termed the "power law of practice." This is directly relevant background for the project, since a power-law relationship (rather than a perfectly straight line) is technically the more precise mathematical description of most real skill-practice data — the project's straight-line approximation is a reasonable, accessible simplification of this more complex, well-established underlying pattern, worth mentioning honestly.

### 2. Ericsson, K. A., Krampe, R. T., & Tesch-Römer, C. (1993). The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance. Psychological Review, 100(3), 363-406.

Ericsson and colleagues studied expert musicians and found that the specific quality of practice — focused, effortful "deliberate practice" rather than just accumulated time — was a strong predictor of skill level, and that total accumulated practice hours followed a fairly predictable relationship with measured skill. This is useful context for the project's own practice-tracking data: not all practice minutes are equal, and the straight-line relationship the project measures may be specific to how consistently focused the practice sessions actually were.

### 3. Heathcote, A., Brown, S., & Mewhort, D. J. K. (2000). The power law repealed: The case for an exponential law of practice. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 7(2), 185-207.

This paper challenges the Newell and Rosenbloom power-law finding directly, arguing that much of the apparent power-law shape in classic practice data was actually a statistical artifact of averaging across many individuals with different learning rates, and that an exponential curve fits individual-level data better. This is a valuable, honest addition: even the "power law of practice" is an actively contested finding in the research literature, which the project should acknowledge rather than presenting any single practice-curve shape as fully settled science.

## Why This Project Impresses

Applies the single most common algebra skill (straight-line equations) to real, personally-collected data — a genuinely satisfying, concrete use of a basic math tool.

---

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

Every unit of this project builds toward the same straight-line equation, made more precise and more personally meaningful at each stage. Unit I collects the first real practice-and-score data. Unit II turns it into an actual best-fit line with a real equation. Unit III explains that exact personal equation to an audience who's heard "practice makes perfect" a thousand times without ever seeing it proven with their own numbers. Unit IV builds a tool that draws the best-fit line and predicts future scores from any student's own data. Unit V asks math teachers to use this exact hands-on activity instead of only abstract textbook line-equation examples. A math teacher sees the single most common algebra skill — y equals mx plus b — turned into something a student built, tested, and genuinely used to predict their own future performance.
