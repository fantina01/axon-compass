# 9. Neuromyths in the Staff Room

*Auditing and Correcting Popular Educational Neuromyths*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Education &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `education/09-neuromyths-in-the-staff-room`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
How prevalent are neuroscience myths (learning styles, "10% of the brain," left/right brain dominance) among teachers, and can a targeted correction campaign reduce their influence on instructional decisions?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Neuromyths persist widely among educators despite decades of debunking, often shaping real instructional time and resource allocation — correcting them is a high-leverage, evidence-cleanup intervention rather than a new curriculum requirement.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Three real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Dekker, S., Lee, N. C., Howard-Jones, P., & Jolles, J. (2012). Predictors of teachers' misconceptions about the brain: Data from six countries. Frontiers in Psychology, 3, 429.

Dekker and colleagues surveyed teachers across the UK, Netherlands, Turkey, Greece, China, and Brazil about common brain-related claims, including several outright myths such as the idea that people only use 10% of their brains, or that individuals learn better when taught in their preferred "learning style." A striking majority of teachers in every country endorsed at least some of these myths, and — notably — teachers who read more popular science about the brain were not less likely to hold these misconceptions, and were sometimes more likely to. This is the direct evidence for the project's core claim that neuromyths are widespread among educators specifically, not just the general public.

### 2. Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D., & Bjork, R. (2008). Learning styles: Concepts and evidence. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 9(3), 105–119.

This widely-cited review examined the specific claim that students learn better when taught in their preferred "style" (visual, auditory, etc.). The authors found that while people do have preferences for how they like to receive information, there is no reliable experimental evidence that matching teaching style to a student's stated preference actually improves learning outcomes — the well-designed studies that could have found this effect consistently did not. This is the direct evidence used to debunk one of the most persistent and popular neuromyths, and it's a central example the project's toolkit should use.

### 3. Howard-Jones, P. A. (2014). Neuroscience and education: Myths and messages. Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 15(12), 817–824.

Howard-Jones surveys how and why neuromyths persist in education despite being repeatedly debunked, arguing that overly simplified translations of real neuroscience findings, combined with the intuitive appeal of certain myths, allow them to spread and resist correction even among engaged, well-meaning teachers. This source is useful for the project's "correction" component specifically — it doesn't just document that myths exist, it discusses why simple debunking often fails and what kind of communication is more likely to actually change teachers' beliefs.

## Full Project Toolkit

**Quick facts:** Team size 2–3 · ~3 hrs/week outside session · Cost: $0 · Needs: real teachers willing to take an anonymous survey.

**Ethics flag:** Moderate. Teachers are adults, not the usual peer participants — get sign-off from your chapter leader before distributing the survey to staff, and keep every response fully anonymous so no teacher can be identified or embarrassed by their answers.

**Materials by unit:** Unit I — an anonymous teacher survey on learning-styles and brain-myth beliefs (5–8 true/false or agree/disagree items). Unit II — the same survey given again after a short correction session. Unit III — a one-page myth-correction handout, recording equipment. Unit IV — spreadsheet for the belief-persistence model.

**Ready-to-use data template (Unit II):** Respondent ID (anonymous) · Pre-Survey Score · Post-Survey Score · Years Teaching (optional, broad range only, never exact).

**Worked example — the hardest step (writing survey items that actually test the myth, not just recognition), Unit I):** instead of asking "have you heard of learning styles," ask something behavior-linked: "I try to teach visual learners differently than auditory learners" (agree/disagree) — this measures whether the myth affects real practice, not just awareness.

**Common pitfalls:** teams sometimes ask leading questions that make the "correct" answer obvious, which biases results — have someone outside the team review survey wording for neutrality before distributing it.

**Elevator pitch:** "We surveyed our own teachers on common brain myths, corrected the biggest one with real evidence, and measured whether the correction actually stuck."

**Low-resource adaptation:** paper surveys collected in a sealed box for true anonymity; no digital tools needed at all.
