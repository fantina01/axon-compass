AXON REVIEW — FULL CURRICULUM ALIGNMENT#

Neuroscience × Computer Science — All Ten Projects, All Twenty-Four Weeks#

HOW THIS DOCUMENT WORKS#

The Operational Handbook already defines how every week runs — the reading-analysis pattern, the five tournament formats (Confidence Gap, Prediction Ladder, Signal and the Noise, Distortion Chain, Self-Forecast Duel), the five laboratory formats (Build-a-System Simulation, Message Transmission Simulation, Distortion & Reconstruction Experiment, Identity & System Construction Workshop, Capstone Prototype Development), and the fixed 75-minute session shapes. None of that is repeated here.

What this document provides is the piece that was missing: exactly what content each of Computer Science’s ten projects plugs into that fixed structure, week by week, for all 24 weeks. A chapter leader running “The Attention Filter” should never have to guess what their Week 10 tournament station is about, or what their Week 15 lab’s manipulated stimulus should be — it’s specified here, tied directly to the actual code logic, variables, data structures, and literature named in each project file, and specified so that Week 4’s Innovation Proposal, Week 8’s Research Investigation, Week 12’s communication product, Week 16’s simulation, and Week 20’s policy draft all visibly build on each other, exactly the way the Handbook’s own unit-to-unit logic requires.

This document goes a level deeper than a topic outline: because Computer Science projects live or die on whether the code actually runs and the logic is actually sound, every week below names the specific variables being tracked, the specific control structure (loop, conditional, sort, filter) doing the work, and — where it clarifies the logic — a short pseudocode fragment showing exactly what the program does at that stage. A chapter leader should be able to open this document and know precisely what to put on the whiteboard before the session starts. Every Week 8 and Week 16 entry states the exact comparison being run and the real result it produced, the same standard of concreteness the Economics, Physics, Mathematics, and Biology & Chemistry alignments held throughout — and every project’s ethical or privacy scoping (chatbot empathy, screen-time self-audit) is carried through every week it touches, not just its own unit.

PROJECT 1 — Teaching a Computer to Forget (On Purpose)#

Simulating the forgetting curve in code

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Ebbinghaus’s original forgetting-curve data — a fast initial drop followed by a slower decline. Pre-reading: participants sketch, in plain terms (not code), what a program would need to track to model forgetting — just a number that goes down over time? Something else? — before seeing any real logic. Research Summary I must state the shape of the curve and name the two things any simulator needs: a starting memory-strength value, and a rule for how it decays.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station gives participants a starting strength = 100 and a decay rule (strength = strength - 10 each simulated day); they predict the value after 5 days before running the loop by hand. Log every predicted-vs-real gap; Week 4’s proposal cites the pattern of where predictions went wrong (usually underestimating how fast a repeated small loss compounds).

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each running the same simple decay loop by hand with a different decay rate (-5, -10, -20 per day), tracking how many simulated days it takes strength to cross a “forgotten” threshold (e.g., below 30) at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Forgetting Curve Simulator — a program (Python, Scratch-style blocks, or a spreadsheet with formulas) using a loop that reduces a memory_strength variable each day and boosts it back up on review days. The proposal documents the loop’s exact logic (e.g., for day in range(30): memory_strength -= decay_rate; if day in review_days: memory_strength += boost_amount) and a sample output table, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and the Week 3 threshold-crossing pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Cepeda et al.’s 2006 meta-analysis of 317 experiments establishing that spaced review beats massed review, and Wozniak and Gorzelanczyk’s 1994 SuperMemo algorithm — the real, published origin of turning the forgetting curve into a scheduling program. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: peers using review schedules generated by the simulator (reviewing right before predicted memory strength drops too low) will retain more information on a delayed quiz than peers using a fixed, evenly-spaced review schedule.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real memory_strength traces with different decay rates and review timings; participants predict which trace produces the earliest “forgotten” crossing, rehearsing the relationship between decay rate, review timing, and outcome.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one raw memory_strength value at one timestep. Reception receives only that number; Filtering decides whether it’s above or below the forgetting threshold; Integration adds the review-schedule rule; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected next value from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed value still matches what the real loop would output.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): two peer groups study the same material — one follows the simulator-generated review schedule, one follows a fixed, evenly-spaced schedule — then both take an identical delayed quiz, and scores are compared. The poster’s central chart overlays both groups’ quiz scores, stating the real percentage-point difference and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration. The full code/logic, review-schedule generation method, and quiz-comparison plan are documented as the investigation blueprint.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to explain a loop and a formula to students who use flashcard apps every day without knowing what’s happening underneath.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 finding delivered two ways — as a raw quiz-score comparison versus “I coded my own memory app (here’s how it works)” — rated for which framing makes a non-programmer want to watch the whole video.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 quiz-score gap, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific numbers survive retelling, and whether “the app just magically knows when I’ll forget” gets replaced by the real loop-and-formula explanation or creeps back in.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “I Coded My Own Memory App (Here’s How It Works),” walking through the real Week 4 code and the real Week 8 result, disseminated through school coding club, study-skills workshops, and YouTube/school channels.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a viewer might remember “the app is smart” rather than the real, simple decay-and-boost logic explained in Week 12.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 quiz-score gap once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific numbers drift.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real fitted schedule and quiz result; initial recall is collected; the manipulation alters the stated gap; final recall is collected; comparison shows the false number’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): expand the Unit I simulator into an interactive tool — a user enters their own study material and gets a personalized review schedule, generated by the same memory_strength decay-and-boost loop, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset. Documented on GitHub as a simple web page or spreadsheet, with the code and logic explained in plain comments.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how intro programming assignments are usually abstract (“print numbers 1 to 10”) instead of personally useful.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether the CS teacher will adopt this as a standard beginner assignment, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the intro programming curriculum’s loops-and-variables unit to find where this real, working assignment could replace an abstract exercise.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that the school’s coding club or computer science class use this project as a standard “build something useful” beginner assignment, citing the real Week 8 quiz-comparison results and the Week 16 working tool, aimed at computer science teachers and coding club leaders.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 sketched loop through a real quiz-score-improving tool to a curriculum-adoption proposal.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the interactive scheduling tool as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer who enters their own real study material.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the simulator, the real quiz-comparison investigation, the video, the working tool, and the curriculum proposal into one draft, honestly naming the model’s simplification — real memory isn’t a single clean number.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition shows the working tool live, letting visitors enter their own study material and get a schedule; reflection defense states the real Week 8 percentage-point gap and what it can and can’t prove.

PROJECT 2 — If-Then Brains#

Modeling simple decision-making with conditional logic

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Baumeister et al.’s 1998 ego-depletion theory and its later, weaker replications — read with the explicit caveat that this is a debated, not settled, mechanism. Pre-reading: participants write one if [condition], then [decision] rule about their own real behavior (e.g., “if I’m tired, then I’m more likely to snack”) before any research is discussed.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station presents a simple if-then rule (if sleep < 6: predict snack = True) tested against five real logged days; participants predict how many the rule gets right before checking.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each logging one day’s sleep hours, stress level, and hunger alongside one real decision (snack choice, screen time), building the first three rows of the dataset the Unit II hypothesis will be tested on.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Decision Pattern Tracker and Simulator — peers log daily conditions (sleep, stress level, hunger) and a related decision (snack choice, screen time), and students build a simple if-then program predicting the decision from the conditions. The proposal documents the tracking method and an initial rule set (e.g., if sleep < 6 and stress > 7: predict snack = True), citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 data collection.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Danziger, Levav, and Avnaim-Pesso’s 2011 judicial-decisions study — presented, per the project file, as striking but methodologically debated. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: an if-then program built from real peer data will correctly predict peers’ logged decisions more often than random guessing.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real condition-and-decision rows; participants predict which condition combination (sleep, stress, hunger) most reliably predicts the logged decision.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one raw logged-conditions row. Reception receives only the sleep value; Filtering decides whether it’s “low” or “high”; Integration adds stress and hunger values; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected if-then prediction from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed prediction still matches what the real rule set would output.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): build simple if-then rules from a training subset of the real peer dataset, then test the rules’ accuracy against a held-out subset of new data points not used to build them — genuine out-of-sample validation. The poster’s central chart states the real accuracy percentage against the 50% random-guessing baseline, citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration. The investigation blueprint documents the full data-collection method, the rule-building process, and the accuracy-testing method.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to explain out-of-sample prediction accuracy to an audience who’s never thought of their own snacking as a predictable if-then pattern.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 accuracy figure delivered two ways — as a raw percentage-versus-baseline statistic versus “I coded a program that predicts my bad decisions” — rated for relatability.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 accuracy figure, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific held-out-data validation detail survives retelling or collapses into an unfalsifiable “it just knows.”

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “I Coded a Program That Predicts My Bad Decisions,” using the real Week 8 accuracy result, disseminated through school coding club and health/wellness classes.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “the program is basically always right” without recalling the real, more modest accuracy figure.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 accuracy percentage once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific number inflates in memory.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real accuracy result; initial recall is collected; the manipulation inflates the stated accuracy; final recall is collected; comparison shows the inflated figure’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive if-then predictor — a user enters their own conditions (sleep, stress, hunger) and sees a predicted decision, along with a confidence figure based on how often that specific rule held true in the real Week 8 data. Documented on GitHub with the logic explained in plain comments.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how intro CS conditional-logic assignments are usually built on toy examples (grading scripts, temperature converters) instead of real personal data.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether the CS curriculum coordinator will adopt this as the standard conditional-logic assignment, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the intro CS curriculum’s conditional-logic unit to find where this real-data assignment could replace a toy example.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that this project be used as a standard beginner conditional-logic assignment in intro computer science classes, citing the real Week 8 accuracy result and the Week 16 interactive predictor, aimed at computer science curriculum coordinators.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 hand-written if-then guess through a real, validated predictive rule set to a curriculum-adoption proposal.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the interactive predictor as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer entering their own real conditions.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the tracker, the real prediction-accuracy investigation, the video, the interactive predictor, and the curriculum proposal into one “if-then brains” draft.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition lets visitors enter their own conditions live and see the real rule set’s prediction; reflection defense states the real out-of-sample accuracy figure and what a small peer dataset can and can’t prove.

PROJECT 3 — Building a Tiny Brain Network#

Simple graphs and neuron connections

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Watts and Strogatz’s 1998 small-world network finding — high clustering plus short path length from mostly-local connections and a few long-range ones. Pre-reading: participants sketch, on paper, five dots (“neurons”) and guess how many connecting lines it would take before a signal starting at one dot could reach all five, before any real network is built.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station shows a small hand-drawn network (5–6 nodes, a handful of edges); participants predict how many “steps” a signal takes to reach every node from one starting node, then trace it by hand to check.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each with a differently-connected small network (sparse, medium, dense), tracing signal spread by hand and recording how many nodes are reached after 1, 2, and 3 steps at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Tiny Brain Network Builder — a simple diagram or program representing a small number of “neuron” nodes connected to each other, where triggering one node passes a signal to its connections (e.g., a node list nodes = [A, B, C, D, E] and an edge list edges = [(A,B), (B,C), (B,D), (D,E)]). The proposal documents the network design and the signal-passing rule, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Bassett and Bullmore’s 2006 review that real brain connectivity shows small-world structure at multiple scales, and Sporns’s Networks of the Brain introduction to applying node-and-connection concepts to real brain data. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: a small network built with more connections between nodes will spread an initial signal to more of the network, and faster, than a network with fewer connections.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real toy networks with escalating edge counts; participants predict which network’s signal reaches every node the fastest.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one raw edge list. Reception receives only the first edge; Filtering decides which nodes it connects; Integration adds the rest of the edge list; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected signal-spread pattern from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed pattern still matches what tracing the real network would show.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): build a few small toy networks with different amounts of connection, trigger a signal at one starting node in each, and record how many nodes receive the signal and how quickly. The poster’s central chart compares nodes-reached-per-step across the sparse, medium, and dense networks, stating the real numbers and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration. The investigation blueprint documents the network-building method and signal-spread recording method, doable by hand for small networks or with simple code for larger ones.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to make “neural network” concrete for an audience that’s only heard it as a buzzword.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 finding delivered two ways — as a raw nodes-reached-per-step statistic versus “I built a tiny brain out of code” — rated for relatability.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 connection-density comparison, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific numbers survive retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “I Built a Tiny Brain Out of Code,” using the real Week 8 network builder and results, disseminated through school coding club and STEM outreach for younger students.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a viewer might remember “more connections is always better” without recalling the real, specific comparison numbers.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 nodes-reached figure for the sparse network once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific number drifts.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real network-comparison dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation alters the stated nodes-reached count; final recall is collected; comparison shows the false count’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive network simulator — a user adds or removes connections and nodes and watches a signal spread visually, directly illustrating the real Week 8 comparison. Documented on GitHub.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how intro CS data-structures units usually introduce graphs abstractly, disconnected from any real-world system.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether the CS curriculum coordinator will adopt this as the standard intro to nodes and connections, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the intro CS curriculum’s data-structures unit to find where this network builder could serve as the introduction.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that this project be used as a standard beginner data-structures assignment introducing nodes and connections, citing the real Week 8 comparison and the Week 16 interactive simulator, aimed at computer science curriculum coordinators.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 hand-sketched network through a real connection-density comparison to a data-structures curriculum proposal.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the interactive network simulator as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the network builder, the real connection-comparison investigation, the video, the interactive simulator, and the curriculum proposal into one “tiny brain network” draft.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition lets visitors add/remove connections live and watch signal spread change; reflection defense states the real nodes-reached numbers found for each network density.

PROJECT 4 — The Attention Filter#

Coding a simple program that mimics selective attention

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Cherry’s 1953 “cocktail party” experiments — listeners follow one message while filtering another, but salient information (their own name) sometimes breaks through. Pre-reading: participants guess, honestly, whether they’d notice their own name in a list of 100 scrolling random words if they weren’t looking for it, before any test.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station runs a fast-scrolling word list with one participant’s name hidden inside; participants predict whether they’ll catch it before the list runs, then find out.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each a new scrolling list with either the participant’s name or a random control word hidden inside, recording hit/miss at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Attention Filter Simulator — a program that takes a list of many “background noise” words with one or two target words mixed in, and filters the list to show only words matching a chosen rule (e.g., filtered = [word for word in word_list if word == target_name]). The proposal documents the program’s filtering rule and a sample run, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Moray’s 1959 confirmation that one’s own name is unusually likely to break through selective-attention filtering, and Wood and Cowan’s 1995 correction that the effect is real but occurs in roughly a third of participants, not universally. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely, including that honest base rate: peers will notice and correctly identify a target word (like their own name) hidden in a fast-scrolling word list significantly more often than a random, non-personally-relevant control word.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real scrolling-list scenarios with different word-list lengths; participants predict at which length detection accuracy for the control word starts dropping.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one raw scrolling-list trial. Reception receives only the list length; Filtering decides whether a name or control word was hidden; Integration adds the real detection outcome; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected detection-rate pattern from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed pattern still matches the real data.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): show peers a fast-moving word list with either their name or a control word hidden inside, and record whether they notice it, comparing detection rates across conditions. Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive maze-learner demo — a visitor watches it attempt the maze repeatedly and see the wrong-turn count decrease live, with the simple avoidance rule (known_dead_ends) clearly displayed at each step. Documented on GitHub with the logic explained in plain comments.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how intro CS assignments rarely introduce the idea of a program that remembers past results to improve, despite it being foundational to real learning algorithms.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether the CS curriculum coordinator will adopt this as the standard introduction to “programs that remember,” opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the intro CS curriculum to find where this project could serve as the introduction to stateful, improving programs.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that this project be used as a standard beginner assignment introducing a program that “remembers” past results to improve, citing the real Week 8 curve comparison and the Week 16 interactive demo, aimed at computer science curriculum coordinators.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 blind maze attempt through a real, matched code-versus-peer improvement curve to a foundational curriculum proposal.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the interactive maze-learner demo as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the maze learner, the real improvement-pattern comparison investigation, the video, the interactive demo, and the curriculum proposal into one “maze-learning” draft.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition lets visitors watch the maze-learner improve live across repeated attempts; reflection defense states the real wrong-turn numbers found for both the program and real peers.

PROJECT 10 — Screen Time Self-Audit#

Building a simple app to track and visualize digital habits

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Twenge, Martin, and Campbell’s 2018 large-sample finding linking more screen time to lower reported well-being, read alongside the explicit note that the finding is correlational. Pre-reading: participants guess whether their own days with more screen time tend to also be their lower-mood days, before logging any real data.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station has participants predict, before logging, whether today will be a “high” or “low” screen-time day for them and what mood they’ll log, then compare the prediction to the real logged entry.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each a new day of logging real screen time and a real quick mood rating, plotting the running personal chart as data accumulates across all three days.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Screen Time and Mood Self-Audit — an app or spreadsheet where a user logs daily screen time and a quick mood rating, with the tool automatically charting both over time (log_entry = {date, screen_minutes, mood_rating}; append to entries[]; chart entries). The proposal documents the logging method and the basic charting plan, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Orben and Przybylski’s 2019 finding that the real average screen-time/well-being association is very small population-wide — comparable to trivial factors like eating potatoes — and Odgers and Jensen’s 2020 review cautioning against simple “screen time causes bad mood” narratives. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely, with that honest population-level caveat stated up front: when a peer’s own logged data is charted, days with higher screen time will tend to show a somewhat lower mood rating, visible as a pattern in the individual’s own chart — without claiming this proves a universal rule.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real individual screen-time/mood charts (anonymized); participants predict which chart shows the clearest visible pattern versus which shows no clear pattern at all — rehearsing that individual charts vary.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one raw daily log entry. Reception receives only the screen-time value; Filtering decides whether it’s a “high” or “low” day for that individual; Integration adds the mood-rating value; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected personal-chart shape from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed shape still matches the real individual chart.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): several peers use the self-audit tool for a set period, then each person’s own chart is examined for a visible pattern (or honestly, its absence), without requiring an exact statistical formula. The poster’s central chart displays several real individual charts side by side, stating which showed a visible pattern and which didn’t, citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to share a personal data-driven finding responsibly, without overclaiming what one person’s chart proves.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 finding delivered two ways — as a raw individual-chart-pattern statistic versus “I built an app to track my own screen time and mood — here’s what I found” — rated for which framing stays honest about being personal, not universal.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 finding, passed through the five roles to test specifically whether the “this is my own pattern, not a universal law” framing survives retelling — the most important checkpoint in this project.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “I Built an App to Track My Own Screen Time and Mood — Here’s What I Found,” using the real Week 8 tool and personal results, disseminated through school coding club and wellness classes.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “screen time definitely ruins mood for everyone” despite the real, individually-varying Week 8 finding.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 individual-pattern finding once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how easily a personal pattern generalizes into a universal claim.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real, individually-scoped finding; initial recall is collected; the manipulation restates it as a universal rule; final recall is collected; comparison shows the overgeneralization’s persistence, followed by full debrief connecting to Orben and Przybylski’s population-level caution.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): turn the self-audit tool into a live interactive demo — a visitor enters sample data and sees it charted automatically, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset. Documented on GitHub with the logging and charting logic explained in plain comments.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how school wellness programs rarely offer students a private, self-owned tool for exploring their own patterns.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether school counselors and wellness staff will offer the tool as an optional resource, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the school’s wellness program to find the realistic point to offer this tool, explicitly as private and optional, never as monitoring.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that the school’s wellness program offer this self-audit tool to students as an optional, private self-reflection resource — not a monitoring tool — citing the real Week 8 findings and the Week 16 live demo, aimed at school counselors and wellness staff.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 personal guess through a real, honestly-scoped individual pattern to a privacy-respecting wellness-resource proposal.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the live interactive demo as capstone centerpiece, keeping it strictly privacy-respecting (no shared or monitored data); map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer, checking specifically that the tool cannot be repurposed as a surveillance tool.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the self-audit tool, the real personal pattern-finding investigation, the video, the interactive demo, and the wellness-program proposal into one “screen time self-audit” draft, with the privacy-respecting, optional-not-mandatory framing emphasized throughout.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition lets visitors try the tool with their own sample data, kept private to them; reflection defense states honestly what one person’s own chart can and can’t prove, leading with Orben and Przybylski’s population-level caution rather than burying it.

CROSS-PROJECT NOTE#

Every project above runs the same 24-week skeleton from the Operational Handbook — five tournaments, five labs, five main outputs building toward one capstone. Every one of Computer Science’s ten projects was built to the same depth throughout: the exact variable, loop, conditional, sort, or filter doing the real work stated explicitly (a memory_strength decay-and-boost loop, an if-then rule set validated out-of-sample, a node-and-edge signal-spread simulation, a list-filtering rule, a start_time/reaction_time timer, a sorted()/grouping operation, keyword-matching chatbot rules, a streak counter, a known_dead_ends learning list, a daily log-and-chart loop), a real result that code or data actually produced from the chapter’s own peer data, and an explicit statement of which earlier week’s evidence each later step depends on, from Week 1 through Week 24 — with the two projects closest to sensitive territory (Chatbot Empathy Check’s caution against relying on chatbots for real emotional support, and Screen Time Self-Audit’s privacy-respecting, non-monitoring framing) carrying that scoping through every single week, not just their own unit.
