7. Chatbot Empathy Check#

Testing How Well a Simple Rule-Based Chatbot Responds to Emotional Language

Field: Neuroscience × Computer Science  ·  Project ID: computer-science/07-chatbot-empathy-check

Big Question

Using only simple keyword-matching rules (no advanced AI needed), can students build a basic chatbot and test how well — or poorly — it responds to emotional messages compared to a real person?

Why This Matters

Many students already interact with simple chatbots, but rarely think critically about how limited basic rule-based systems are at understanding emotional nuance — building one from scratch, with simple if-then keyword rules, makes those limitations concrete and teaches healthy skepticism about AI’s current capabilities.

Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II#

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they’re what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

1. Weizenbaum, J. (1966). ELIZA — a computer program for the study of natural language communication between man and machine. Communications of the ACM, 9(1), 36-45.#

Weizenbaum’s ELIZA was one of the first chatbots, using simple keyword-matching and pattern rules (much like the project’s own chatbot) to simulate a psychotherapist. Surprisingly, many users reported feeling genuinely understood by ELIZA despite its extremely simple rule-based design — a phenomenon Weizenbaum himself found concerning, since it suggested people can attribute understanding to a system with none. This is the foundational, historical source for the entire concept the project investigates.

2. Reeves, B., & Nass, C. (1996). The Media Equation: How People Treat Computers, Television, and New Media Like Real People and Places. Cambridge University Press.#

Reeves and Nass’s research found that people unconsciously apply social rules and expect social responsiveness from computers and media, even when they consciously know they’re interacting with a machine. This is directly useful for the project’s investigation: it helps explain why people might rate even a simple rule-based chatbot’s responses somewhat favorably, despite knowing intellectually that no real understanding is happening behind them.

3. Luxton, D. D. (2014). Recommendations for the ethical use and design of artificial intelligent care providers. Artificial Intelligence in Medicine, 62(1), 1-10.#

Luxton discusses the ethical considerations of AI-based systems presenting themselves as caring or empathetic, including the specific risk of users over-relying on simple systems for genuine emotional support they cannot actually provide. This is directly relevant to the project’s responsible messaging component — the explicit caution against using simple chatbots as a substitute for real emotional support is grounded in genuine, published ethical guidance, not just an assumption.

Exact Steps, Unit by Unit#

Every step below plugs into the fixed season process described in The Universal Process — tournaments and laboratories run identically regardless of field. Only the content below is specific to this project.

Unit I — Learning Innovation Challenge#

Weeks 1–4 · Required artifact: Innovation Proposal I

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Run the Week 2 Confidence Gap tournament (see the Universal Process chapter).

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] Students design a “Simple Empathy Chatbot” — a basic rule-based chatbot (using simple if-then keyword matching, no advanced AI/machine learning needed) that responds to emotional keywords in a message (like “stressed,” “excited,” “sad”) with a pre-written, appropriate-sounding response. Innovation Proposal I documents the chatbot’s rule list and a sample conversation.

Unit II — Research Investigation#

Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: Research Investigation / Prospectus

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 6 Prediction Ladder tournament + Week 7 Build-a-System laboratory.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] Literature review: plain-language summaries of how simple rule-based chatbots work versus more advanced systems, plus basic research on what makes human empathetic responses feel genuine (tone matching, follow-up questions).

  • [ ] Hypothesis: peers will rate responses from a real person as noticeably more empathetic and satisfying than responses from the rule-based chatbot, even when both are responding to the same emotional messages.

  • [ ] Variables: response source (chatbot vs. real person) as the thing being compared; peer-rated empathy/satisfaction score as the outcome.

  • [ ] Methodology: have peers send the same set of emotional messages to both the chatbot and a real person (blind to which is which where possible), then rate the responses, and compare average ratings.

Unit III — Public Communication & Social Impact#

Weeks 9–12 · Required artifact: Communication Product + Dissemination Plan

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 10 Signal and the Noise tournament + Week 11 Message Transmission laboratory.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] A short video, “I Built a Chatbot and Tested If It Actually Understands Feelings,” using the same chatbot and rating results; dissemination through school coding club and health/wellness classes, with a clear message about not relying on simple chatbots for real emotional support.

Unit IV — Memory/Decision Systems Simulation#

Weeks 13–16 · Required artifact: Simulation Model IV

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 14 Distortion Chain tournament + Week 15 Distortion & Reconstruction laboratory.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] Simulates the chatbot interactively, letting visitors type a message and see the rule-based response, alongside a clear explanation of exactly which keyword triggered which response, showing the simplicity behind the system; documented on GitHub.

Unit V — Policy Formation Simulation#

Weeks 17–20 · Required artifact: Policy Draft Document

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 18 Self-Forecast Duel tournament + Week 19 Identity & System Construction laboratory.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] Policy proposal recommending schools include a basic AI-literacy lesson (using this chatbot project as the example) so students understand the real limitations of simple chatbot systems, aimed at computer science and health curriculum coordinators.

Unit VI — Integration & Summit#

Weeks 21–24 · Required artifact: Capstone Project

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 22 Capstone Prototype Development laboratory, leading into the Week 24 Summit.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] Capstone integrates the chatbot, the empathy-rating study, the video, the interactive demo, and the AI-literacy curriculum proposal into one “chatbot empathy check” portfolio.

Skills This Project Builds#

  • Building a working rule-based chatbot using keyword-matching logic.

  • Designing a real, controlled comparison between chatbot and human responses.

  • Communicating AI capabilities and limitations responsibly to a general audience.

  • Building a transparent, explorable demo showing exactly how the system works.

  • Proposing an AI-literacy lesson to a CS or health curriculum.

Why This Project Impresses#

Builds real (if simple) working software and uses it to responsibly teach an important, current lesson about AI limitations — technically grounded and genuinely thoughtful.

How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio#

Every unit of this project builds toward the same responsible conclusion: simple chatbots are limited, and knowing exactly why matters. Unit I builds the first working rule-based chatbot. Unit II tests it directly against real human responses to the same messages. Unit III explains that real comparison to an audience who uses chatbots without examining their limits. Unit IV makes the chatbot’s simple logic fully visible and explorable. Unit V proposes this exact project become the standard AI-literacy lesson. A CS or AI-ethics mentor sees a student who built real, working software specifically to teach an important, current lesson about AI’s real limitations, responsibly and rigorously.

Maximize Your Impact — What To Do With This After the Season#

  • Propose the project as a standard AI-literacy lesson for your school’s computer science or health curriculum.

  • Submit the empathy-rating study to a science fair under AI ethics or human-computer interaction.

  • Share the interactive chatbot demo publicly, with its clear limitations explanation, as an AI-literacy teaching tool.
