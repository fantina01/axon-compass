Neuroscience × Biology & Chemistry — All Ten Projects, All Twenty-Four Weeks#

HOW THIS DOCUMENT WORKS#

The Operational Handbook already defines how every week runs — the reading-analysis pattern, the five tournament formats (Confidence Gap, Prediction Ladder, Signal and the Noise, Distortion Chain, Self-Forecast Duel), the five laboratory formats (Build-a-System Simulation, Message Transmission Simulation, Distortion & Reconstruction Experiment, Identity & System Construction Workshop, Capstone Prototype Development), and the fixed 75-minute session shapes. None of that is repeated here.

What this document provides is the piece that was missing: exactly what content each of Biology & Chemistry’s ten projects plugs into that fixed structure, week by week, for all 24 weeks. A chapter leader running “The Chemistry of a Craving” should never have to guess what their Week 10 tournament station is about, or what their Week 15 lab’s manipulated stimulus should be — it’s specified here, tied directly to the mechanisms, comparisons, and literature named in each project file, and specified so that Week 4’s Innovation Proposal, Week 8’s Research Investigation, Week 12’s communication product, Week 16’s simulation, and Week 20’s policy draft all visibly build on each other, exactly the way the Handbook’s own unit-to-unit logic requires.

Every entry below names the correct universal format from the Handbook and then gives the project-specific content for that slot — nothing here contradicts or replaces the Handbook’s mechanics. Because several of this field’s projects (craving chemistry, medications, symmetry-adjacent topics like antioxidant marketing) sit close to sensitive or health-adjacent territory, every relevant week below keeps the same disclaimers, consent language, and educational-not-medical framing that the project files themselves specify — nothing here loosens that scoping.

PROJECT 1 — The Chemistry of a Craving#

Neurotransmitter molecules and food choice

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Volkow, Wang, and Baler’s 2013 review that highly palatable, processed foods trigger stronger dopamine release than whole foods, and can shift the brain’s reward threshold over time. Pre-reading: participants write down, honestly, one food they find hard to stop eating and guess whether that’s “just willpower” or has a real chemical explanation, before the reading resolves it.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station asks participants to predict, before discussing, whether a whole food or a processed sugary food produces a stronger dopamine response — then the real research finding is revealed. Log every prediction against the real answer.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials of a simple taste/craving-rating exercise, comparing self-rated “want to keep eating” scores across three different snack types, tracking the pattern across all three.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Craving Chemistry Explainer Kit — a diagram-based lesson showing the basic dopamine reward pathway and how high-sugar/high-fat foods trigger it more strongly than whole foods, paired with a simple mindful-eating strategy card. The proposal documents a peer survey on cravings and eating patterns and the explainer kit’s content, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 rating pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Avena, Rada, and Hoebel’s 2008 review of sugar’s neurochemical effects in animal studies, and Small, Jones-Gotman, and Dagher’s 2003 human brain-imaging evidence that meal pleasantness correlates with real dopamine release. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely, including the honest caveat that “sugar addiction” evidence is largely animal-based and still debated in humans: peers who receive the craving-chemistry explanation will report more intentional (rather than automatic) snack choices over a one-week period compared to a group that does not receive the explanation.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real food-choice scenarios; participants predict which triggers the largest self-reported craving, calibrating intuitions about hyper-palatable versus whole foods.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a raw self-reported snack-choice log. Reception receives only the food name; Filtering decides whether it counts as “intentional” or “automatic”; Integration adds the craving-chemistry explanation; Reconstruction rebuilds an expected choice pattern from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed pattern still matches the real log.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the pre/post survey design across two peer groups, one receiving the explainer kit and one not, tracking snack-choice patterns over one week using a simple tally-based scoring method. The poster’s central chart compares the real percentage of intentional-versus-automatic choices between groups, citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to replace shame-based food messaging with real biology, aimed at students and parents frustrated by snack habits.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 finding delivered two ways — as a raw intentional-vs-automatic percentage versus “why you can’t just have one chip (the chemistry behind it)” — rated for which framing feels less shame-based.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 finding, passed through the five roles to test whether the non-shaming framing survives retelling or collapses back into “it’s just willpower.”

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Why You Can’t Just Have One Chip (The Chemistry Behind It),” using the real Week 8 data, disseminated through school health class, cafeteria posters, and the parent newsletter.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “cravings are pure willpower” despite the real chemistry explained in Week 8.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 finding once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how easily the chemistry explanation drifts back into a willpower framing.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real Week 8 finding; initial recall is collected; the manipulation reframes the finding as a willpower failure; final recall is collected; comparison shows how easily the shame-based framing creeps back in, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive diagram-based model showing dopamine release levels for different food types (whole food versus processed/sugary food) and how repeated exposure to high-reward foods can raise the bar for what feels satisfying over time — a user selects a food and sees an estimated relative dopamine-response bar, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset. Documented on GitHub with citations to the underlying research.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how school health curricula usually deliver generic nutrition advice without any real chemistry mechanism behind it.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether the school wellness committee will adopt the craving-chemistry lesson, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the school’s health curriculum and wellness committee structure to find the realistic point to insert this lesson.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that the school health curriculum include this craving-chemistry lesson alongside standard nutrition content, citing the real Week 8 survey results and Week 16 diagram model, aimed at health teachers and the school wellness committee.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 honest self-reflection through a real measured behavior shift to a curriculum recommendation.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the dopamine-response diagram model as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the explainer kit, the real pre/post investigation, the video, the diagram model, and the curriculum proposal into one draft, honestly naming the limitations — self-report data, small peer sample.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition shows the explainer kit next to the dopamine-response diagram; reflection defense states the real percentage shift found and what self-reported data can and can’t prove.

PROJECT 2 — pH and the Brain#

How blood chemistry balance affects mental clarity

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Adan’s 2012 review that even mild dehydration (1–2% of body weight in fluid loss) is associated with measurable declines in attention, memory, and mood. Pre-reading: participants guess, as a percent, how much water loss they think it takes to measurably hurt concentration, before the real figure is revealed.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station has participants predict their own score on a short attention test before and after drinking a glass of water, then compares predicted-vs-real change.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials of a short letter-cancellation-style attention test at different points in the day, recording self-reported water intake alongside the score each time.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Hydration and Focus Tracker — peers track their water intake and take a short focus/attention test at different points in the day, alongside a plain-language explanation of how dehydration affects blood chemistry balance. The proposal documents the tracking method and the chemistry explanation, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Ganio et al.’s 2011 controlled study measuring cognitive decline under mild dehydration versus proper hydration, and Boron and Boulpaep’s standard physiology explanation of how the body’s buffering systems maintain stable blood pH. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: peers who are more hydrated (based on self-tracked water intake) will score higher on a short attention test than when they are less hydrated, measured within the same individuals on different days.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real hydration-level scenarios; participants predict which shows the largest attention-score drop.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is a raw self-reported water-intake figure for one day. Reception receives only that number; Filtering decides whether it counts as “hydrated” or “under-hydrated”; Integration adds the attention-test score for that day; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected relationship from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed relationship still matches the real data.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the within-person comparison across multiple days with varying reported water intake, using a repeatable attention test. The poster’s central chart plots attention score against hydration level across the real collected days, citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to connect standard chemistry-class pH lessons to a genuinely relevant, personal health question.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 finding delivered two ways — as a raw hydration-vs-score statistic versus “why chemistry class pH lessons actually matter for your grades” — rated for relatability.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 finding, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific hydration-score relationship survives retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Why Chemistry Class pH Lessons Actually Matter for Your Grades,” using the real Week 8 tracker data, disseminated through school health class and sports teams.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “water doesn’t really affect focus that much” despite the real measured data.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 finding once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific relationship weakens in memory.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real hydration/attention dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation flattens the reported relationship; final recall is collected; comparison shows the flattening’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive diagram showing how the body’s chemical buffering systems work to maintain stable blood pH, and what happens when hydration/breathing patterns push against that balance, using a basic acid-base chemistry framework familiar from litmus-paper-style lessons, calibrated with the real Week 8 dataset. Documented on GitHub.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how limited water-bottle access or water-break policy could be a real, low-cost point of school intervention.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether school administration will approve more water breaks and in-class water bottles, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the school’s current water-access policy to find the realistic point for a low-cost change.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that the school allow more frequent water breaks and encourage water bottles in class, citing the real Week 8 data, aimed at school administration and health teachers.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 percent-loss guess through a real within-person hydration-and-focus dataset to a low-cost policy ask.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the buffering-system diagram as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the tracker, the real hydration-study investigation, the video, the buffering-system diagram, and the water-access policy into one “pH and the brain” draft.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition displays the real hydration-versus-attention chart beside the buffering-system diagram; reflection defense states the real numbers found and what a self-tracked dataset can and can’t prove.

PROJECT 3 — Cells Under Stress#

Comparing neuron health under simulated conditions

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Attwell and Laughlin’s 2001 finding that neurons consume disproportionately large amounts of energy maintaining their signaling capability. Pre-reading: participants guess whether a neuron or a skin cell needs more constant energy delivery to survive, before the reading resolves it.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station presents a pair of cell types (neuron vs. skin cell, or neuron vs. muscle cell); participants predict which is more vulnerable to a brief interruption in oxygen or nutrient supply, before the real comparison is revealed.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials each comparing a different resource (oxygen, glucose, repair capacity) between neurons and a more resilient cell type, using the published comparison data, tracking the predicted-vs-real vulnerability ranking each time.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Neuron Vulnerability Explainer Kit comparing basic cell-biology features of neurons (high energy use, long processes, limited division/repair) to a more resilient cell type, explaining why lifestyle factors that reduce oxygen or nutrient delivery hit neurons especially hard. The proposal documents the comparison chart and its biological basis, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Harris, Jolivet, and Attwell’s 2012 review of how neurons’ energy needs are supplied with very limited local reserves, and Rink and Khanna’s 2011 review of how quickly neural tissue is damaged when oxygen delivery is interrupted. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: peers who receive the explainer kit will more accurately answer a follow-up quiz about why sleep and stress affect the brain specifically, compared to a group that only receives general “sleep is good for you” messaging.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real resource-shortfall scenarios (a few minutes without oxygen, a day of poor sleep, chronic stress); participants predict which would harm neurons the most based on the comparison chart.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one raw resource-shortfall scenario. Reception receives only the scenario description; Filtering decides which cell type it affects most; Integration adds the neuron-versus-resilient-cell comparison; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected explanation from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed explanation still matches the real cell-biology comparison.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the two-group comparison — one group receives the cell-biology explainer, one receives only generic “sleep is good” messaging — then both take the same follow-up quiz, and scores are compared. The poster’s central chart compares average quiz scores between groups, citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to make a genuinely important health message land harder than generic advice, for health classes tired of hearing “sleep is good for you.”

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 finding delivered two ways — as a raw quiz-score comparison versus “why your brain cells have it harder than your skin cells” — rated for relatability.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 quiz-score comparison, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific numbers survive retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Why Your Brain Cells Have It Harder Than Your Skin Cells,” using the real Week 8 comparison chart, disseminated through school biology class and health workshops.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “all cells need the same things” without recalling the real, specific vulnerability comparison.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 quiz-score gap once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific gap drifts.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real quiz-comparison dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation flattens the reported score gap; final recall is collected; comparison shows the flattening’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive side-by-side diagram comparing “resource needs” (oxygen, nutrients, repair ability) between a neuron and a more resilient cell type, letting a user see how a shortfall in any resource affects each cell type differently, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset. Documented on GitHub.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how biology curricula usually teach cell biology and health education as separate, unconnected units.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether biology curriculum coordinators will adopt this comparison as a standard lesson, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the school’s biology curriculum to find the realistic point for a cell-biology-meets-health-education lesson.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that biology curricula include this neuron-vulnerability comparison as a standard lesson connecting cell biology to health education, citing the real Week 8 quiz results and Week 16 diagram, aimed at biology curriculum coordinators.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 guess through a real quiz-score improvement to a curriculum proposal.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the resource-needs diagram as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the explainer kit, the real quiz-comparison investigation, the video, the resource-needs diagram, and the curriculum proposal into one “cells under stress” draft.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition shows the resource-needs diagram beside the real quiz-score comparison; reflection defense states the real numbers found and what a two-group school comparison can and can’t prove.

PROJECT 4 — The Chemical Messengers Chart#

Mapping hormones and neurotransmitters like a periodic table

UNIT I — Weeks 1–4#

Week 1 (Reading): Article I focus is Nestler, Hyman, and Malenka’s standard organization of neurotransmitters and hormones by chemical class and functional effect. Pre-reading: participants guess how many major neurotransmitters/hormones they can name and correctly categorize by function, before seeing the real organized chart.

Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP): the station shows a short unorganized list of messenger names; participants predict how many they’ll correctly recall and categorize a minute later, then attempt the real recall.

Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment): three trials, each studying a different short chemical-messenger list — one unorganized, one grouped by chemical category, one grouped by function — recording recall accuracy at every trial.

Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I): design the Chemical Messenger Reference Chart — organizing major neurotransmitters and hormones by basic chemical category and by general function. The proposal documents the chart design and category choices, citing the Week 2 confidence gap and Week 3 trial pattern.

UNIT II — Weeks 5–8#

Week 5 (Reading): Article II focus is Mayer’s 2011 finding that serotonin is produced extensively outside the brain, and Sapolsky’s synthesis of how stress hormones interact with broader physiology. Research Summary II must state the season’s testable hypothesis precisely: peers using the organized reference chart will correctly recall more messenger-function pairings on a follow-up quiz than peers given the same information as an unorganized list.

Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER): stations present five real messenger-function pairings; participants predict which pairing is hardest to remember without an organizing structure.

Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION): the stimulus is one raw messenger name. Reception receives only the name; Filtering decides which chemical category it belongs to; Integration adds its general function; Reconstruction rebuilds the expected chart entry from memory; Decision judges whether the reconstructed entry still matches the real chart.

Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation): run the two-group study — one studies the organized chart, one studies the same information as an unorganized list — then both take an identical recall quiz, and scores are compared. The poster’s central chart compares average recall accuracy between the organized and unorganized groups, citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to present a study tool to biology and chemistry classes drowning in unconnected vocabulary.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 finding delivered two ways — as a raw recall-accuracy statistic versus “a periodic table for your brain chemicals” — rated for relatability.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 recall-accuracy comparison, passed through the five roles to test whether the specific numbers survive retelling.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “A ‘Periodic Table’ for Your Brain Chemicals,” using the real Week 8 chart and results, disseminated through school biology and chemistry classes.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a student might remember “I basically knew all the messengers” without recalling the real, lower unorganized-list recall score.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 recall-accuracy gap once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how the specific gap drifts.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real recall-comparison dataset; initial recall is collected; the manipulation narrows the reported gap; final recall is collected; comparison shows the narrowing’s persistence, followed by full debrief.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive version of the chart — a user clicks a messenger and sees its chemical category, main functions, and related messengers highlighted, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset. Documented on GitHub.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how biology and chemistry curricula usually teach this vocabulary as separate memorization lists rather than one organized system.

The poster’s central chart compares before-and-after belief ratings for each claim, stating the real average shift and citing the Week 6 tournament’s calibration.

UNIT III — Weeks 9–12#

Week 9 (Reading): Article III focus is how to model honest, careful science communication on a topic saturated with marketing.

Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE): the real Week 8 finding delivered two ways — as a raw belief-shift statistic versus “do ‘brain-boosting’ antioxidant foods actually work? let’s check the chemistry” — rated for which framing stays honest without dismissing the real chemistry entirely.

Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION): the ground truth is the real Week 8 belief-shift finding, passed through the five roles to test specifically whether the careful, nuanced framing survives retelling or collapses into either “antioxidants are a scam” or “antioxidants are proven” — the most important checkpoint in this project.

Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project): produce “Do ‘Brain-Boosting’ Antioxidant Foods Actually Work? Let’s Check the Chemistry,” using the real Week 8 fact-check comparison, disseminated through school chemistry/health classes and general audiences, modeling careful, honest science communication.

UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16#

Week 13 (Reading): Article IV focus is memory reconstruction, applied to why a listener might remember “antioxidants are definitely good” or “antioxidants are a myth” — either oversimplification — despite the real, nuanced Week 8 finding.

Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN): state the real Week 8 nuanced finding once, distract, then ask for a restatement — quantifying how easily the nuance collapses into one of the two oversimplified extremes.

Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT): observation is the real, nuanced Week 8 finding; initial recall is collected; the manipulation pushes the restatement toward one extreme; final recall is collected; comparison shows the nuance’s fragility, followed by full debrief connecting to science-reporting oversimplification generally.

Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation): build the interactive diagram showing a basic oxidation reaction and how an antioxidant molecule can neutralize a reactive molecule, helping users understand the real, limited-scope mechanism behind the claims, calibrated using the real Week 8 dataset. Documented on GitHub with clear, honest sourcing.

UNIT V — Weeks 17–20#

Week 17 (Reading): Integration article focus — applied to how health/nutrition curricula rarely teach students to evaluate food-marketing claims against real research.

Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL): sealed predictions about whether health and science curriculum coordinators will adopt a media-literacy component using this case study, opened in Week 19.

Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP): map the school’s health/nutrition curriculum to find the realistic point for a media-literacy lesson built on this case study.

Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation): draft the recommendation that the school’s health/nutrition curriculum include a media-literacy component specifically addressing food marketing claims, using this antioxidant example as the case study, citing the real Week 8 belief-shift data and Week 16 diagram, aimed at health and science curriculum coordinators.

UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24#

Week 21 (Synthesis): the throughline runs from a Week 1 belief rating through a real measured belief-shift finding to a media-literacy curriculum proposal.

Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT): finalize the oxidation-reaction diagram as capstone centerpiece; map at least three evidence links back to Units I, II, and IV; feedback-test with an unfamiliar peer, checking specifically that the nuanced framing holds up under questioning.

Week 23 (Capstone Draft): integrate the fact-check kit, the real belief-shift investigation, the video, the oxidation-reaction diagram, and the media-literacy curriculum proposal into one “antioxidants and brain aging” draft.

Week 24 (Summit): exhibition displays the real before-and-after belief ratings beside the oxidation-reaction diagram; reflection defense leads with the honest, nuanced finding rather than either oversimplified extreme.

CROSS-PROJECT NOTE#

Every project above runs the same 24-week skeleton from the Operational Handbook — five tournaments, five labs, five main outputs building toward one capstone. Every one of Biology & Chemistry’s ten projects was built to the same depth throughout: a real biological or chemical mechanism stated explicitly (the dopamine reward pathway, blood-pH buffering, neuron energy vulnerability, chemical-messenger classification, glucose-response chemistry, gene-to-protein variation, reaction-rate kinetics, enzyme lock-and-key binding, the olfactory-to-memory pathway, oxidation-reduction chemistry), a real comparison or measurement the chapter’s own peer data produced, and an explicit statement of which earlier week’s evidence each later step depends on, from Week 1 through Week 24 — with the sensitive-topic projects (craving, medications, antioxidant marketing) carrying their disclaimers and ethical framing through every single week, not just their own unit.
