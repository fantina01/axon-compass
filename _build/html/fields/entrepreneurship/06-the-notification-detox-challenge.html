6. The Notification Detox Challenge#

A Real Program Testing Focus and Phone Habits

Field: Neuroscience × Entrepreneurship  ·  Project ID: entrepreneurship/06-the-notification-detox-challenge

Big Question

Can students run a real, short program helping classmates reduce phone notifications for a set period, and prove — with real data — that it improves focus or mood?

Why This Matters

Phone/notification habits are a universally relatable problem with real, testable science behind why constant notifications hurt focus — running a real challenge program teaches the same “build a small service, get real participants, measure real results” loop as any other pathway here, on a topic every student already cares about.

Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II#

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they’re what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

1. Ferster, C. B., & Skinner, B. F. (1957). Schedules of Reinforcement. Appleton-Century-Crofts.#

(Verified in the Policy field’s “Attention Economy and Democratic Deliberation” project.) Variable-ratio reinforcement — reward arriving after an unpredictable number of actions — produces the most persistent, habit-forming behavior of any reward schedule, which is the foundational behavioral-science mechanism behind why unpredictable notifications are specifically hard to disengage from.

2. Kushlev, K., Proulx, J., & Dunn, E. W. (2016). ‘Silence your phones’: Smartphone notifications increase inattention and hyperactivity symptoms. Proceedings of the CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems.#

This controlled study found that participants who kept notifications on reported significantly higher inattention and restlessness during a task than participants with notifications silenced — direct experimental evidence, not just correlation, that notifications specifically (not phone use in general) disrupt focus. This is useful, targeted evidence for the project’s specific intervention of disabling notifications rather than avoiding phones altogether.

3. Kushlev, K., & Dunn, E. W. (2019). Smartphones distract parents from cultivating feelings of connection when spending time with their children. Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 36(6), 1619-1639.#

This study extended notification-related distraction research into a real social context, finding notifications reduced people’s sense of connection during time with others. It’s a useful, broader piece of evidence for the project’s rationale — the benefit of reducing notifications isn’t only about individual focus, it may also extend to the quality of a person’s real-world social interactions during the challenge period.

Exact Steps, Unit by Unit#

Every step below plugs into the fixed season process described in The Universal Process — tournaments and laboratories run identically regardless of field. Only the content below is specific to this project.

Unit I — Learning Innovation Challenge#

Weeks 1–4 · Required artifact: Innovation Proposal I

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Run the Week 2 Confidence Gap tournament (see the Universal Process chapter).

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] Students design and run a real “Notification Detox” — participants turn off non-essential notifications for a set period (like a week) and check in with a simple daily rating. Innovation Proposal I documents the program rules and sign-up method.

Unit II — Research Investigation#

Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: Research Investigation / Prospectus

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 6 Prediction Ladder tournament + Week 7 Build-a-System laboratory.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] Literature review: basic science of how notifications interrupt attention, explained simply.

  • [ ] Hypothesis: participants will report better focus and less stress by the end of the week than at the start.

  • [ ] Variables: before-and-after self-ratings for the same real participants.

  • [ ] Methodology: real ratings collected before and after the challenge.

Unit III — Public Communication & Social Impact#

Weeks 9–12 · Required artifact: Communication Product + Dissemination Plan

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 10 Signal and the Noise tournament + Week 11 Message Transmission laboratory.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] A short recap post/video, “We Turned Off Notifications for a Week — Here’s What Really Happened,” using real participant results and quotes; shared to recruit the next round.

Unit IV — Memory/Decision Systems Simulation#

Weeks 13–16 · Required artifact: Simulation Model IV

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 14 Distortion Chain tournament + Week 15 Distortion & Reconstruction laboratory.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] A simple chart of every participant’s before/after ratings, shared openly.

Unit V — Policy Formation Simulation#

Weeks 17–20 · Required artifact: Policy Draft Document

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 18 Self-Forecast Duel tournament + Week 19 Identity & System Construction laboratory.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] A real pitch to a teacher or wellness club to make the challenge a regular school event, backed by the real results.

Unit VI — Integration & Summit#

Weeks 21–24 · Required artifact: Capstone Project

Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter): Week 22 Capstone Prototype Development laboratory, leading into the Week 24 Summit.

Steps specific to this project:

  • [ ] The Summit presentation is the actual case for running it school-wide, with real participants able to speak to what changed for them.

Skills This Project Builds#

  • Designing and running a real peer behavior-change program.

  • Collecting honest before/after participant data.

  • Recruiting real participants and sustaining engagement over a full week.

  • Pitching a real institution to adopt the program permanently.

  • Communicating real, sometimes imperfect results honestly.

Why This Project Impresses#

A real, run program with real willing participants and honest results — proof of the ability to organize people around an idea and show it worked.

How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio#

This project’s throughline is a real program with real willing participants, made more convincing at each stage. Unit I launches the actual challenge. Unit II measures real before-and-after focus and stress ratings. Unit III shares those real results, including honest quotes from real participants, to recruit the next round. Unit IV turns the results into an honest, visible chart. Unit V is a real pitch to a teacher or wellness club to run it school-wide. A mentor sees a student who organized real people around a real idea and can prove, with real data, that it worked.

Maximize Your Impact — What To Do With This After the Season#

  • Pitch the challenge to a teacher or wellness club as a recurring school event.

  • Submit the real participant results to a student wellness or digital-health innovation competition.

  • Share the challenge format publicly as a template other schools could run.
