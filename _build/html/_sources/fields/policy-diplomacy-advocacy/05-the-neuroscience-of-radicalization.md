```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can understanding the neural reward mechanisms underlying group belonging and identity fusion improve the design of counter-extremism messaging aimed at at-risk youth?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Extremist recruitment deliberately exploits social-reward and belonging circuitry; counter-messaging campaigns that ignore this mechanism (relying only on counter-argument) have shown limited effectiveness, making mechanism-informed message design a genuinely high-stakes, evidence-gap-filling project.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Atran, S. (2016). The devoted actor: Unconditional commitment and intractable conflict across cultures. Current Anthropology, 57(S13), S192-S203.

Atran's research on "sacred values" and identity fusion describes how some group commitments become resistant to ordinary cost-benefit reasoning once they're fused with personal identity, and how strong feelings of belonging and shared purpose — not just ideology — are central to what makes group commitment powerful, including in extremist contexts. This paper is useful for the project's core premise: it grounds "belonging" as a genuine, identity-level psychological mechanism, not just a vague motivational factor.

### 2. Whitehouse, H. (2018). Dying for the group: Towards a general theory of extreme self-sacrifice. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 41, e192.

Whitehouse's identity fusion theory proposes that when personal and group identity become psychologically merged, group members can become willing to sacrifice significant personal interests — including, in extreme cases, their own safety — for the group. This theoretical framework is useful for the project specifically because it treats extreme group commitment as arising from an identifiable psychological mechanism (identity fusion) that could, in principle, be addressed by offering alternative, healthier sources of the same fused belonging.

### 3. Horgan, J. (2008). From profiles to pathways and roots to routes: Perspectives from psychology on radicalization into terrorism. The ANNALS of the American Academy of Political and Social Science, 618(1), 80-94.

Horgan argues against simplistic "radicalization profile" thinking and instead frames radicalization as a process people move through step by step, often driven significantly by a search for belonging, purpose, and significance rather than ideological conviction alone — and that disengagement interventions are more effective when they address these underlying needs rather than only countering ideological claims. This is directly relevant to the project's core design choice: focusing counter-messaging on alternative belonging pathways, not only fact-correction.
