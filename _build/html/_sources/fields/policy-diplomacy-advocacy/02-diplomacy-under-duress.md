## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Arnsten, A. F. T. (2009). Stress signalling pathways that impair prefrontal cortex structure and function. Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 10(6), 410-422.

(See the fuller summary under the Economics field's "Stress Hormones and Bad Trades" project — this is the same source.) The key finding relevant here: acute stress chemically impairs prefrontal cortex function, the brain region most responsible for deliberate, careful reasoning, while strengthening more reflexive responses — directly explaining why high-stakes negotiations conducted under time pressure or threat may produce worse decisions than the same negotiators would make calmly.

### 2. Vecchi, G. M., Van Hasselt, V. B., & Romano, S. J. (2005). Crisis (hostage) negotiation: Current strategies and issues in high-risk conflict resolution. Aggression and Violent Behavior, 10(5), 533-551.

This paper describes the FBI's Behavioral Change Stairway Model, a structured crisis-negotiation framework built around active listening and de-escalation before any attempt at influencing behavior, explicitly designed around the recognition that a person in crisis cannot access rational decision-making until their emotional arousal is reduced first. This is real, professionally-developed negotiation doctrine (not a laboratory study) that already incorporates the Arnsten-style stress-and-reasoning relationship into practice, giving the project a genuine professional framework to build its own protocol from.

### 3. Beersma, B., & De Dreu, C. K. W. (2005). Conflict's consequences: Effects of social motives on postnegotiation creative and convergent group functioning and performance. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 89(3), 358-374.

This study found that negotiators' social motives (cooperative versus purely competitive) shaped both the quality of the negotiated outcome and the group's later functioning, and that these effects interacted with the pressure and structure of the negotiation process itself. It's included as a broader negotiation-psychology source connecting stress and process design specifically to negotiated-outcome quality, which is the actual dependent variable in the project's own tournament exercise.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

Every unit of this project builds the same case: that good diplomacy is trainable, not just innate talent under pressure. Unit I's pilot negotiation exercise shows real decision-quality decline under manufactured stress. Unit II turns that into a proper between-subjects comparison of protocol-trained versus untrained negotiators. Unit III explains that exact finding to the Model UN and debate communities who live this pressure every competition season. Unit IV visualizes the stress-reasoning curve the Unit II data actually traced, and shows precisely how the protocol is designed to interrupt it. Unit V's ask — that youth diplomacy programs formally teach stress-resilience — is aimed at the real governing organizations that run these programs. A mentor in international relations or debate coaching sees a student who didn't just compete in these formats, but studied and improved the underlying cognitive skill everyone else takes for granted.
