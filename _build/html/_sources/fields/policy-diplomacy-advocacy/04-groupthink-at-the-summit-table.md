```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Do the same neural conformity mechanisms that drive groupthink in small groups also shape diplomatic delegation decision-making, and can structured dissent protocols counteract this?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Historical foreign-policy disasters (the Bay of Pigs is the textbook case) are frequently attributed to groupthink; social-neuroscience findings on conformity (Berns' brain-imaging conformity studies) give a mechanistic account of *why* dissent gets suppressed in high-status group settings, directly informing better delegation design.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Janis, I. L. (1972). Victims of Groupthink: A Psychological Study of Foreign-Policy Decisions and Fiascoes. Houghton Mifflin.

Janis coined the term "groupthink" through case studies of major U.S. foreign policy failures (most famously the Bay of Pigs invasion), identifying a cluster of symptoms — including an illusion of unanimity, self-censorship of doubts, and pressure on dissenters to conform — that emerge in cohesive, high-status decision-making groups and lead to poor-quality decisions. This is the founding, canonical work behind the entire project, and it's specifically valuable because it's built from real historical case studies rather than laboratory simulations.

### 2. Berns, G. S., et al. (2005). Neurobiological correlates of social conformity and independence during mental rotation. Biological Psychiatry, 58(3), 245-253.

(See the fuller summary under the Economics field's "Social Proof and the Neural Herd" project — this is the same source.) The key finding relevant here: conforming to an incorrect group answer was associated with changes in perception-related brain activity, suggesting group pressure can genuinely alter what a person perceives to be true, not just what they're willing to say aloud — a mechanistic explanation for why Janis's groupthink dynamic can happen even among well-intentioned, capable decision-makers.

### 3. Esser, J. K. (1998). Alive and well after 25 years: A review of groupthink research. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 73(2-3), 116-141.

Esser's review assessed the accumulated evidence on groupthink 25 years after Janis's original theory, finding partial empirical support for some of Janis's specific predictions but also identifying that group cohesiveness alone is a weaker predictor of groupthink than Janis originally proposed — factors like directive leadership and insulation from outside opinions appear to matter more. This is a useful, honest corrective source: the project should present groupthink as a real, well-documented risk pattern, but not treat every one of Janis's original specific claims as fully confirmed by later research.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This portfolio takes one of history's most famous decision-making failures and turns it into something a student built, tested, and fixed themselves. Unit I's pilot group-decision exercise shows real conformity pressure emerging. Unit II's structured comparison between protocol and no-protocol groups produces a real, scored measure of decision quality. Unit III explains that finding to the Model UN and student-government communities who make group decisions constantly. Unit IV's conformity-shift model visualizes exactly how individual estimates drift toward a group norm, and how the dissent protocol interrupts that drift. Unit V's adoption recommendation goes straight to the governing bodies of the programs where this problem actually shows up. A mentor in political science or organizational psychology sees a student engaging directly with Janis's classic theory, not just quoting it — testing whether a real fix actually works.
