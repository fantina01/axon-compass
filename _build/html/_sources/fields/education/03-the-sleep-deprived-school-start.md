# 3. The Sleep-Deprived School Start

*Advocating for Circadian-Aligned School Schedules*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Education &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `education/03-the-sleep-deprived-school-start`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Does aligning school start times with adolescent circadian biology improve academic performance and mental health enough to justify the logistical cost of changing them?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note

```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Three real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Carskadon, M. A. (2011). Sleep in adolescents: The perfect storm. Pediatric Clinics of North America, 58(3), 637–647.

Carskadon's research program documented a real, biological shift in adolescents' circadian rhythm during puberty: the body's internal clock delays, so melatonin (the hormone that signals it's time to sleep) is released later in the evening than it was in childhood. Combined with early school start times that don't move to match this shift, most teenagers are chronically sleep-restricted during the school year — not because of poor discipline, but because of a mismatch between biology and schedule. This paper is the core scientific justification for the project's central claim: that school start time is a biological issue, not just a lifestyle one.

### 2. Wahlstrom, K. (2002). Changing times: Findings from the first longitudinal study of later high school start times. NASSP Bulletin, 86(633), 3–21.

Wahlstrom's research tracked real school districts that delayed their start times and measured the before/after effects on students. Districts that moved start times later saw increases in average reported sleep duration, along with improvements in attendance and reductions in students falling asleep in class — real-world evidence, not just laboratory data, that the biological mismatch documented by Carskadon translates into measurable school outcomes when it's addressed. This is the practical, policy-relevant evidence the project's school-board pitch leans on.

### 3. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2015). Schools Start Too Early. CDC Vital Signs / MMWR Weekly Report.

The CDC's Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report compiled national data showing fewer than 1 in 5 U.S. middle and high schools started at the 8:30 a.m. or later time recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics, and summarized the public-health case linking insufficient adolescent sleep to worse academic performance, mood problems, and elevated risk of car accidents among teen drivers. As a government public-health report rather than a single study, this source is useful specifically for establishing that the school-start-time problem is not confined to one district — it's a documented national pattern the project's local pitch can situate itself within.

## Full Project Toolkit

**Quick facts:** Team size 2–4 · ~2–3 hrs/week outside session · Cost: $0 · Needs: access to the school's actual bell schedule.

**Ethics flag:** Low-risk survey of sleep habits — anonymize all responses, and avoid asking about anything beyond sleep timing (no need to ask why someone sleeps poorly).

**Materials by unit:** Unit I — a copy of the current bell schedule, a simple anonymous sleep survey (paper or Google Form). Unit II — the same survey plus a short in-class attention task (a 2-minute letter-cancellation sheet works well and needs no equipment). Unit III — video/audio recording setup. Unit IV — spreadsheet for the two-process model.

**Ready-to-use data template (Unit II):** Participant ID · Typical Bedtime · Typical Wake Time · Total Sleep (auto-calculated) · Attention Task Score · Self-Rated Alertness (1–5).

**Worked example — the hardest step (the two-process sleep model, Unit IV):** two simple curves added together — a "sleep pressure" line that rises steadily through the day, and a "circadian alertness" wave that dips in the early afternoon and evening. Plot both on the same chart with time-of-day on the x-axis; where the sleep-pressure line is high and the circadian line is low is where alertness is worst — that intersection is the whole point of the model.

**Common pitfalls:** teams sometimes conflate "tired" with "sleepy" in survey wording — be specific ("how sleepy do you feel right now" is different from "how tired are you of school").

**Elevator pitch:** "We measured how our own school's early start time affects real students' sleep and attention, and built a model showing exactly why later start times are backed by real biology, not just preference."

**Low-resource adaptation:** the survey runs on paper slips collected in a box; the attention task is a simple printed letter-crossing-out sheet, hand-scored.
