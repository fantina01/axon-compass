# 6. DNA and Individual Differences

*Genetics Basics and Why Brains Aren't All Wired the Same*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Biology & Chemistry &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `biology-chemistry/06-dna-and-individual-differences`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Using basic genetics concepts already taught in biology class (genes, variation, inherited traits), can students explain why people naturally differ in things like sensitivity to caffeine or need for sleep?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Genetic variation is usually taught through visible traits like eye color, but the same basic principles (gene variants leading to different protein function) explain real differences in brain-related traits, connecting standard genetics lessons to something personally relevant.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Cornelis, M. C., El-Sohemy, A., Kabagambe, E. K., & Campos, H. (2006). Coffee, CYP1A2 genotype, and risk of myocardial infarction. JAMA, 295(10), 1135-1141.

This study identified that variants in the CYP1A2 gene affect how quickly a person's liver metabolizes caffeine — some genetic variants process caffeine much faster than others — and that this genetic difference in turn shapes health outcomes related to coffee consumption. This is the direct genetic evidence behind the project's claim that individual differences in caffeine sensitivity have a real, identifiable genetic basis, not just a matter of habit or tolerance.

### 2. He, Y., Jones, C. R., Fujiki, N., et al. (2009). The transcriptional repressor DEC2 regulates sleep length in mammals. Science, 325(5942), 866-870.

He and colleagues identified a specific gene mutation (in DEC2) associated with people who naturally need less sleep than average, and confirmed the effect by introducing the same mutation into mice, which also showed reduced sleep needs. This is a striking, well-verified example of a specific gene variant directly shaping a brain-related trait — sleep need — precisely the kind of concrete genetics-and-brain-trait link the project's explainer is built to teach.

### 3. Cornelis, M. C. (2019). The impact of caffeine and coffee on human health. Nutrients, 11(2), 416.

This more recent review updates the caffeine-genetics research, discussing how genetic variation in caffeine metabolism (building on the CYP1A2 findings above) interacts with individual differences in caffeine's effects on sleep, anxiety, and alertness. It's a useful, more current companion source for grounding the project's explainer in up-to-date genetics research rather than relying only on the original, older studies.

## Why This Project Impresses

Uses core, standard genetics vocabulary and concepts to explain something personally relatable, without needing any genetic testing or advanced molecular biology.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

Every unit of this project deepens the same idea: "everyone's different" has a real genetic mechanism, not just a vague truism. Unit I builds the first explainer using real gene-variant examples. Unit II tests whether the genetics-based explanation improves understanding compared to a generic statement. Unit III explains that same mechanism to an audience who's never realized their own caffeine tolerance might be written in their DNA. Unit IV diagrams the actual gene-to-protein pathway. Unit V asks biology curricula to teach genetics through brain-related traits, not just eye color. A biology mentor sees standard genetics vocabulary turned into something genuinely personal and memorable.
