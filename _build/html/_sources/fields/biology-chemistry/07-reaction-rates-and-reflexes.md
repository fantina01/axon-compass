# 7. Reaction Rates and Reflexes

*Basic Chemical Kinetics and Nerve Signal Speed*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Biology & Chemistry &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `biology-chemistry/07-reaction-rates-and-reflexes`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Using the basic chemistry idea that reactions happen faster or slower depending on conditions (like temperature), can students explain why nerve signals and reflexes can be affected by things like body temperature or fatigue?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Chemical reaction rate concepts (a standard chemistry-class topic — reactions generally speed up with higher temperature) apply directly to the chemical reactions underlying nerve signaling, giving a concrete, testable link between an abstract chemistry principle and something as tangible as reflex speed.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Hodgkin, A. L., & Katz, B. (1949). The effect of temperature on the electrical activity of the giant axon of the squid. Journal of Physiology, 109(1-2), 240-249.

Hodgkin and Katz measured how nerve conduction speed and the shape of the nerve impulse itself changed with temperature, finding that nerve signaling slowed measurably as temperature decreased — an early, direct physiological demonstration that basic chemical reaction rate principles (reactions generally slow at lower temperatures) apply directly to nerve function. This is the foundational neuroscience evidence behind the project's core analogy between chemistry-class reaction rates and reflex speed.

### 2. Ranatunga, K. W. (1998). Temperature-dependence of mechanical power output in mammalian (rat) skeletal muscle. Experimental Physiology, 83(3), 371-376.

This study specifically measured how muscle contraction speed and power output change with temperature in mammalian muscle, complementing the nerve-conduction evidence by showing the muscular side of a reflex (not just the neural signal) is also temperature-sensitive. This is useful for the project because a real "cold hands, slow reflex" measurement involves both the nerve signal and the muscle response, and both are affected by temperature.

### 3. Van't Hoff, J. H. (1884). Etudes de Dynamique Chimique. Frederik Muller & Co.

Van't Hoff's foundational work in chemical kinetics established the general principle (later refined into the "Q10" concept) that chemical reaction rates increase predictably with temperature — the basic chemistry principle this entire project applies to a biological system. It's included as the original chemistry-side source underlying the whole analogy, alongside the specific nerve-conduction physiology evidence above.

*Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: **Research Investigation / Prospectus***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 6 **Prediction Ladder** tournament + Week 7 **Build-a-System** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Literature review:** basic chemical reaction rate concepts (temperature's effect on reaction speed), plain-language summaries of how nerve signal chemistry can be affected by temperature at the body's surface and extremities.
- [ ] **Hypothesis:** peers will show slightly slower reaction times immediately after the cooling condition compared to the warming condition.

- [ ] **Methodology:** within-person comparison, each peer tested under both conditions on different occasions, using a simple repeatable reaction-time test, with clear safety limits on temperature exposure.
