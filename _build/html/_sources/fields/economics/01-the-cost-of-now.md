# 1. The Cost of Now

*Modeling Temporal Discounting in Teen Financial Decisions*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Economics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `economics/01-the-cost-of-now`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can teaching adolescents about neural temporal discounting (the brain's bias toward smaller-sooner rewards over larger-later ones) improve their real financial decision-making?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Adolescents show steeper discount rates than adults due to still-maturing prefrontal control over limbic reward circuits, which partly explains high rates of impulsive spending and low savings behavior among teens — a neuroscience finding with direct, testable financial-education implications.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. McClure, S. M., Laibson, D. I., Loewenstein, G., & Cohen, J. D. (2004). Separate neural systems value immediate and delayed monetary rewards. Science, 306(5695), 503–507.

Using brain imaging, McClure and colleagues found that choices involving an immediately available reward activate limbic, dopamine-linked brain regions more strongly than choices about delayed rewards, while a separate set of prefrontal and parietal regions is engaged consistently regardless of delay. This supports a "dual-system" view of decision-making, where impulsive, present-focused valuation and patient, deliberate valuation are handled by genuinely different neural systems rather than a single unified process — evidence the project draws on directly to explain why immediate rewards can feel disproportionately compelling.

### 2. Laibson, D. (1997). Golden eggs and hyperbolic discounting. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 112(2), 443–477.

Laibson formalized "hyperbolic discounting" — the observation that people don't discount future rewards at a constant rate (as classical economic models assumed) but instead show a steep drop in value for anything not immediate, followed by a much flatter discount rate further out. This explains a common real pattern: someone might genuinely prefer a larger reward next month over a smaller one today, but if both are pushed forward a month, they'll switch to preferring the smaller-but-sooner option once "today" arrives. This is the mathematical foundation for why commitment devices (like automatic savings transfers) work — they remove the moment where hyperbolic discounting would otherwise flip a decision.

### 3. Kirby, K. N., & Maraković, N. N. (1996). Delay-discounting probabilistic rewards: Rates decrease as amounts increase. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 3(1), 100–104.

Kirby and Maraković found that the rate at which people discount a future reward isn't fixed — it depends on the size of the reward itself; people discount small rewards more steeply than large ones. This matters for the project because it means financial literacy interventions can't use a single "one-size-fits-all" discount-rate lesson — the same person may behave very impulsively about a $5 decision while being comparatively patient about a $500 one, which shapes how the module should frame realistic savings scenarios.

### Unit II — Research Investigation
*Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: **Research Investigation / Prospectus***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 6 **Prediction Ladder** tournament + Week 7 **Build-a-System** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Literature review focus:** hyperbolic discounting models (Laibson), neural correlates of discounting (McClure et al.'s beta-delta model), adolescent prefrontal development.
- [ ] **Hypothesis:** students exposed to the module show reduced discount rates (measured via choice tasks) and improved intended savings behavior compared to a control group receiving standard financial literacy content.
- [ ] **Variables:** module type (discounting-aware vs. standard) as IV; discount rate from choice task and self-reported savings intention as DV.
- [ ] **Methodology:** matched-group pre/post design across two classes.
- [ ] **Investigation blueprint:** full choice-task instrument design, scoring method, and administration protocol.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This project is not five separate assignments about "money and the brain" — it is one argument, built brick by brick, that gets stronger every unit. It starts as a hunch in Unit I: a peer survey showing classmates make worse choices about money "later" than money "now." By Unit II, that hunch has become a real, falsifiable claim, tested with an actual before/after study comparing two groups of real students. Unit III takes that exact tested claim — not a watered-down version of it — and puts it in front of an audience that actually needs to hear it: parents and peers who are skeptical that "just teach kids to wait" ever works. Unit IV is where the project stops being an argument and becomes a tool: the same discounting curve from the literature is now something anyone can touch, drag, and see change in real time. And Unit V is the moment the whole thing stops being a school project and becomes a request a real institution could actually act on — a specific ask to adopt this exact module into a real curriculum standard, backed by the Unit II data and the Unit IV model as evidence. By the Summit, a reader isn't looking at five things. They're looking at one idea that has been tested, explained, modeled, and proposed as policy — the exact arc a real research-to-policy pipeline follows, compressed into one season, done by a high schooler.
