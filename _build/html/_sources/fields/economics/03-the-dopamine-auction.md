# 3. The Dopamine Auction

*Modeling Reward Prediction Error in Consumer Bidding Behavior*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Economics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `economics/03-the-dopamine-auction`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Does the brain's reward-prediction-error signal (as described in dopaminergic learning theory) explain why people overbid in auction settings, and can awareness of this reduce overbidding?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Reward-prediction-error models (originally from animal learning, later linked to dopamine signaling) have been extended to explain market anomalies like auction overbidding — connecting a core neuroscience mechanism directly to a real economic behavior with financial consequences.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Schultz, W., Dayan, P., & Montague, P. R. (1997). A neural substrate of prediction and reward. Science, 275(5306), 1593–1599.

This landmark paper established that dopamine neurons don't simply fire in response to reward itself — they fire in response to the difference between an expected reward and the actual outcome, a signal called "reward prediction error." When an outcome is better than expected, dopamine neurons fire more; when it's worse than expected, firing dips below baseline. This is the foundational finding behind the project's core mechanism: the excitement of a live auction comes partly from repeated, escalating prediction-error signals as each new bid changes what outcome seems likely.

### 2. Thaler, R. H. (1988). Anomalies: The winner's curse. Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2(1), 191–202.

Thaler's paper describes the "winner's curse": in auctions with many bidders estimating an uncertain value, the winning bid is disproportionately likely to come from whoever overestimated the value most, meaning winners systematically tend to overpay relative to the item's true worth. This is the classic economic description of the exact real-world pattern (auction overbidding) the project is trying to explain neurally — pairing this with the Schultz et al. mechanism connects a well-documented economic anomaly to a plausible brain-level explanation.

### 3. Delgado, M. R. (2007). Reward-related responses in the human striatum. Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences, 1104(1), 70–88.

Delgado's review summarizes human neuroimaging evidence that the ventral striatum — a key reward-processing region — responds not just to receiving a reward but anticipating one, and that this anticipatory response can be heightened by uncertainty and competition, both hallmarks of a live auction. This source extends the core reward-prediction-error mechanism specifically into human imaging studies (rather than only the animal studies underlying the original Schultz et al. work), which is useful given the project involves human participants.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

What makes this portfolio compelling is that every unit deepens the reader's understanding of the exact same moment: the second right before you raise your hand at an auction. Unit I catches that moment in a mock auction and shows real overbidding. Unit II explains why, with a controlled comparison between informed and uninformed bidders. Unit III turns that explanation into a story anyone can follow, aimed at people who've felt that same rush in a bidding war online. Unit IV lets a visitor actually watch a simulated prediction-error signal spike with each new bid — the same mechanism from the Unit II hypothesis, now visible. Unit V takes the whole arc and turns it into a real ask: that consumer-protection education include this exact lesson. A mentor doesn't see "a project about auctions" — they see a single, sharpening argument about how excitement overrides rational judgment, proven, modeled, and pitched for real-world use.
