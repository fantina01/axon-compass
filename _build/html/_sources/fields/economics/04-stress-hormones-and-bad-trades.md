# 4. Stress Hormones and Bad Trades

*Cortisol, Risk Tolerance, and Market Behavior*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Economics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `economics/04-stress-hormones-and-bad-trades`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Does acute stress (via cortisol) shift risk tolerance in ways that could explain irrational financial decisions, and can stress-awareness training improve decision quality under pressure?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Research linking cortisol to shifts in risk preference offers a physiological explanation for panic-selling and other costly financial behaviors during high-stress periods, with direct relevance to both personal finance and market stability.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Coates, J. M., & Herbert, J. (2008). Endogenous steroids and financial risk taking on a London trading floor. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 105(16), 6167–6170.

Coates and Herbert measured cortisol and testosterone levels in real professional traders on a London trading floor across two weeks, and found that a trader's cortisol level was significantly higher on days with greater market volatility, and correlated with how much risk that trader subsequently took. This is a rare example of a hormone study conducted in a genuine high-stakes financial workplace rather than a lab, and it's the direct empirical basis for the project's claim that market stress and physiological stress hormones are measurably linked in real trading behavior.

### 2. Arnsten, A. F. T. (2009). Stress signalling pathways that impair prefrontal cortex structure and function. Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 10(6), 410–422.

Arnsten's review explains the neurochemistry behind why acute stress impairs prefrontal cortex function specifically — the brain region most responsible for weighing options and controlling impulses — while simultaneously strengthening more reflexive, habit-driven brain circuits. This gives the project a mechanistic explanation for *why* elevated cortisol would shift someone toward more impulsive, less deliberate financial decisions, connecting Coates and Herbert's correlational field finding to an underlying causal pathway.

### 3. Kandasamy, N., Hardy, B., Page, L., et al. (2014). Cortisol shapes financial risk preferences: Evidence from Cambridge trading room. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 111(9), 3608–3613.

This study went a step further than Coates and Herbert by experimentally administering cortisol to participants in a simulated trading environment (rather than only observing naturally occurring hormone levels), and found that raising cortisol caused participants to become more risk-averse over several days — a more nuanced finding than "stress simply causes reckless risk-taking." This is a useful, more rigorous companion source, and it appropriately complicates the project's hypothesis: the real relationship between stress hormones and risk-taking may depend on timing and stress duration, not just stress presence.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

The power of this portfolio is that Unit IV and Unit V couldn't exist without Unit II, and Unit II couldn't have been designed without Unit I's pilot observation — nothing here is decorative. The pilot game in Unit I notices something real: people take worse risks under time pressure. Unit II turns that into an actual within-subject experiment with real peer data. Unit III explains that same finding to an audience who lives it — young people already anxious about their own money decisions. Unit IV's stress-risk curve isn't a generic diagram; it's built directly from the Unit II results, so a visitor can see the actual shape of the effect the student found. Unit V's policy ask — that investor-education programs teach stress-awareness — is backed by that same specific data chain, start to finish. This is what makes it portfolio-worthy: every later artifact makes a specific, visible reference back to what came before it, so the whole season reads as one continuously strengthening case, not six disconnected deliverables.
