# 7. Sleep and Grades, Plotted

*Reading a Scatter Graph for Real Patterns*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Mathematics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `mathematics/07-sleep-and-grades-plotted`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Using a simple scatter graph (no formulas, just dots on a grid), can students see whether more sleep really is connected to better grades among their own peers?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
A scatter graph is one of the simplest tools for spotting whether two things tend to go together, and applying it to sleep and grades — a question every student cares about — makes an ordinary math-class skill feel immediately meaningful.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Curcio, G., Ferrara, M., & De Gennaro, L. (2006). Sleep loss, learning capacity and academic performance. Sleep Medicine Reviews, 10(5), 323-337.

This review specifically compiled evidence linking sleep duration and quality to academic performance in students, finding a consistent association between shorter or poorer sleep and worse grades or standardized test scores across many studies, while also noting that the relationship is correlational in most of the reviewed research, not necessarily strictly causal in every study design. This is the direct, subject-matched evidence behind the project's central scatter-graph hypothesis.

### 2. Wolfson, A. R., & Carskadon, M. A. (1998). Sleep schedules and daytime functioning in adolescents. Child Development, 69(4), 875-887.

This large study of adolescents found that students who reported better grades also reported more total sleep and more consistent sleep schedules than students with lower grades, even after considering school-night versus weekend differences. It's useful specifically because it's an adolescent-focused (not general adult) study, closely matching the project's own student population.

### 3. Dewald, J. F., Meijer, A. M., Oort, F. J., Kerkhof, G. A., & Bögels, S. M. (2010). The influence of sleep quality, sleep duration and sleepiness on school performance in children and adolescents: A meta-analysis. Sleep Medicine Reviews, 14(3), 179-189.

This meta-analysis pooled many studies and found that sleep quality and daytime sleepiness were actually somewhat stronger predictors of school performance than raw sleep duration alone — meaning two students who report the same number of sleep hours might still perform differently if their sleep quality differs. This is a useful, honest nuance for the project: a scatter graph plotting only sleep hours (not quality) is likely to show real but imperfect scatter, for a genuine, explainable reason.

*Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: **Research Investigation / Prospectus***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 6 **Prediction Ladder** tournament + Week 7 **Build-a-System** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Hypothesis:** when plotted on a scatter graph (sleep hours on one axis, grade average on the other), the dots will tend to trend upward — more sleep generally paired with higher grades — even without a perfect, exact relationship.
- [ ] **Variables:** sleep hours as one axis; grade average as the other axis.
- [ ] **Methodology:** collect the survey data, plot every peer as one dot on the scatter graph, and describe the overall pattern in the dots (trending up, trending down, or no clear pattern) without requiring an exact statistical formula.

## Skills This Project Builds


## Why This Project Impresses

Uses the simplest possible data-visualization tool (dots on a grid) to explore a real, relatable question — showing that basic graphing skills are genuinely powerful research tools.

---

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

Every unit of this project builds the same scatter graph, made more real and more carefully explained at each stage. Unit I collects the first real sleep-and-grades data pairs. Unit II turns it into an actual plotted pattern examined honestly for its overall trend. Unit III explains that real pattern to an audience of students and parents who care deeply about this exact question. Unit IV builds a tool so the scatter graph updates live as more data comes in. Unit V asks health class to teach graphing through this exact personally relevant example. A math or health teacher sees a student who used the simplest possible data-visualization tool — dots on a grid — to explore a real, important question about their own peer group, and who understood that a scattered pattern with real variation is itself an honest, meaningful scientific result.
