# 8. Slicing the Brain

*Basic Geometry and Brain Region Proportions*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Mathematics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `mathematics/08-slicing-the-brain`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Using simple area and percentage math (like a pie chart), can students accurately show how much of the brain's space is used for different jobs, like movement, vision, or memory?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
People often picture the brain as doing one thing at a time in one spot, but comparing the actual proportion of space different functions take up — using nothing more than basic area and percentage calculations — gives a much more accurate, visual picture of the brain's real "floor plan."
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Penfield, W., & Rasmussen, T. (1950). The Cerebral Cortex of Man: A Clinical Study of Localization of Function. Macmillan.

Penfield and Rasmussen's classic work, based on direct brain stimulation during surgery on awake patients, mapped which regions of the brain's surface corresponded to sensation and movement in different body parts — producing the famous distorted "homunculus" diagram where body parts are drawn in proportion to how much cortical space they occupy, not their actual physical size. While this project focuses on functional regions more broadly (vision, movement, memory) rather than the detailed homunculus map specifically, this is the foundational source establishing that cortical space allocation is proportionally very different from body or task proportions — the core surprising fact behind the whole project.

### 2. Van Essen, D. C. (2005). Corticocortical and thalamocortical information flow in the primate visual system. Progress in Brain Research, 149, 173-185.

Van Essen's research is part of a body of work establishing that the visual system alone occupies an unusually large proportion of the primate cortex — a substantial fraction of the entire brain's surface is devoted to processing vision, more than most people intuitively expect. This is a useful, specific data point supporting the project's likely finding that peers underestimate how much brain space vision actually occupies compared to other functions.

### 3. Felleman, D. J., & Van Essen, D. C. (1991). Distributed hierarchical processing in the primate cerebral cortex. Cerebral Cortex, 1(1), 1-47.

This influential paper mapped the network of interconnected visual processing areas in the primate cortex, providing detailed anatomical evidence for just how many distinct cortical regions are devoted to different aspects of visual processing (motion, color, depth, and more), reinforcing that "vision" alone is not one small area but a large, distributed system. This is a good source for the project to cite when explaining why the pie-chart proportion for vision tends to surprise people who expect it to be small.

## Why This Project Impresses

Connects a basic, required geometry skill (turning percentages into pie-chart angles) to a genuinely interesting, corrective picture of how the brain is organized.

---

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This project turns a percentage-to-angle geometry skill into a genuinely surprising discovery about the brain's own "floor plan." Unit I calculates and draws the first pie chart. Unit II turns it into a real guess-versus-reality comparison with peers. Unit III explains that exact surprising comparison to an audience who's never thought about brain space in proportional terms. Unit IV builds a tool connecting any set of percentages directly to a properly scaled pie chart. Unit V asks math and biology teachers to teach percentage-to-angle conversion through this exact example. A geometry or biology teacher sees a student who used a skill everyone learns — converting percentages into pie-chart angles — to correct a real, common misconception about how the brain allocates its own space.
