# 10. Symmetry and the Face

*Basic Geometry and Attractiveness Perception*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Mathematics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `mathematics/10-symmetry-and-the-face`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Using simple measurements of symmetry (comparing the left and right sides of a shape), can students test the popular claim that facial symmetry is linked to how attractive a face is judged to be?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
"Symmetric faces are more attractive" is a widely repeated claim, and testing it only requires basic measuring and comparison — the same skill used to check if a shape is symmetric in a geometry class — applied to real (with consent, and using simple outlines/silhouettes rather than real photos, to keep things respectful) face-shape comparisons.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Grammer, K., & Thornhill, R. (1994). Human (Homo sapiens) facial attractiveness and sexual selection: The role of symmetry and averageness. Journal of Comparative Psychology, 108(3), 233-242.

This influential study found that faces rated as more symmetrical were also rated as more attractive on average, and proposed this preference might be linked to symmetry serving as a signal of underlying health or developmental stability. This is the foundational, most widely-cited source behind the popular claim this project sets out to test — essential reading, but the project should also read the more recent, more nuanced findings below before drawing its own conclusions.

### 2. Rhodes, G., Yoshikawa, S., Palermo, R., et al. (2007). Perceived health contributes to the attractiveness of facial symmetry, averageness, and sexual dimorphism. Perception, 36(8), 1244-1252.

This later, carefully controlled study found something important the original Grammer and Thornhill work couldn't fully separate out: when researchers statistically controlled for how healthy a face merely appeared to look, most of the apparent connection between symmetry and attractiveness disappeared. In other words, symmetry may be attractive mainly because it makes a face look healthier, not because symmetry itself is inherently appealing. This is a crucial, honest correction the project should include — it changes what the "symmetry is attractive" finding actually means.

### 3. Zaidel, D. W., Chen, A. C., & German, C. (1995). She is not a beauty even when she smiles: Possible evolutionary basis for a relationship between facial attractiveness and hemispheric specialization. Neuropsychologia, 33(5), 649-655.

This study is included specifically as a useful complicating counterpoint: it found that real human faces are naturally, meaningfully asymmetric (not perfectly symmetric), and that the relationship between symmetry and attractiveness in real, natural face photographs is weaker and less consistent than in studies using computer-manipulated, artificially symmetrized faces. This is important for the project's methodology section: the strength of the symmetry-attractiveness link may partly depend on how the symmetry is created and measured, not just how symmetric a face actually is.

### Unit II — Research Investigation
*Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: **Research Investigation / Prospectus***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 6 **Prediction Ladder** tournament + Week 7 **Build-a-System** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Literature review:** plain-language summaries of research on symmetry and attractiveness judgments.
- [ ] **Hypothesis:** peers will rate more symmetric outline versions as more attractive on average than less symmetric versions of the same basic shape.
- [ ] **Variables:** degree of symmetry (measured simply, like the size of the gap between matching left/right measurements) as the thing being compared; average attractiveness rating as the outcome.
- [ ] **Methodology:** show peers a range of outlines with different, measured amounts of symmetry, collect ratings, and compare average ratings across symmetry levels.

## Why This Project Impresses

Tackles a popular claim with genuine, basic geometric measurement while showing real thoughtfulness about the sensitive nature of the topic — a mature, careful use of simple math.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This project handles a sensitive topic with real care at every single stage, and that consistency is exactly what makes the portfolio impressive. Unit I designs respectful, abstract outline shapes rather than real photos. Unit II turns it into a genuine, carefully measured symmetry-versus-rating comparison. Unit III explains the finding thoughtfully, including the honest complication that later research suggests symmetry itself may not be the real cause. Unit IV builds a tool showing exactly how symmetry is measured, kept deliberately abstract. Unit V recommends the activity only ever be used with abstract shapes, not real photos of real people, protecting against any appearance-based harm. A geometry or psychology teacher sees a student who used basic measurement to responsibly investigate a genuinely popular claim, while showing more caution and nuance about the sensitive subject than most adult treatments of the same topic.
