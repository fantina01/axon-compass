# 5. Resonance and the Startle Reflex

*Damped Oscillator Models of Sensory Habituation*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Physics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `physics/05-resonance-and-the-startle-reflex`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can the physics of damped oscillators (a mass-spring system that settles down after being disturbed) model how the human startle reflex diminishes with repeated identical stimuli?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Habituation — the shrinking response to a repeated stimulus — is a foundational, easily observable neuroscience phenomenon, and the shape of its decline closely resembles the amplitude decay of a damped oscillator, a standard intro-physics system, giving students a genuine (if simplified) mathematical lens on a real behavioral pattern.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Thompson, R. F., & Spencer, W. A. (1966). Habituation: A model phenomenon for the study of neuronal substrates of behavior. Psychological Review, 73(1), 16-43.

Thompson and Spencer's classic paper laid out the defining behavioral characteristics of habituation — including that response decrease is typically negatively accelerated (large drops early, smaller drops later) — establishing the general shape of habituation curves across many species and stimulus types. This is the foundational behavioral-science source for the project's central claim that the startle response follows a predictable, characteristically-shaped decline, which is what makes fitting it to a damped-oscillator-style curve a meaningful comparison rather than an arbitrary one.

### 2. Groves, P. M., & Thompson, R. F. (1970). Habituation: A dual-process theory. Psychological Review, 77(5), 419-450.

Groves and Thompson proposed that habituation isn't a single simple process but the net result of two competing processes — a genuine habituation (decreasing) process and a separate sensitization (increasing) process — whose balance determines the overall response curve shape. This is a useful, more nuanced addition for the project: the real startle-decline curve may not always look like a single clean exponential decay, and the two-process theory explains why some real habituation curves show more complex shapes than a pure damped oscillator would predict.

### 3. Blumenthal, T. D., et al. (2005). Committee report: Guidelines for human startle eyeblink electromyographic studies. Psychophysiology, 42(1), 1-15.

This methodology committee report establishes standardized, ethical, and technically sound procedures for measuring human startle responses (typically via eyeblink) in research settings, including appropriate stimulus intensities and safety considerations. It's included specifically as a practical methods reference to ensure the project's own startle-testing protocol follows established, safe, peer-reviewed research practices rather than improvising an unvalidated method.

## Why This Project Impresses

A genuinely elegant, testable analogy between a classic physics system and a classic neuroscience phenomenon, executed with real (if simple) curve-fitting — appropriately challenging without needing anything beyond intro-level oscillator math.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This project's throughline is the shape of a curve — and every unit makes that shape more precise and more real. Unit I designs an ethical, careful startle-response test. Unit II turns the resulting data into an actual curve-fitting exercise, comparing real habituation data against a genuine damped-oscillator model. Unit III explains that same fitted curve to an audience who has felt this exact phenomenon every time a second jump scare lands softer than the first. Unit IV lets a visitor adjust a damping coefficient and watch predicted decay match (or not match) real collected data. Unit V asks for this exact comparison to become a standard cross-listed physics/psychology lab. A mentor sees a student who took a formula usually confined to mechanical systems and used it to rigorously describe something happening inside a nervous system — an unusually elegant, testable analogy, executed with real data rather than just asserted.
