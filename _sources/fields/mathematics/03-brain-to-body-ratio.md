# 3. Brain-to-Body Ratio

*What Simple Ratios Reveal About Intelligence Myths*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Mathematics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `mathematics/03-brain-to-body-ratio`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Is it true that a bigger brain (relative to body size) means a smarter animal — and can simple ratios test this popular claim?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
People often assume "bigger brain = smarter," but the real comparison scientists use is a ratio (brain weight divided by body weight), and working through this ratio with real animal data is a clean way to test — and often correct — a popular myth using math anyone can do.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Roth, G., & Dicke, U. (2005). Evolution of the brain and intelligence. Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 9(5), 250-257.

Roth and Dicke's review directly examines whether brain size, relative brain size, or brain-to-body ratio actually predicts intelligence across species, and finds that none of these simple size measures correlate as well with intelligence as more detailed factors like the number of cortical neurons and how fast signals travel between them. Strikingly, they note that humans have only marginally more cortical neurons than whales and elephants despite being vastly more intelligent by any behavioral measure — which is exactly the kind of myth-complicating nuance this project should include, not just the simpler "ratio beats raw size" version.

### 2. Jerison, H. J. (1973). Evolution of the Brain and Intelligence. Academic Press.

Jerison's foundational work introduced the encephalization quotient (EQ) — a brain-to-body size ratio adjusted for the expected relationship between the two across species — as an improvement over raw brain-to-body ratio for comparing species. This is the historical origin of using an adjusted ratio (rather than a simple raw ratio) to compare brain size across very differently-sized animals, and it's useful context for the project since even Jerison's more sophisticated EQ measure has since been shown to be an imperfect predictor of intelligence (per Roth & Dicke above).

### 3. Herculano-Houzel, S. (2009). The human brain in numbers: A linearly scaled-up primate brain. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 3, 31.

Herculano-Houzel developed a method to directly count neurons in brains of different sizes (rather than estimating from volume), finding that the human brain actually follows the same basic scaling rules as other primate brains — it isn't special in its ratio of neurons to size, but humans simply have the most cortical neurons of any primate because primate brains scale unusually efficiently compared to other mammal groups. This adds an important, precise correction for the project: the real story isn't a simple "ratio," it's about neuron-packing efficiency, which varies by animal group in ways a simple ratio can't capture.

## Why This Project Impresses

A simple, satisfying use of ratios and proportional reasoning — a required math skill — to correct a real, widely-believed myth using nothing but division.

---

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This project's power is that it takes a simple division problem and uses it to correct a real, widely-believed myth — and does it more honestly than most popular science. Unit I calculates the first ratio comparison. Unit II turns it into a genuine ranking study, showing raw-size rankings and ratio rankings don't match. Unit III explains that mismatch to an audience who assumed "bigger brain equals smarter" was obviously true. Unit IV builds a calculator so anyone can check any animal's ratio for themselves. Unit V asks biology and math teachers to teach proportional reasoning through this exact myth-correcting example. A science teacher sees a student who used nothing more than division to responsibly untangle a genuinely confusing area of popular science — and who was careful enough to note that even the ratio itself isn't the whole story, which is a mark of real intellectual honesty.
