# 1. Percent Better

*Measuring How Much Study Techniques Actually Help*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Mathematics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `mathematics/01-percent-better`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Using nothing more than percentages, can students prove which study method actually improves memory the most?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Students are constantly told "flashcards work" or "re-reading doesn't," but almost nobody actually measures it. Percent improvement is simple enough for anyone to calculate, yet powerful enough to settle real arguments about what study methods work.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. Psychological Science, 17(3), 249-255.

(Verified in the Education field's "Testing as Teaching" project.) Students who quizzed themselves on material outperformed students who reread it, on a test given a week later — the direct evidence that retrieval practice, not just re-exposure, drives the percent-improvement gap this project is designed to measure and prove with real numbers.

### 2. Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L. (2008). The critical importance of retrieval for learning. Science, 319(5865), 966-968.

This follow-up study specifically separated the effect of repeated studying from the effect of repeated testing, and found that repeated retrieval practice alone produced far better long-term retention than repeated restudying — even when students who only restudied felt more confident about their learning. This is useful for the project's percentage comparison specifically because it isolates the retrieval act itself as the active ingredient, not just extra exposure to the material.

### 3. Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4-58.

This large review evaluated ten popular study techniques for actual effectiveness using real classroom evidence, ranking practice testing and distributed practice as "high utility" while ranking highlighting and rereading as "low utility" despite being far more commonly used by students. This gives the project's percent-improvement comparison a broader, well-evidenced context: it's testing one specific high-utility technique against one specific low-utility one, not an arbitrary pairing.

## Exact Steps, Unit by Unit

Every step below plugs into the fixed season process described in {doc}`../../process` — tournaments and laboratories run identically regardless of field. Only the content below is specific to this project.

### Unit I — Learning Innovation Challenge
*Weeks 1–4 · Required artifact: **Innovation Proposal I***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Run the Week 2 **Confidence Gap** tournament (see the Universal Process chapter).

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] Students design a "Study Method Percent Improvement Test" — peers memorize one list of words using flashcards (retrieval practice) and another list using re-reading, then get quizzed on both. Innovation Proposal I documents the two lists, the test plan, and how percent improvement will be calculated (score after ÷ score before, turned into a percent).

### Unit II — Research Investigation
*Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: **Research Investigation / Prospectus***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 6 **Prediction Ladder** tournament + Week 7 **Build-a-System** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Literature review focus:** the testing effect (why quizzing yourself works better than re-reading), explained in plain language with real quoted findings.
- [ ] **Hypothesis:** the flashcard/quiz method will show a higher percent improvement in recall than the re-reading method.
- [ ] **Variables:** study method (flashcards vs. re-reading) as the thing being compared; percent of words correctly recalled as the outcome.
- [ ] **Methodology:** each peer tries both methods (with different word lists to keep it fair), and the class calculates each person's percent score for each method, then averages them.
- [ ] **Investigation blueprint:** a full data table showing exactly how to record and average the percentages.

### Unit III — Public Communication & Social Impact
*Weeks 9–12 · Required artifact: **Communication Product + Dissemination Plan***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 10 **Signal and the Noise** tournament + Week 11 **Message Transmission** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Communication product:** a short video, "We Tested Study Hacks With Math — Here's the Winner," showing the same percentage results.
- [ ] **Strategy document:** target audience = students before exam season; objective = replace study myths with real numbers; misconceptions addressed = "re-reading your notes is studying."
- [ ] **Dissemination:** school morning announcements, study-skills workshops, social media for students.

### Unit IV — Memory/Decision Systems Simulation
*Weeks 13–16 · Required artifact: **Simulation Model IV***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 14 **Distortion Chain** tournament + Week 15 **Distortion & Reconstruction** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **What gets simulated:** a simple bar-chart and percentage calculator where users enter their own "before" and "after" quiz scores for any study method and instantly see their percent improvement.
- [ ] **Mechanisms modeled:** how percent change is calculated, and how averaging many people's percentages gives a more trustworthy answer than just one person's result.
- [ ] **Why simulation matters:** lets any student test their own study method, not just the ones from Unit II.
- [ ] **Implementation:** a simple spreadsheet or webpage calculator; documented on GitHub with clear before/after examples.

## Why This Project Impresses

Shows a student can use simple, honest math to settle a real, common argument — proof that "doing real research" doesn't require advanced formulas, just careful measurement.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This project proves that "real research" doesn't need advanced math to be rigorous — every unit sharpens the exact same simple percentage. Unit I designs the two-list comparison. Unit II turns it into a genuine averaged-percentage study across real peers. Unit III explains that exact real number to students who've never actually measured their own study habits before. Unit IV builds a calculator so anyone, anywhere, can run the same test on their own studying and get their own real percentage. Unit V asks the school to make percent-improvement testing a standard part of study-skills week. A teacher or mentor sees a student who settled a real, common argument — "does re-reading actually work?" — using nothing but percentages and honest measurement, which is exactly the kind of clear, checkable thinking that impresses far more than unnecessary complexity would.
