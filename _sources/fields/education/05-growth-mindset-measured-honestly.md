# 5. Growth Mindset, Measured Honestly

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Three real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Dweck, C. S., & Leggett, E. L. (1988). A social-cognitive approach to motivation and personality. Psychological Review, 95(2), 256–273.

This early paper laid out Dweck's theoretical distinction between a "fixed mindset" (believing ability is a stable trait) and a "growth mindset" (believing ability can be developed through effort), and proposed that this belief shapes how people respond to setbacks — fixed-mindset individuals tend to avoid challenges that risk revealing failure, while growth-mindset individuals treat setbacks as information for improvement. This is the origin of the theory the whole project is built around, and it's useful precisely because it's the original, more cautious theoretical version — before the idea became a popular, sometimes-oversimplified education slogan.

### 2. Yeager, D. S., Hanselman, P., Walton, G. M., et al. (2019). A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement. Nature, 573(7774), 364–369.

This is the largest and most rigorous test of a growth mindset intervention ever run: a nationally representative, pre-registered randomized trial across thousands of 9th-grade students in the United States. The key, nuanced finding — exactly what the project should communicate honestly — is that a short online mindset intervention did improve grades, but the effect was concentrated among lower-achieving students and depended on the surrounding school's peer culture; it was not a uniform, everyone-benefits effect. This is the paper that should replace the oversimplified "growth mindset works for everyone" version of the idea with the real, subgroup-specific finding the project's Unit II design is built around.

### 3. Sisk, V. F., Burgoyne, A. P., Sun, J., Butler, J. L., & Macnamara, B. N. (2018). To what extent and under which circumstances are growth mindsets important to academic achievement? Two meta-analyses. Psychological Science, 29(4), 549–571.

This meta-analysis pooled data across many growth-mindset studies and found that the average relationship between having a growth mindset and academic achievement is real but small, and that mindset interventions on average produced only a small effect on grades — much smaller than popular accounts of the theory often suggest. It also found effects were somewhat larger for academically at-risk or low-income students. This source is the direct evidence for the project's central "measured honestly" framing: it's the rigorous, skeptical counterpoint to overstated mindset claims, without concluding the underlying idea is worthless.

## Full Project Toolkit

**Quick facts:** Team size 2–3 · ~3 hrs/week outside session · Cost: $0 · Needs: identifying an at-risk-of-low-achievement subgroup respectfully.

**Ethics flag:** Moderate. Be careful never to label individual participants as "at risk" to their face or peers — use only class-average or self-reported prior-difficulty measures, kept anonymous, to define the subgroup in the data, never in conversation.

**Materials by unit:** Unit I — a short structured reflection script (10 minutes, written). Unit II — a pre/post grade or persistence measure, an anonymous "how much did you struggle with this unit before" self-rating. Unit III — video equipment. Unit IV — spreadsheet for the subgroup-effect visualization.

**Ready-to-use data template (Unit II):** Participant ID · Prior Difficulty Self-Rating (1–5) · Intervention (Yes/No) · Post-Unit Grade/Persistence Score.

**Worked example — the hardest step (showing a subgroup effect visually, Unit IV):** plot every participant as one dot — x-axis is their prior difficulty rating, y-axis is their outcome, and color the dots by whether they got the intervention. If the effect is real, the colored dots should separate more on the *high-difficulty* side of the chart than the low-difficulty side. That visual split is the entire "Simpson's-paradox-style" point.

**Common pitfalls:** teams often report only the overall average and miss the subgroup pattern entirely — always break results down by the difficulty rating, not just as one combined number.

**Elevator pitch:** "Growth mindset interventions don't work the same for everyone — we tested the real, subgroup-specific version instead of the oversold 'works for all' version, and found where it actually helps."

**Low-resource adaptation:** everything here runs on paper surveys and hand-plotted graph paper; no software required.
