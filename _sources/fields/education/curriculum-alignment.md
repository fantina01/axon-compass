### HOW THIS DOCUMENT WORKS

The Operational Handbook already defines *how* every week runs — the reading-analysis pattern, the five tournament formats (Confidence Gap, Prediction Ladder, Signal and the Noise, Distortion Chain, Self-Forecast Duel), the five laboratory formats (Build-a-System Simulation, Message Transmission Simulation, Distortion & Reconstruction Experiment, Identity & System Construction Workshop, Capstone Prototype Development), and the fixed 75-minute session shapes. None of that is repeated here.

What this document provides is the piece that was missing: **exactly what content each of Education's ten projects plugs into that fixed structure, week by week, for all 24 weeks.** A chapter leader running "The Forgetting Curve Classroom" should never have to guess what their Week 10 tournament station is about, or what their Week 15 lab's manipulated stimulus should be — it's specified here, and it's specified so that Week 4's Innovation Proposal, Week 8's Research Investigation, Week 12's communication product, Week 16's simulation, and Week 20's policy draft all visibly build on each other, exactly the way the Handbook's own unit-to-unit logic requires.

Every entry below names the correct universal format from the Handbook and then gives the project-specific content for that slot — nothing here contradicts or replaces the Handbook's mechanics.

## PROJECT 1 — The Forgetting Curve Classroom
*Redesigning homework around spaced retrieval*

#### UNIT I — Weeks 1–4

**Week 1 (Reading):** Article I focus — the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve and the spacing effect. Pre-reading questions ask participants to estimate how much of tonight's homework they'll remember in a week, then log a real guess to revisit later. Research Summary I must extract one mechanism (spacing beats massing), one daily-life example, one implication for homework design.

**Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP):** the team's own station is the format this week. Participants learn a short vocabulary/formula list, predict their score, then take a delayed test 10–15 minutes later. This station *is* the pilot data behind the project's Unit I claim — save every scorecard, since Week 4's Innovation Proposal cites it directly.

**Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment):** Prediction → Trial 1 → Trial 2 → Trial 3 → Reflection → Interpretation, applied to a simple memorization task repeated across the session, connecting directly to why spacing (not massing) should help — set up as the direct precursor to the homework redesign.

**Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I):** the Spaced Retrieval Homework System — a redesigned homework calendar assigning review at 1/3/7/16-day intervals instead of one-time review. Must cite the Week 1 mechanism, the Week 2 Confidence Gap data, and the Week 3 trial results as its three required evidence points.

#### UNIT II — Weeks 5–8

**Week 5 (Reading):** Article II focus — Cepeda et al.'s spacing-effect meta-analysis and Roediger & Karpicke's testing effect. Research Summary II must state the hypothesis this project will test: spaced-interval homework beats massed review at equal total study time.

**Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER):** stations built from real spacing-vs-massing scenarios (e.g., "which study schedule will produce better recall — daily 10-minute review or one 70-minute session?"). Participants predict outcomes and rate confidence before the reveal, priming the room for the Week 7 lab.

**Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION):** the stimulus is a short study schedule passed through the five roles (Reception → Filtering → Integration → Reconstruction → Decision) — by Team 5, the "decision" is whether to recommend spaced or massed review, and the team logs exactly where the schedule's logic degraded along the way.

**Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation):** a matched-groups comparison across two class sections (spaced vs. massed homework), pre/post vocabulary or formula recall tests over a 4-week window. Poster must show the retention data as a graph and cite the Week 6–7 evidence in its discussion.

#### UNIT III — Weeks 9–12

**Week 9 (Reading):** Article III focus — how sound and framing affect whether people trust a message, applied here to how a "less homework" claim gets received skeptically by parents and teachers.

**Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE):** two framings of the *same* Unit II data — one statistic-heavy, one narrative-driven — read aloud to test which framing participants find more convincing, directly informing the podcast script's tone.

**Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION):** the ground-truth message is the project's real Unit II finding. Team 2 delivers it in confident vs. hesitant vs. defensive tones; Team 5's final "decision" (trust it or not) becomes direct evidence for how to frame the Week 12 podcast.

**Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project):** the "Why Cramming Fails" podcast, built from the Week 10–11 framing evidence, targeting skeptical parents and teachers, pitched through the PTA/newsletter channel named in the project brief.

#### UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16

**Week 13 (Reading):** Article IV focus — memory reconstruction, applied here to why people *believe* cramming works even when the data says otherwise (a confidence-vs-accuracy mismatch, same shape as the Week 2 Confidence Gap data).

**Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN):** participants recall a short spacing-schedule scenario, get distracted, then recall it again — testing whether people's mental model of "the schedule that worked" drifts inaccurately, directly motivating why a simulator (not memory alone) is needed.

**Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT):** Observation → Initial Recall → Manipulation → Final Recall → Comparison, using a short study-schedule story with a leading detail injected, followed by a full debrief. Findings feed directly into justifying the Week 16 simulator's design.

**Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation):** the interactive forgetting-curve/interval calculator (see the Full Project Toolkit's worked example), letting a user adjust interval length and watch predicted retention change — explicitly justified by Weeks 14–15's evidence that people's intuitions about spacing are unreliable.

#### UNIT V — Weeks 17–20

**Week 17 (Reading):** Integration article focus — applied here to how a school's *identity* around homework norms ("we've always assigned nightly review") resists evidence-based change.

**Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL):** each team seals three predictions about how their own homework-redesign pitch will be received by the school (will the PTA approve a pilot? will teachers adopt it?), revisited in Week 19.

**Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP):** map the *school's* system (not the individual's) — origin (why nightly-homework norms exist), influence (what shaped current policy), stakeholder (teachers/parents/students), environmental, and system components — identifying exactly which lever is most changeable.

**Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation):** the Evidence-Based Homework Policy, citing Unit II's investigation and Unit IV's simulation, defended in the Policy Forum against teacher/parent panel questions about feasibility.

#### UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24

**Week 21 (Synthesis):** the unified framework connects all four systems through one throughline: the homework schedule that started as a mockup, got tested, got explained, got modeled, and got proposed as policy.

**Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT):** Idea Selection confirms the spaced-homework system as the capstone; Evidence Mapping links it to at least 3 principles (spacing effect, testing effect, confidence-accuracy mismatch); Prototype Construction finalizes the interval calculator; Feedback Loop tests it with peers unfamiliar with the project.

**Week 23 (Capstone Draft):** full write-up integrating the proposal, study, communication product, simulation, and policy draft into one coherent document with an implementation plan for next season's pilot.

**Week 24 (Summit):** exhibition pairs the homework mockup with the live interval calculator; the reflection defense addresses the project's honestly-stated limitation (small sample, single school) and names next steps for a real pilot.

## PROJECT 2 — Cognitive Load Classrooms
*Redesigning slides and worksheets to reduce extraneous load*

#### UNIT I — Weeks 1–4

**Week 1 (Reading):** Article I focus — Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory. Pre-reading questions ask participants to rate how "busy" a sample slide feels before they know why.

**Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP):** the station teaches a short concept using a deliberately cluttered slide; participants predict their comprehension score before testing it — the mismatch between confidence and score becomes the project's first piece of evidence that bad design fools the learner, not just the material.

**Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment):** three trials comparing recall from a cluttered vs. a clean version of the same short lesson, tracking accuracy and self-rated difficulty across trials.

**Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I):** the Cognitive-Load-Optimized Slide Kit, citing the specific CLT violations audited in Week 1, the Week 2 confidence-mismatch data, and the Week 3 trial comparison.

#### UNIT II — Weeks 5–8

**Week 5 (Reading):** Article II focus — Mayer & Moreno's multimedia learning principles, extending Sweller's theory into concrete redesign rules.

**Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER):** stations present redesign choices (e.g., "narrated audio vs. on-screen text — which produces better recall?") and participants predict outcomes before the reveal.

**Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION):** the stimulus is a cluttered slide passed through the five processing roles, showing exactly where extraneous load causes information loss by the time it reaches "Conscious Thought" (Team 5).

**Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation):** the counterbalanced within-subject comparison (original vs. redesigned materials across two topics), poster showing comprehension-quiz results with a bar chart.

#### UNIT III — Weeks 9–12

**Week 9 (Reading):** Article III focus — how framing and delivery affect whether a "design change" pitch lands with teachers who feel their materials are already fine.

**Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE):** the same redesign pitch delivered two ways (technical jargon vs. plain "less clutter, more learning" language) to find which framing teachers respond to better.

**Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION):** the ground truth is the project's core finding; the simulation tests how it survives being explained teacher-to-teacher through the five roles, informing the "Death by PowerPoint" video's actual script.

**Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project):** the "Death by PowerPoint" awareness video, targeting teachers and PTA, using the Week 10–11 evidence on framing.

#### UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16

**Week 13 (Reading):** Article IV focus — memory reconstruction, applied to why a teacher might "remember" their own slides as clearer than they actually are.

**Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN):** participants view a cluttered slide, get distracted, then recall its content — quantifying exactly how much detail is lost, motivating the need for an objective model rather than self-report.

**Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT):** a lesson concept is taught with an embedded misleading visual; initial vs. final recall comparison shows exactly where extraneous load caused a factual error to stick.

**Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation):** the interactive working-memory-load slider (elements on screen vs. predicted comprehension drop-off), justified directly by Weeks 14–15's data.

#### UNIT V — Weeks 17–20

**Week 17 (Reading):** Integration article focus — applied to how "instructional design" becomes part of a teacher's professional identity, and why redesign can feel like criticism.

**Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL):** predictions about whether a specific teacher will adopt the redesigned template within two weeks of seeing it, revisited in Week 19.

**Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP):** map the school's instructional-design culture as a system — where does resistance to redesign actually come from, and which stakeholder has the power to normalize the change?

**Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation):** the district instructional-design style guide proposal, defended with the Unit II comprehension data and Unit IV load model.

#### UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24

**Week 21 (Synthesis):** the throughline — one cluttered slide, audited, tested, explained, modeled, and turned into a district-level design standard.

**Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT):** finalize the slide kit as the capstone artifact, map it to at least 3 CLT principles, get peer feedback on a sample redesigned lesson.

**Week 23 (Capstone Draft):** full integration of audit, study, video, model, and policy proposal.

**Week 24 (Summit):** before/after slide kit displayed beside the live load-slider tool; reflection defense addresses generalizability across subjects.

## PROJECT 3 — The Sleep-Deprived School Start
*Advocating for circadian-aligned school schedules*

#### UNIT I — Weeks 1–4

**Week 1 (Reading):** Article I focus — Carskadon's adolescent circadian-delay research. Pre-reading asks participants to estimate their own average school-night sleep before checking it against their real logged hours.

**Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP):** a station testing a short memory/attention task, run once with participants self-reporting how "rested" they feel, predicting their score — sleep-related overconfidence becomes an early data point.

**Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment):** three trials of a simple attention task, run at the actual time of day the first class period starts, tracking performance across the session as a proxy for real morning alertness.

**Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I):** the Circadian-Aligned Bell Schedule proposal for the participants' own school, citing Week 1's biology, the Week 2 confidence data, and Week 3's morning-alertness trial.

#### UNIT II — Weeks 5–8

**Week 5 (Reading):** Article II focus — CDC school-start-time guidance and Wahlstrom's real-district start-time studies.

**Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER):** predict-and-reveal stations using real published start-time-change outcomes from other districts (attendance, tardiness) before the reveal.

**Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION):** the stimulus is a student's real school-night sleep log passed through the five roles, ending in a "Decision" team recommendation about start time — modeling how administrators might interpret (or misinterpret) sleep data.

**Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation):** the sleep-survey study comparing self-reported sleep, attention-test performance, and tardiness patterns among the school's own students, poster with a sleep-vs-performance chart.

#### UNIT III — Weeks 9–12

**Week 9 (Reading):** Article III focus — how sound and messaging framing affect whether a "later start time" proposal is heard as reasonable or as coddling.

**Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE):** two framings of the same sleep-survey finding (student-wellbeing framing vs. academic-performance framing) tested for persuasiveness.

**Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION):** the ground truth is the Unit II sleep-survey finding, passed through delivery/attention/reconstruction/decision roles to pressure-test how it might land with a skeptical school board member.

**Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project):** the "8 AM Is Too Early" documentary, aimed at parents and local officials, built from the Week 10–11 framing evidence.

#### UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16

**Week 13 (Reading):** Article IV focus — memory reconstruction, applied to why adults might misremember their own teenage sleep needs as similar to a teen's today.

**Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN):** participants recall a short sleep-statistic-laden paragraph, get distracted, recall again — testing how easily real sleep data gets garbled when repeated secondhand (relevant to how the proposal will travel through a school board).

**Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT):** a short "district sleep report" story with one leading statistic injected; comparison shows how easily a false number sticks, motivating precise sourcing in the Week 16 model.

**Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation):** the two-process sleep model (homeostatic pressure + circadian rhythm) letting a user drag start time and see predicted alertness change, justified by Weeks 14–15's evidence for careful, precise data use.

#### UNIT V — Weeks 17–20

**Week 17 (Reading):** Integration article focus — applied to how "early start time" has become part of the school's institutional identity and scheduling logistics (buses, sports).

**Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL):** predictions about how the school board will respond to a start-time pitch, revisited in Week 19.

**Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP):** map the school system's actual constraints (bus routes, after-school sports, parent work schedules) to find the most realistic implementation path.

**Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation):** the specific start-time-shift proposal, addressing bus/logistics objections directly, defended with Unit II data and Unit IV's alertness model.

#### UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24

**Week 21 (Synthesis):** the throughline — one circadian mismatch, surveyed, explained, modeled, and turned into a real proposal a school board could act on.

**Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT):** finalize the alertness-curve simulator as the capstone artifact, mapped to circadian biology, homeostatic sleep pressure, and the sleep-survey data.

**Week 23 (Capstone Draft):** integrate survey, documentary, model, and policy proposal into one advocacy package.

**Week 24 (Summit):** exhibition includes the sleep survey and live alertness simulator; reflection defense addresses the transportation-cost objection honestly.

## PROJECT 4 — Stereotype Threat in the Classroom
*Redesigning test framing to protect performance*

**⚠ Ethics escalation applies to this entire project — see the Full Project Toolkit's ethics flag before running any Unit II data collection.**

#### UNIT I — Weeks 1–4

**Week 1 (Reading):** Article I focus — Steele & Aronson's foundational stereotype-threat studies. Pre-reading questions ask participants to reflect (privately, ungraded) on any time test instructions themselves affected their confidence.

**Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP):** a neutral station task (unrelated to identity) still used to build the habit of predicting performance before testing — kept deliberately low-stakes and unrelated to stereotype content this week, since the sensitive framing work doesn't start until Unit II under full ethics review.

**Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment):** three trials on a neutral task, establishing the team's general prediction-vs-performance baseline methodology before it's applied to the real study design.

**Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I):** the Threat-Neutral Test Framing Protocol — the redesigned instruction wording — drafted and reviewed by an adult advisor per the Ethics Checklist, citing Week 1's mechanism.

#### UNIT II — Weeks 5–8

**Week 5 (Reading):** Article II focus — Spencer, Steele & Quinn's extension to gender and math performance, and Flore & Wicherts' meta-analysis showing the effect is real but smaller than early work suggested.

**Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER):** predict-and-reveal stations using real published stereotype-threat effect sizes (from the honestly-calibrated literature) rather than any live manipulation.

**Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION):** the stimulus is a test-instruction script passed through the five roles, showing how subtly a diagnostic framing cue can survive even careful editing — direct rehearsal for writing a truly neutral script.

**Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation):** the full randomized framing-assignment study, run only after Ethics Checklist Section 4 sign-off, poster reporting score and stress-rating comparisons between conditions.

#### UNIT III — Weeks 9–12

**Week 9 (Reading):** Article III focus — how communication framing shapes whether a sensitive finding builds trust or triggers defensiveness in an audience.

**Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE):** two framings of the Unit II finding (accusatory vs. constructive) tested for audience reaction, directly shaping the Week 12 campaign's tone.

**Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION):** the ground truth is the study's real finding, passed through the five roles to pressure-test how a teacher-training audience might receive it defensively — informing careful, blame-free scripting.

**Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project):** the "Words Before the Test" campaign for teachers and test-designers, built from the Week 10–11 framing evidence, careful never to imply any teacher intended harm.

#### UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16

**Week 13 (Reading):** Article IV focus — memory reconstruction, applied to how a student's memory of "how a test felt" can shift based on the outcome.

**Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN):** a neutral recall task (not identity-related) demonstrating the general distortion mechanism, kept deliberately separated from any sensitive content.

**Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT):** run only with neutral, non-identity-linked material per the Ethics Checklist — the point this week is the general reconstruction mechanism, not another identity-linked manipulation.

**Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation):** the modeled performance-gap-by-wording-intensity simulator, built from published effect sizes (not new data collection), letting a user see how small wording changes are predicted to shift outcomes.

#### UNIT V — Weeks 17–20

**Week 17 (Reading):** Integration article focus — applied to how testing culture itself becomes part of a school's identity and resists format change.

**Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL):** predictions about whether the school's assessment office will adopt threat-neutral language, revisited in Week 19.

**Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP):** map the school's assessment culture as a system, identifying who actually writes test instructions and where the leverage point for change is.

**Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation):** the district test-framing standard proposal, citing Unit II's design and Unit IV's modeled effect sizes, addressing equity officers directly.

#### UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24

**Week 21 (Synthesis):** the throughline — one redesigned sentence of test instructions, tested, explained carefully, modeled, and proposed as a real assessment standard.

**Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT):** finalize the framing protocol as the capstone artifact, evidence-mapped to at least 3 principles, feedback-tested with peers.

**Week 23 (Capstone Draft):** integrate the framing protocol, study, campaign, model, and policy proposal.

**Week 24 (Summit):** presented with full transparency about effect-size uncertainty; reflection defense explicitly addresses how the team weighed real evidence against overstated popular claims.

## PROJECT 5 — Growth Mindset, Measured Honestly
*An evidence-calibrated mindset intervention*

#### UNIT I — Weeks 1–4

**Week 1 (Reading):** Article I focus — Dweck's original theory, introduced alongside a caution: this unit will test the calibrated version, not the popular oversold one.

**Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP):** a moderately difficult station task where participants predict their score, connecting directly to the "does believing you can improve change how you predict yourself" question at the heart of the project.

**Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment):** three trials on a task with escalating difficulty, tracking whether persistence (not just accuracy) changes across trials — an early behavioral signal relevant to mindset.

**Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I):** the scaled-down, evidence-calibrated mindset intervention (short structured reflection before a difficult unit), targeted specifically at students showing signs of lower prior achievement, not the whole class.

#### UNIT II — Weeks 5–8

**Week 5 (Reading):** Article II focus — Yeager et al.'s 2019 national pre-registered replication, showing effects are real but subgroup-specific and modest.

**Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER):** stations present real subgroup-vs-aggregate outcome scenarios; participants predict which pattern is real before the reveal, rehearsing the Simpson's-paradox-style reasoning Unit II's analysis will require.

**Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION):** the stimulus is a "mindset intervention pitch" passed through the five roles, showing how an oversold, uncalibrated version of the message can end up as the final "Decision" if not corrected at every stage.

**Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation):** the pre-registered, subgroup-stratified study directly modeled on Yeager et al.'s design, poster reporting outcomes broken out by prior-achievement subgroup, not just an overall average.

#### UNIT III — Weeks 9–12

**Week 9 (Reading):** Article III focus — how communication framing determines whether a nuanced finding gets heard as nuanced or gets flattened into a soundbite.

**Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE):** the same subgroup finding framed as "growth mindset works!" vs. "growth mindset works for some students, here's who" — testing which framing an audience actually retains accurately.

**Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION):** the ground truth is the real subgroup finding; the simulation tests how easily it flattens into an oversimplified claim by the time it reaches "Decision" — direct rehearsal for writing an accurate video script.

**Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project):** the "Does Growth Mindset Actually Work?" explainer video for teachers, built from the Week 10–11 evidence on how to keep nuance intact.

#### UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16

**Week 13 (Reading):** Article IV focus — memory reconstruction, applied to why a teacher might remember "the mindset assembly worked great" based on a vivid anecdote rather than real data.

**Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN):** participants recall a short mixed-results dataset, get distracted, recall again — testing how easily an aggregate result gets misremembered as uniform.

**Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT):** a short "mindset study summary" with a subtly overstated claim injected; comparison shows how easily nuance is lost in retelling, motivating the precision of the Week 16 model.

**Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation):** the subgroup-effect visualization (see the Full Project Toolkit's worked example), directly justified by Weeks 14–15's evidence that aggregate numbers can mislead.

#### UNIT V — Weeks 17–20

**Week 17 (Reading):** Integration article focus — applied to how "growth mindset" became part of many schools' institutional identity, sometimes ahead of the evidence.

**Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL):** predictions about whether the school will accept a subgroup-targeted (not blanket) mindset policy, revisited in Week 19.

**Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP):** map why blanket, assembly-style interventions became the norm, and where a more targeted policy could realistically be adopted instead.

**Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation):** the policy recommending subgroup-targeted (not blanket) mindset interventions, defended with Unit II's data and Unit IV's subgroup model, honestly noting fade-out risk.

#### UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24

**Week 21 (Synthesis):** the throughline — a popular claim, tested honestly, explained without oversimplifying, modeled precisely, and turned into a more responsible policy.

**Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT):** finalize the subgroup-visualization tool as the capstone artifact, evidence-mapped to the replication literature.

**Week 23 (Capstone Draft):** integrate the calibrated intervention, study, video, model, and policy proposal.

**Week 24 (Summit):** reflection defense frames the whole project as a case study in scientific self-correction, from the popular claim to the calibrated one.

## PROJECT 7 — Attention Span or Attention Design?
*Redesigning lesson pacing around vigilance decrement*

#### UNIT I — Weeks 1–4

**Week 1 (Reading):** Article I focus — Mackworth's vigilance-decrement research. Pre-reading asks participants to predict how long they can sustain focus on a boring task before testing it.

**Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP):** a sustained-attention station (a long, low-stimulation monitoring task) where participants predict how their performance will change over time, then compare to their real decline curve.

**Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment):** three trials of the same monitoring task at different points in the session, tracking the real decline pattern directly.

**Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I):** the Vigilance-Paced Lesson Plan template with built-in attention-reset breaks, citing Week 1's mechanism and the Week 2–3 decline data.

#### UNIT II — Weeks 5–8

**Week 5 (Reading):** Article II focus — Warm, Parasuraman & Matthews' finding that vigilance is effortful and stressful, not passive.

**Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER):** stations present real lesson-pacing scenarios (with vs. without breaks); participants predict which produces better sustained comprehension before the reveal.

**Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION):** the stimulus is a long unbroken lesson explanation passed through the five roles, showing where "Attention System" (Team 3) starts dropping information as fatigue sets in.

**Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation):** the within-class comparison of paced vs. unbroken lesson days, poster showing attention-check accuracy over time for both conditions.

#### UNIT III — Weeks 9–12

**Week 9 (Reading):** Article III focus — how a message's delivery pacing itself affects whether an audience stays engaged — deliberately mirroring the project's own topic.

**Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE):** the same "attention isn't broken, schedules are" message delivered in a rushed vs. well-paced style, testing which version participants actually retain.

**Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION):** the ground truth is the Unit II finding, passed through the five roles to test how well it survives being explained teacher-to-teacher without oversimplifying into "just take more breaks."

**Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project):** the "Your Brain Isn't Broken, Your Schedule Is" campaign, built from the Week 10–11 evidence, targeting teacher training and wellness clubs.

#### UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16

**Week 13 (Reading):** Article IV focus — memory reconstruction, applied to why a teacher might misremember a long lesson as more engaging than the attention data actually shows.

**Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN):** participants recall a long lesson excerpt, get distracted, recall again — quantifying how much detail from the "fatigued" portion is lost compared to the "fresh" portion.

**Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT):** a lesson-pacing case study with a subtly misleading detail injected; comparison shows how easily the "just push through" narrative persists inaccurately.

**Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation):** the vigilance-decrement curve with adjustable break intervals, justified by Weeks 14–15's evidence.

#### UNIT V — Weeks 17–20

**Week 17 (Reading):** Integration article focus — applied to how "cover the material" pacing culture becomes part of a school's teaching identity.

**Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL):** predictions about whether teachers will actually adopt paced lesson plans, revisited in Week 19.

**Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP):** map the school's pacing culture as a system, identifying where curriculum-pressure constraints could realistically be worked around.

**Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation):** the standard break-interval recommendation for lesson-planning guidelines, citing Unit II and IV evidence.

#### UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24

**Week 21 (Synthesis):** the throughline — attention fatigue, observed, tested, explained, modeled, and turned into a real pacing standard.

**Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT):** finalize the vigilance-curve tool as the capstone artifact, evidence-mapped to the vigilance-decrement literature.

**Week 23 (Capstone Draft):** integrate observation, study, campaign, model, and policy proposal.

**Week 24 (Summit):** reflection defense addresses how a "deceptively simple" reframe (schedule, not student, is the problem) was backed with real, testable evidence throughout.

## PROJECT 8 — Testing as Teaching
*Reframing formative assessment using the testing effect*

#### UNIT I — Weeks 1–4

**Week 1 (Reading):** Article I focus — Roediger & Karpicke's testing-effect studies. Pre-reading asks participants to predict whether quizzing or re-reading helps more, before finding out.

**Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP):** a station comparing quiz-based vs. re-reading review on a short word list, participants predicting their own delayed-recall score under each method.

**Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment):** three trials comparing quiz-based and re-reading review across the session, tracking recall accuracy directly.

**Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I):** the Low-Stakes Retrieval Quiz System, replacing passive review time with ungraded quizzes, citing Week 1's mechanism and the Week 2–3 comparison data.

#### UNIT II — Weeks 5–8

**Week 5 (Reading):** Article II focus — Putnam, Sungkhasettee & Roediger's applied testing-effect findings, including the anxiety question.

**Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER):** stations present real classroom scenarios (quiz-based vs. re-reading review); participants predict outcomes and rate confidence before the reveal.

**Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION):** the stimulus is a study session passed through the five roles, contrasting what survives when "Memory System" (Team 4) processes retrieval-based vs. re-reading-based input.

**Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation):** the matched-group comparison across parallel class sections (retrieval quiz vs. passive re-reading), poster reporting delayed recall and anxiety-rating results side by side.

#### UNIT III — Weeks 9–12

**Week 9 (Reading):** Article III focus — how framing affects whether "more quizzes" is heard as a burden or a benefit by anxious students and skeptical parents.

**Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE):** the same low-stakes-quiz proposal framed as "more testing" vs. "smarter, shorter studying," testing which framing reduces perceived anxiety.

**Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION):** the ground truth is the Unit II anxiety-and-recall finding, passed through the five roles to pressure-test how it might be misheard as "more high-stakes testing" if not framed carefully.

**Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project):** the "Why Quizzes Aren't the Enemy" podcast, built from the Week 10–11 evidence, addressing anxiety concerns directly for teacher PD and parent newsletters.

#### UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16

**Week 13 (Reading):** Article IV focus — memory reconstruction, applied to why students might remember re-reading as "working" because it felt easier, despite weaker real retention.

**Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN):** participants recall a re-read vs. quizzed passage, get distracted, recall again — directly demonstrating the felt-ease-vs-real-retention gap.

**Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT):** a study-session case with a misleading "felt like I learned it" detail injected; comparison shows how confidence and accuracy diverge, motivating the Week 16 model's precision.

**Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation):** the retention-curve comparison (quiz-based vs. re-reading), justified directly by Weeks 14–15's evidence.

#### UNIT V — Weeks 17–20

**Week 17 (Reading):** Integration article focus — applied to how grading culture ("tests are for grades") makes low-stakes quizzing feel contradictory to a school's identity.

**Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL):** predictions about whether teachers will adopt low-stakes quizzes given workload concerns, revisited in Week 19.

**Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP):** map the school's grading culture as a system, identifying where formative (not summative) quizzing could realistically fit without adding real teacher workload.

**Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation):** the grading-policy proposal building low-stakes retrieval quizzes in as formative tools, citing Unit II and IV evidence, honestly addressing teacher workload limitations.

#### UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24

**Week 21 (Synthesis):** the throughline — a contested classroom practice, tested fairly, explained without minimizing anxiety concerns, modeled precisely, and turned into a workable grading-policy compromise.

**Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT):** finalize the quiz-cadence system as the capstone artifact, evidence-mapped to the testing-effect literature.

**Week 23 (Capstone Draft):** integrate the redesigned quiz system, study, podcast, model, and policy ask.

**Week 24 (Summit):** reflection defense addresses the anxiety question honestly, showing the real data rather than dismissing the concern.

## PROJECT 9 — Neuromyths in the Staff Room
*Auditing and correcting popular educational neuromyths*

#### UNIT I — Weeks 1–4

**Week 1 (Reading):** Article I focus — Dekker et al.'s neuromyth-prevalence findings. Pre-reading asks participants to rate their own belief in several common brain claims before learning which are myths.

**Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP):** a true/false neuromyth quiz where participants predict their own score before taking it — high-confidence wrong answers become the project's first real evidence of how convincing myths can feel.

**Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment):** three trials of the neuromyth quiz with corrective feedback between trials, tracking whether belief scores shift with each correction round.

**Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I):** the Neuromyth Audit and Correction Toolkit, starting with a real survey of teachers' beliefs, citing Week 1's mechanism and the Week 2–3 correction data.

#### UNIT II — Weeks 5–8

**Week 5 (Reading):** Article II focus — Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork's learning-styles debunking review.

**Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER):** stations present real neuromyth statements; participants predict whether each is true or false and rate confidence before the reveal, rehearsing the exact survey-item design needed for Unit II.

**Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION):** the stimulus is a neuromyth statement passed through the five roles, showing how a myth survives even careful "correction" attempts if not addressed precisely.

**Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation):** the pre/post teacher survey around a staff PD correction session, poster reporting belief-score change with anonymized, respectful data handling per the Ethics Checklist.

#### UNIT III — Weeks 9–12

**Week 9 (Reading):** Article III focus — Howard-Jones' work on why simple debunking often fails to actually change belief.

**Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE):** the same myth-correction fact framed as a blunt correction vs. a curious "let's check together" framing, testing which produces less defensiveness.

**Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION):** the ground truth is the Unit II survey finding, passed through the five roles to pressure-test how a correction might be received defensively by staff who've held a myth for years.

**Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project):** the "Busting the Learning Styles Myth" explainer series, built from the Week 10–11 evidence on non-defensive framing, for staff PD days.

#### UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16

**Week 13 (Reading):** Article IV focus — memory reconstruction, applied to why a myth can feel true anecdotally even after correction (confirmation bias reinforcing old belief).

**Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN):** participants recall a corrected-myth statement, get distracted, recall again — testing whether the original myth or the correction is what actually sticks.

**Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT):** a myth-correction case study with a subtly reverted detail injected; comparison shows how easily a correction can "snap back" toward the original myth, motivating the Week 16 model.

**Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation):** the belief-persistence model showing how a false belief can survive despite correction due to confirmation bias, justified by Weeks 14–15's evidence.

#### UNIT V — Weeks 17–20

**Week 17 (Reading):** Integration article focus — applied to how neuromyths become embedded in a school's professional-development identity over years.

**Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL):** predictions about whether new-teacher onboarding will adopt a neuromyth-literacy module, revisited in Week 19.

**Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP):** map the school's PD system, identifying where a neuromyth-literacy module could realistically be inserted into existing onboarding.

**Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation):** the mandatory neuromyth-literacy-module proposal for new-teacher onboarding, citing Unit II survey data and Unit IV's belief-persistence model.

#### UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24

**Week 21 (Synthesis):** the throughline — a self-reflexive audit of the education system's own evidence hygiene, tested, explained respectfully, modeled, and turned into an onboarding policy.

**Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT):** finalize the audit-and-correction toolkit as the capstone artifact, evidence-mapped to the neuromyth-prevalence literature.

**Week 23 (Capstone Draft):** integrate the audit, study, campaign, model, and onboarding policy.

**Week 24 (Summit):** reflection defense highlights the unusually meta-level nature of the project — using scientific literacy on the education system itself.

## PROJECT 10 — Recess and the Developing Brain
*Defending unstructured play time with neuroscience*

#### UNIT I — Weeks 1–4

**Week 1 (Reading):** Article I focus — Pellegrini's recess/cognitive-performance research. Pre-reading asks participants to predict whether more or less recess time helps afternoon focus, before checking published evidence.

**Week 2 (Game Tournament — THE CONFIDENCE GAP):** an attention-check station run once before and once after a short active break, participants predicting whether the break will help or hurt their next-task performance.

**Week 3 (Field-in-Action Experiment):** three trials of an attention task, some preceded by a short active break and some not, tracking performance across conditions.

**Week 4 (Main Output — Innovation Proposal I):** the Recess Impact Dashboard proposal documenting current recess policy and a redesigned schedule, citing Week 1's mechanism and the Week 2–3 data.

#### UNIT II — Weeks 5–8

**Week 5 (Reading):** Article II focus — Diamond's executive-function-and-physical-activity research, and Hillman, Erickson & Kramer's exercise-and-cognition review.

**Week 6 (Game Tournament — THE PREDICTION LADDER):** stations present real recess-cut policy scenarios; participants predict the likely effect on afternoon behavior before the reveal.

**Week 7 (Cognitive Laboratory — BUILD-A-SYSTEM SIMULATION):** the stimulus is an afternoon lesson passed through the five roles under two conditions (recess vs. no recess beforehand), showing where "Attention System" (Team 3) performs differently.

**Week 8 (Main Output — Research Investigation):** the natural comparison across real school days with and without scheduled recess, poster reporting afternoon attention-task performance and aggregate behavior-incident counts.

#### UNIT III — Weeks 9–12

**Week 9 (Reading):** Article III focus — how framing determines whether "protect recess" sounds like real evidence or like resistance to academic rigor.

**Week 10 (Game Tournament — SIGNAL AND THE NOISE):** the same recess-and-focus finding framed as "more play" vs. "smarter scheduling for better academics," testing which framing lands better with achievement-focused audiences.

**Week 11 (Cognitive Laboratory — MESSAGE TRANSMISSION SIMULATION):** the ground truth is the Unit II finding, passed through the five roles to pressure-test how it might be dismissed as "just wanting more playtime" if not framed around the academic-outcome evidence.

**Week 12 (Main Output — Public Communication Project):** the "Cutting Recess, Cutting Focus" documentary, built from the Week 10–11 evidence, targeting parent associations and school-board public comment.

#### UNIT IV — Weeks 13–16

**Week 13 (Reading):** Article IV focus — memory reconstruction, applied to why an administrator might remember a "productive" no-recess afternoon more positively than the actual attention data supports.

**Week 14 (Game Tournament — THE DISTORTION CHAIN):** participants recall a short afternoon-behavior report, get distracted, recall again — testing how easily "the afternoon went fine" becomes the remembered version regardless of real incident counts.

**Week 15 (Cognitive Laboratory — DISTORTION & RECONSTRUCTION EXPERIMENT):** an afternoon-schedule case study with a misleading "recess wasn't necessary" detail injected; comparison shows how easily this belief persists inaccurately, motivating the precision of the Week 16 model.

**Week 16 (Main Output — Memory Systems Simulation):** the executive-function-capacity-over-the-day model, with and without a play break, justified directly by Weeks 14–15's evidence.

#### UNIT V — Weeks 17–20

**Week 17 (Reading):** Integration article focus — applied to how "more instructional minutes" became part of many schools' identity under achievement pressure, sometimes at recess's expense.

**Week 18 (Game Tournament — THE SELF-FORECAST DUEL):** predictions about whether the school board will vote to protect a minimum recess allotment, revisited in Week 19.

**Week 19 (Cognitive Laboratory — IDENTITY & SYSTEM CONSTRUCTION WORKSHOP):** map the district's scheduling-pressure system, identifying where the strongest, most fundable argument for protecting recess actually lies.

**Week 20 (Main Output — Policy Formation Simulation):** the minimum-recess-protection policy proposal, citing Unit II data and Unit IV modeling, targeted at school boards and superintendents.

#### UNIT VI — Weeks 21–24

**Week 21 (Synthesis):** the throughline — a policy trend, questioned with real data, explained persuasively, modeled precisely, and turned into a real protection proposal.

**Week 22 (Cognitive Laboratory — CAPSTONE PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT):** finalize the executive-function model as the capstone artifact, evidence-mapped to the exercise-and-cognition literature.

**Week 23 (Capstone Draft):** integrate the dashboard, study, documentary, model, and protection policy.

**Week 24 (Summit):** presented as advocacy-ready material for a real school-board meeting; reflection defense addresses how developmental neuroscience translated into genuine local action.

### CROSS-PROJECT NOTE

Every project above runs the exact same 24-week skeleton from the Operational Handbook — five tournaments (Confidence Gap → Prediction Ladder → Signal and the Noise → Distortion Chain → Self-Forecast Duel), five labs (Build-a-System → Message Transmission → Distortion & Reconstruction → Identity & System Construction → Capstone Prototype Development), and five main outputs building toward one capstone. What changes, project to project, is only the content poured into that fixed shape — which is exactly what makes it possible for a chapter leader to run all ten of these, or any mix of them alongside a different field entirely, without ever learning a tenth different process.
