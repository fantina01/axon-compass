# 7. Anchoring in the Aisles

*Neural Reference Points and Retail Price Perception*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Economics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `economics/07-anchoring-in-the-aisles`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can teaching consumers about anchoring effects (how an initial reference price skews perceived value of subsequent prices) improve resistance to manipulative pricing tactics?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Anchoring is one of the most replicated cognitive biases and underlies common retail tactics (inflated "original prices" next to sale prices); consumer-education interventions grounded in the mechanism itself tend to be more durable than generic "be a smart shopper" advice.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131.

This is the original paper introducing the anchoring-and-adjustment heuristic, among other cognitive biases. In one now-famous demonstration, participants who spun a rigged wheel landing on a high number gave systematically higher estimates for an unrelated question (the percentage of African countries in the UN) than participants who saw a low number — even though the wheel had no logical connection to the question. This establishes that an arbitrary, irrelevant number can measurably shift subsequent judgments, which is the psychological mechanism the entire project applies to retail "was $X, now $Y" pricing.

### 2. Ariely, D., Loewenstein, G., & Prelec, D. (2003). 'Coherent arbitrary': Stable demand curves without stable preferences. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 118(1), 73–105.

Ariely and colleagues showed that even arbitrary anchors — like the last two digits of a participant's own social security number — could shift how much people were willing to pay for ordinary consumer items, and that once anchored, participants' subsequent price judgments stayed internally consistent with that arbitrary starting point. This extends the classic anchoring finding directly into a purchasing/willingness-to-pay context, closely mirroring the project's retail pricing scenario.

### 3. Nunes, J. C., & Boatwright, P. (2004). Incidental prices and their effect on willingness to pay. Journal of Marketing Research, 41(4), 457–466.

This study found that prices seen on completely unrelated nearby products (like a T-shirt priced at a store display next to an unrelated item) could shift how much people were willing to pay for the actual item they came to buy — a real retail-environment demonstration of anchoring in action. This is useful specifically because it's set in an actual shopping-adjacent context, closely paralleling the project's mock-store experimental design.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This project's portfolio strength comes from its precision: it never drifts from one specific, testable claim — that seeing a fake "original price" distorts a shopper's sense of real value. The Unit I toolkit and mock-store pilot establish the phenomenon. Unit II's controlled price-judgment experiment measures it precisely. Unit III's explainer video takes that exact, precise finding to people who shop every week. Unit IV's reference-dependent valuation curve is literally the mathematical shape of what Unit II measured, made interactive. Unit V's policy ask for clearer "original price" disclosure standards is a specific, realistic regulatory recommendation backed directly by that data. A reader moving through this portfolio sees the same sharp, well-defined claim get more rigorously proven and more broadly applied at every single step — nothing vague, nothing padded, which is exactly what makes a tightly-scoped project often outshine a more ambitious-sounding but looser one.
