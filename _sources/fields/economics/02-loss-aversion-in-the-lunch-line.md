# 2. Loss Aversion in the Lunch Line

*Nudging Healthier School Cafeteria Choices*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Economics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `economics/02-loss-aversion-in-the-lunch-line`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can framing cafeteria choices around loss aversion (rather than gain-framed health messaging) increase healthy food selection without restricting choice?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Loss aversion — the neural/behavioral tendency to weigh losses roughly twice as heavily as equivalent gains — is a well-established mechanism behind "nudge" interventions; testing it in a cafeteria setting offers a genuinely low-cost, choice-preserving path to better nutrition outcomes.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1979). Prospect theory: An analysis of decision under risk. Econometrica, 47(2), 263–291.

This is the founding paper of prospect theory and one of the most cited papers in economics. Kahneman and Tversky showed that people evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point (not in absolute terms), and that losses relative to that reference point are felt roughly twice as strongly as equivalent gains — a pattern they termed loss aversion. This paper is the theoretical bedrock for the entire project: loss-framed cafeteria messaging works specifically because it activates this asymmetry between how losses and gains are weighted psychologically.

### 2. Thaler, R. H., Sunstein, C. R., & Balz, J. P. (2012). Choice architecture. In Handbook of Behavioral Economics (also released as a Social Science Research Network working paper).

Thaler and Sunstein's broader "choice architecture" and "nudge" framework describes how the way choices are presented — not just what the choices are — predictably shapes decisions, without restricting anyone's actual options. Applied to food environments specifically, their framework is the basis for real cafeteria interventions (like changing the order or visibility of food items) that have been shown to shift what people choose. This grounds the project's signage redesign in the broader, well-established nudge literature rather than treating loss-framing as an isolated trick.

### 3. Hanks, A. S., Just, D. R., Smith, L. E., & Wansink, B. (2012). Healthy convenience: nudging students toward healthier choices in the lunchroom. Journal of Public Health, 34(3), 370–376.

This field study tested small, low-cost changes to how food was positioned and labeled in real school cafeterias and found measurable shifts toward healthier selections without removing any food options or changing prices. It's included specifically because it's real cafeteria-setting evidence, not just laboratory data — directly relevant to whether the project's own signage intervention is likely to produce a real effect in an actual school lunch line. (Note: some studies from this research group have faced later scrutiny over data practices in unrelated papers by the same lab; students should treat this as one supporting data point among several, not the sole basis for the hypothesis.)

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

Every artifact in this project points at the exact same redesigned cafeteria sign. The Unit I mockup is the idea. The Unit II A/B test is proof the idea works, with real numbers from a real cafeteria line. The Unit III video takes those exact numbers and makes the case to the people who'd actually decide whether to adopt it — student government, wellness staff. The Unit IV model doesn't introduce a new concept; it makes the Unit II finding visible, showing precisely why loss-framed language outperforms gain-framed language on a value curve anyone can manipulate. The Unit V policy ask isn't hypothetical — it's a specific, adoptable signage guideline for the district's actual nutrition services team, citing the actual data collected in Unit II. A judge or mentor reading this portfolio sees a straight line from "I noticed something" to "I proved it" to "I explained it to the people who needed to hear it" to "I'm asking the right people to adopt it" — that line, unbroken, is what makes a portfolio feel like real research rather than a school assignment.
