## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Kaplan, S. (1995). The restorative benefits of nature: Toward an integrative framework. Journal of Environmental Psychology, 15(3), 169-182.

(Verified in the Education field's "Attention Span or Attention Design" project.) Kaplan's Attention Restoration Theory shows directed attention becomes fatigued with sustained use and can be restored by brief breaks — the direct theoretical basis for why the Focus Kit's built-in break structure should genuinely help, not just feel like a pause.

### 2. Ariga, A., & Lleras, A. (2011). Brief and rare mental breaks keep you focused: Deactivation and reactivation of task goals preempt vigilance decrements. Cognition, 118(3), 439-443.

This study found that inserting brief, occasional mental breaks during a long, monotonous task significantly improved sustained performance compared to working straight through — even very short breaks measurably delayed the vigilance decrement. This is directly useful, practical evidence for the Focus Kit's core design choice: short scheduled breaks during a study session, not just at the end of it.

### 3. Mackworth, N. H. (1948). The breakdown of vigilance during prolonged visual search. Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1(1), 6-21.

(Verified in the Education field's "Attention Span or Attention Design" project.) Mackworth's classic finding — that sustained attention reliably declines within about 30 minutes even on simple, motivating tasks — is the foundational evidence for why any study session, no matter how important, benefits from a break structure built around real attention limits.

## Exact Steps, Unit by Unit

Every step below plugs into the fixed season process described in {doc}`../../process` — tournaments and laboratories run identically regardless of field. Only the content below is specific to this project.

### Unit I — Learning Innovation Challenge
*Weeks 1–4 · Required artifact: **Innovation Proposal I***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Run the Week 2 **Confidence Gap** tournament (see the Universal Process chapter).

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] Students build a first version of a "Focus Kit" — this could be a simple physical kit (a timer, a printed checklist, a phone-lockbox idea) or a digital tool (a printable planner, a simple app concept) based on real attention-restoration research (like the idea that short breaks and removing visual clutter genuinely help). Innovation Proposal I documents the first prototype and who it's for.

### Unit II — Research Investigation
*Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: **Research Investigation / Prospectus***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 6 **Prediction Ladder** tournament + Week 7 **Build-a-System** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Literature review focus:** attention-restoration and vigilance-decrement research, explained simply as the "why" behind the kit's design choices.
- [ ] **Hypothesis:** peers who use the Focus Kit for a study session report better focus and get more done than peers who study without it.
- [ ] **Variables:** kit use (yes/no) as the thing being compared; self-rated focus and amount of work completed as the outcome.
- [ ] **Methodology:** give the kit to one group of peers for a study session and not to another, then compare results — this doubles as real product-testing data, not just a school assignment.
- [ ] **Investigation blueprint:** the test plan and a simple feedback form peers fill out (which is also, in disguise, real customer feedback).

### Unit III — Public Communication & Social Impact
*Weeks 9–12 · Required artifact: **Communication Product + Dissemination Plan***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 10 **Signal and the Noise** tournament + Week 11 **Message Transmission** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Communication product:** a short pitch video showing the same Focus Kit in action, the same test results, and why it works.
- [ ] **Strategy document:** target audience = students before exams; objective = get people to actually want and try the kit; misconceptions addressed = "focus products are gimmicks."
- [ ] **Dissemination:** posted where students actually are — school social media, study-group chats, a table at school.

### Unit IV — Memory/Decision Systems Simulation
*Weeks 13–16 · Required artifact: **Simulation Model IV***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 14 **Distortion Chain** tournament + Week 15 **Distortion & Reconstruction** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **What gets simulated:** nothing fancy — a simple before/after tracker showing focus ratings and things-completed for everyone who tried the kit, turned into an easy chart anyone can look at in ten seconds.
- [ ] **Why this matters:** real customers (peers) want proof it works, fast — this is the "does it actually work" evidence a real product needs.
- [ ] **Implementation:** a simple spreadsheet or one-page results chart; documented and shared openly so anyone can see the real numbers.

### Unit V — Policy Formation Simulation
*Weeks 17–20 · Required artifact: **Policy Draft Document***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 18 **Self-Forecast Duel** tournament + Week 19 **Identity & System Construction** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Policy proposal:** not a government policy — a real ask to the school: could the Focus Kit be sold or given out at the school store, library, or during exam week? Students write this as an actual pitch to a real decision-maker (librarian, student store, PTA), not a hypothetical policy brief.
- [ ] **Evidence:** the real test results from Unit II and the results chart from Unit IV.

### Unit VI — Integration & Summit
*Weeks 21–24 · Required artifact: **Capstone Project***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 22 **Capstone Prototype Development** laboratory, leading into the Week 24 Summit.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] Students set up a real table or booth showing the kit, the results, the pitch video, and the actual pitch made (or being made) to the school. The presentation is the pitch itself, practiced live. Reflection defense: what real people said when they tried it, what didn't work, what they'd change next.

## Why This Project Impresses

Because it's a real thing that real people tried and gave real feedback on — not a hypothetical business plan. That's exactly what impresses admissions readers, accelerators, and mentors: proof the student can build something, test it, and improve it.
*Skills demonstrated:* prototyping, running a real test, collecting honest feedback, pitching to a real decision-maker, iterating based on what actually happened — not studying business theory, doing business.
**One Portfolio:** the kit, the test, the pitch video, the results, and the real ask to the school are all the same product idea, getting more real and more tested at every step.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

Every unit of this project is the same real, physical product, tested more rigorously and pitched more seriously at each stage. Unit I builds the first version. Unit II tests it against a real control group in a real study session. Unit III pitches that same tested product to the exact people who'd actually use it. Unit IV turns the results into a simple, honest chart anyone can check in ten seconds. Unit V is a real ask to the school store or library, not a hypothetical one. A mentor or accelerator reader sees a genuine product-development loop — build, test, pitch, prove, ask — executed start to finish in one season, which is exactly the track record early-stage founders are expected to show.
