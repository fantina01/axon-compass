# 6. Sorting Out Sleep

*Using Simple Data Sorting and Charting to Find Sleep Patterns*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Computer Science &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `computer-science/06-sorting-out-sleep`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can basic programming skills for sorting and organizing data (the kind used to sort a list of names or numbers) reveal genuine patterns in a class's sleep and screen-time habits?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Sorting and organizing data is one of the most fundamental computer science skills, and applying it to real, personally-collected sleep and screen-time data turns an abstract skill (sorting algorithms, basic charting) into a tool for discovering something genuinely useful about the class's own habits.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Cain, N., & Gradisar, M. (2010). Electronic media use and sleep in school-aged children and adolescents: A review. Sleep Medicine, 11(8), 735-742.

This review compiled evidence specifically on how screen and electronic media use before bed is associated with delayed sleep onset and reduced total sleep time in school-aged children and adolescents. This is the direct, age-matched evidence behind the project's central hypothesis linking evening screen time to reduced sleep hours.

### 2. Chang, A. M., Aeschbach, D., Duffy, J. F., & Czeisler, C. A. (2015). Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, delaying circadian timing and reducing next-morning alertness. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 112(4), 1232-1237.

This controlled study specifically isolated the effect of screen light exposure before bed (comparing e-readers to printed books) and found it delayed melatonin release, shifted circadian timing later, and reduced next-morning alertness — mechanistic evidence for why screen use specifically, not just staying up doing something else, affects sleep.

### 3. Hale, L., & Guan, S. (2015). Screen time and sleep among school-aged children and adolescents: A systematic literature review. Sleep Medicine Reviews, 21, 50-58.

This systematic review pooled findings across many studies and found a fairly consistent association between screen time and both shorter sleep duration and poorer sleep quality in this age group, while also noting the research is mostly correlational, meaning it can't fully rule out that other factors (like general evening routines) contribute to both screen use and sleep patterns together.

*Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: **Research Investigation / Prospectus***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 6 **Prediction Ladder** tournament + Week 7 **Build-a-System** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Literature review:** plain-language summaries of research linking screen time before bed to sleep quality, plus basic sorting and data-organization programming concepts (arranging data from smallest to largest, grouping into categories).
- [ ] **Hypothesis:** when the sorted data is examined, peers with higher evening screen time will tend to cluster toward lower reported sleep hours.
- [ ] **Variables:** evening screen time as one measured quantity; sleep hours as the other.
- [ ] **Methodology:** collect the survey data, sort and organize it (e.g., group peers into screen-time categories and compare average sleep hours per category), and look at the resulting pattern.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This project's throughline is real class data, organized and understood more deeply at every stage. Unit I collects the first real sleep-and-screen-time survey. Unit II turns it into a genuine sorted, categorized analysis looking for a real pattern. Unit III explains that exact class-specific finding to the same peers who provided the data. Unit IV turns the sorting and charting into a live, reusable tool. Unit V asks health class to adopt this exact activity as a joint CS-health lesson. A CS or data-science mentor sees core sorting and organization skills used to investigate something the whole class genuinely wanted to know about themselves.
