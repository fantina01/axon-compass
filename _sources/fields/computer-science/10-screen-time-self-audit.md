# 10. Screen Time Self-Audit

*Building a Simple App to Track and Visualize Digital Habits*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Computer Science &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `computer-science/10-screen-time-self-audit`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can a beginner-built app that logs and charts a user's own screen time and mood help reveal real personal patterns between digital habits and wellbeing?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Most screen-time tracking is done by phone settings the user doesn't examine closely; building a simple, personal tracker from scratch — logging both screen time and a quick mood check-in — teaches basic data-logging and charting skills while producing a genuinely useful, self-reflective tool.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Twenge, J. M., Martin, G. N., & Campbell, W. K. (2018). Decreases in psychological well-being among American adolescents after 2012 and links to screen time during the rise of smartphone technology. Emotion, 18(6), 765-780.

This large-scale study found that adolescents reporting more screen time also reported lower psychological well-being on average, and that this association appeared alongside the broader rise of smartphone adoption after 2012. This is the direct, large-sample evidence behind the project's central hypothesis, though it's worth noting the finding is correlational — screen time and mood being linked doesn't necessarily mean one directly causes the other in every case.

### 2. Orben, A., & Przybylski, A. K. (2019). The association between adolescent well-being and digital technology use. Nature Human Behaviour, 3(2), 173-182.

This influential, more statistically rigorous study found that the real average association between screen time and adolescent well-being, across large national datasets, is very small — comparable in size to fairly trivial factors like eating potatoes — and much smaller than earlier headlines suggested. This is an essential, honest counterbalance for the project: the true population-level relationship is weak on average, even though an individual student's own personal data might still show a real, meaningful pattern for them specifically.

### 3. Odgers, C. L., & Jensen, M. R. (2020). Annual research review: Adolescent mental health in the digital age — facts, fears, and future directions. Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 61(3), 336-348.

This review synthesizes the broader, mixed research on adolescent digital technology use and mental health, concluding that simple "screen time causes bad mood" narratives oversimplify a genuinely complex picture where what someone does on their screen, and their individual circumstances, likely matter more than raw screen-time hours alone. This is useful for framing the project's own personal self-audit honestly: it's exploring one individual's own pattern, not proving a universal causal law.

### Unit II — Research Investigation
*Weeks 5–8 · Required artifact: **Research Investigation / Prospectus***

**Process for this unit (same for every project in the chapter):** Week 6 **Prediction Ladder** tournament + Week 7 **Build-a-System** laboratory.

**Steps specific to this project:**

- [ ] **Literature review:** plain-language summaries of research on screen time and mood/wellbeing, plus basic programming/spreadsheet concepts for logging and charting data over time.
- [ ] **Hypothesis:** when a peer's own logged data is charted, days with higher screen time will tend to show a somewhat lower mood rating, visible as a pattern in the individual's own chart.
- [ ] **Variables:** daily screen time as one measured quantity; daily mood rating as the other, tracked for the same individual over multiple days.
- [ ] **Methodology:** have several peers use the self-audit tool for a set period, then examine each person's own chart for a visible pattern (or lack of one), without requiring an exact statistical formula.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

This project's throughline is one person's own real data, made more useful and more honestly interpreted at every stage. Unit I builds the first working self-audit tool. Unit II examines the resulting personal pattern honestly, without overclaiming. Unit III explains that same honest, personal finding to peers curious about their own habits. Unit IV turns the tool into a live, reusable, privacy-respecting demo. Unit V proposes the school's wellness program offer it as an optional self-reflection resource, not a monitoring tool. A CS or wellness mentor sees a student who built something genuinely useful and handled a sensitive personal-data topic with real care and honesty about what the data can and can't prove.
