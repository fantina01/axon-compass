# 6. The Physics of a Concussion

*Impact Forces, Momentum, and Traumatic Brain Injury Risk*

**Field:** Neuroscience × Physics &nbsp;·&nbsp; **Project ID:** `physics/06-the-physics-of-a-concussion`

```{admonition} Big Question
:class: tip
Can basic mechanics (force, momentum, impulse) explain why certain impacts are far more likely to cause traumatic brain injury than others of similar visible severity?
```

```{admonition} Why This Matters
:class: note
Concussion risk in sports is a major public-health issue, and the physics behind it — impulse (force × time), rotational vs. linear acceleration, and energy transfer — is standard intro-mechanics material directly applicable to real equipment-design and rule-making decisions.
```

## Core Literature — Read This Before Starting Unit II

Real, verified sources for this project. Read these before Unit II begins — they're what your literature review and hypothesis should be built on.

### 1. Holbourn, A. H. S. (1943). Mechanics of head injuries. The Lancet, 242(6267), 438-441.

Holbourn's classic paper was among the first to argue that rotational acceleration of the brain, not just linear/direct impact force, is a major cause of traumatic brain injury — proposing that shearing forces from rotational movement damage brain tissue differently than a straight-on impact would. This is a foundational insight behind modern helmet design and testing standards, and it's the source of the project's key point that a helmet reducing linear force alone doesn't fully address concussion risk.

### 2. Rowson, S., & Duma, S. M. (2013). Brain injury prediction: Assessing the combined probability of concussion using linear and rotational head acceleration. Annals of Biomedical Engineering, 41(5), 873-882.

Rowson and Duma developed a combined injury-risk model using real, measured head-impact data from football players (via instrumented helmets), incorporating both linear and rotational acceleration to predict concussion probability more accurately than either measure alone. This is directly useful, modern applied evidence connecting the project's impulse-momentum physics to real, quantified injury-risk data from actual sports impacts.

### 3. Broglio, S. P., et al. (2010). Head impacts during high school football: A biomechanical assessment. Journal of Athletic Training, 45(6), 549-559.

This study measured real head-impact forces experienced by high school football players over a season using instrumented helmets, providing real-world impact-force data (rather than only laboratory drop-test data) relevant to student-athlete populations specifically. It's included because it grounds the project's helmet-comparison analysis in data from athletes closer in age to the project's own participants and audience.

## How the Six Outputs Link Into One Portfolio

Every unit of this project sharpens the same practical question: which helmet actually protects better, and why? Unit I starts with a real comparison of publicly available helmet safety ratings. Unit II applies real impulse-momentum physics to explain what separates higher- and lower-rated helmets, using genuine published test data. Unit III explains that exact analysis to the athletes and coaches who make real equipment decisions. Unit IV visualizes precisely why spreading out an impact over more time reduces peak force — the core physics behind every padded helmet ever designed. Unit V turns the whole analysis into a specific, actionable equipment-purchasing recommendation for the school's own athletic program. An athletic director or biomedical engineering mentor sees a student who applied foundational mechanics to a genuinely important safety decision, with real data backing every step from observation to recommendation.
